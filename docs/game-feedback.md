# The game: feedback

Everything the game says back to the player — the HUD, the sound, the music, the fireworks, the light that runs through the cluster, and the camera that follows a good shot down.

Part of the BS3D documentation. CLAUDE.md holds the project overview, the build commands, the ball grid and the repo-wide conventions, and says which of these documents covers what.

## The ripple: the cluster answers a landing (Game)

Every ball that lands sends a wave of light out through the cluster — the balls touching the impact flare first, then the ones touching those, each fading as the next takes over. It is what makes the cluster read as a connected, living body rather than a heap of independent spheres: the shot does not just stick, the thing it stuck to answers. The resting heartbeat was turned down for it (`BALL_PULSE_DEPTH` 0.55 → 0.38): the cluster now has two things to say with its own light, and a breath swinging over half the emission drowns out the event.

- **It travels by connectivity, not by distance, and that is the whole of why it looks right.** `StartRipple` is a breadth-first walk over the balls that *touch* (`BallsMap.GetNeighboringCells`) outwards from the landed cell, so a ball's hop count is how many balls the light has to pass through to reach it — which is exactly its delay. A wave evaluated from a world-space radius in the shader would cross the holes a played cluster is full of as if they were not there; a walk goes **around** them, including around the hole the matched group has just left, which is the most satisfying thing it does. It is started *after* the release, so it walks the cluster that is left rather than the one that was.
- **The origin cell seeds the walk whether or not a ball is still standing in it.** A shot that completes a group is released along with it, so by the time this runs the cell it landed in is usually empty and the wave has to start from the balls around the gap. (Measured: a landing that matched reported `0 balls lit` from its own cell before this was handled.)
- **Only the resulting brightness rides on the GPU** — `ModelInstance.Ripple`, a `Single` in TEXCOORD7 beside `Dissolve`, zero meaning "not flaring" so nothing has to know it exists to opt out. The shader term is behind `[branch] if (RippleStrength > 0)`, a *uniform*, so it cannot diverge and the Testbed and map editor (which never set it) pay nothing.
- **The flare has to be mostly WHITE, and this is the trap that cost the most.** Adding light in the ball's own colour piles it into the one channel already near the top of the ACES curve, so a red ball taking a full-strength flare goes from bright red to *very slightly* brighter red. It was verifiably in the instance data at 0.97 and could not be seen on screen at all — a diagnostic that added to the green channel instead lit the cluster up like a Christmas tree. The hue is normalised to peak 1 and carried 65 % to white (`RippleWhiten`); lifting the channels the ball does **not** have is what "lighting up" looks like, and it settles the dark types for free (the 8-ball would otherwise sit the wave out entirely).
- **Three numbers decide whether it reads as a wave or as a flash**, and it is the ratio between them: the lit band is as many balls deep as the flare's length over the hop delay. The first build had a 0.36 s flare stepping every 0.045 s — nine balls deep against a reach of twelve, i.e. very nearly the whole cluster alight at once, which read as the shot flashing the lot. Keep the band around three or four balls (`RIPPLE_HOP_SECONDS` 0.09, attack 0.05, decay 0.22, `RIPPLE_MAX_HOPS` 14).
- **The amplitude falloff is squared, and that is a glare problem before it is a look problem.** The *count* of balls at a given hop grows as its square in a packed lattice, so a linear falloff leaves hundreds of them near full brightness a few hops out. At `BALL_RIPPLE_STRENGTH` 1.1 that flooded the glare's bright pass, whose six streak arms overlapped into a wash and — added before the ACES curve — blew the **entire frame** white for one frame (measured: a single sample at 174 mean against a 137 baseline). It looked like a rendering fault, not an effect. The published tuning holds the whole-frame mean flat to ±1 while the cluster itself clearly flares.
- **The same wave carries the ceiling's alarm, and the SIGN of the per-instance float says which.** Positive is the landing's own light; negative is the alarm — one channel, two meanings, exactly as `Dissolve` encodes its two directions. It costs nothing (a ball could only be in one wave anyway; the newest to reach it takes it over) and it means the shader picks the colour with a select rather than a second uniform.
- **The alarm REPLACES the ball's colour rather than adding to it**, and that is the whole difference between a warning and a wash. Added, a red flare on a green ball is yellow, on a red one a slightly brighter red, on black a pale grey — every ball came out a different pastel and none of them said "red". Blended (`RippleAlarmCoverage` 0.95, short of 1 so the balls still read as balls), the cluster momentarily *turns* red.
- **The state lives on `PhysicsBall`** (`RippleTime`, counting up from a negative delay, and `RippleAmplitude`), for the reason `Occlusion` does: a ball crosses between the structure and the falling balls in one step, and a group cut loose mid-wave should keep glowing on the way down rather than snapping dark. It is advanced from inside the shared `ClusterCollector` walk, through the one hook that component takes at construction (the Game's `AdvanceRipple`; the Testbed and the editor hand over none and pay a predicted branch on a readonly field per ball) — the one place that visits every ball exactly once a frame, the same contract the occlusion ease and the attach glide rely on, and one that `BallRenderSet.BeginFrame` now enforces rather than assumes. A ball the wave reaches again while still lit simply takes the newer wave: the nearest impact wins, which is what a burst of quick shots should look like.
- **`BallLanding` replaced the event's parameter list.** `BallLanded` had grown from "what fell" to a world position, then a ball type, then the cell; four positional arguments of which two are vectors is unreadable at the call site, and the ripple needed the **lattice** cell, which cannot be asked of a world position (`MapToWorld`/`WorldToMap` were deliberately deleted — see "Physics in the game" in `docs/game-session.md`).

## The ceiling announces itself (Game)

**A step waits for the drop cinematic, and the reason is that it was saying the wrong thing.** The step comes due on the frame a shot is *fired*, but the shot leaves at `SHOOT_SPEED` and lands about a tenth of a second later — so when that shot cut a large group loose, the glass flashing red and driving its alarm wave down the cluster landed **on top of the drop cinematic**. A player who had just made the best shot of the level was shown the game's one punishment animation while watching their reward, and it read as having done something wrong. Nothing was wrong; only the order was.

- `ReleaseCeilingStep` holds a due step for `CEILING_STEP_HOLD` (0.45 s) — long enough for the shot to have landed and a cinematic to have engaged if it is going to — and then for that cinematic to be over. On an ordinary shot the descent still arrives promptly enough to read as the answer to firing.
- **A count, not a flag.** A level with `ceilingStep` of 1 steps on every shot, and two shots inside the hold must not lose one of them. The hold is re-armed per release rather than shared, so queued steps come down one at a time instead of as a single double-height lurch.
- It cannot deadlock: the release is gated on a timer and on `_cinematic.Engaged`, never on a shot resolving, so a ball that never comes to rest cannot leave a level unloseable.
- The `[ceiling]` line carries `waited`, which is the one figure that says whether the deferral did anything. Measured over three played levels: every ordinary step reads `waited 0.45 s` (the minimum), and the two steps that landed on a cinematic read **`waited 1.51 s`**.

The glass stepping down is the pressure the whole `ceilingStep` rule exists to apply, and it was **arriving unnoticed**: a translucent plate sliding a little way down against a sky, while the player's eye is on the cluster, is very nearly invisible. It now says so twice, on the frame it steps.

- **The plate lights up red.** `EmissiveTint` is a `float3` on `InstancedModelRenderer`, added at the end of `ShadePixel` so every technique that shades through it can use it, zero everywhere else. `_ceilingFlash` spikes to 1 on a step and decays **linearly** (the `CameraShake` rule — it genuinely ends), and the tint is scaled by its *square*, so the glass is unmistakable on the frame it steps and has thinned well before the slide finishes. It marks the event; it does not colour the plate for the duration.
- **`EmissiveTint` is deliberately not multiplied by the material's alpha**, unlike the specular ambient beside it: alpha is how much of what is *behind* a surface comes through, and a pane that is glowing is emitting rather than transmitting. Attenuating a warning by the glass's own transparency is what would make it invisible.
- **The number has to be far over 1 for the same reason.** The plate is well under half opaque (`CeilingPlate.GLASS_ALPHA`, and deliberately not restated as a number here — the percentage this sentence used to carry was left behind by a retune and lied for it), so most of what is seen where the glass is is the *sky behind it*; at 1.5 the red merely tinted that blue-white and the plate came out pink. At 6 it reads as a hot red slab. (It is still not a pure red — a mostly transparent plate over a blue-violet sky cannot be — but it is unmistakable, and it blooms.)
- **And a red wave runs down through the cluster**, seeded from *every ball on the topmost occupied level at once* (`StartCeilingRipple`) rather than from a point, because that is what a descent is: a shock delivered by the ceiling to everything hanging on it. The walk then only ever moves outwards, so it travels down the way the push does. See "The ripple" for how the two waves share one channel.

## The floor alarm: the laser grid (Game)

The descending ceiling's warning at the other end of the field. When the cluster's lowest ball is within **three more ceiling steps of the death line** (`LASER_WARN_STEPS`), a grid of pulsing red laser beams appears across the field's footprint, hovering on the exact plane a ball's *surface* would touch at the moment the level is lost (`CEILING_DEATH_Y − BALL_RADIUS`; the loss compares ball centres against the line, and since #239 only once they have *held* it — see "A cluster that merely swings has not lost" in `docs/game-session.md`, which is why the net can now be touched for a moment without the level ending). Since the line was lowered onto the island that plane is **half a unit over the island's stone cap** — which is what fixes the line's own clearance at one unit rather than the half it would otherwise want: any less and the net is drawn inside the cap it is meant to hover over, and it is also what left #239 no room to lower the line a second time. It stands down when the danger passes — the player frees the bottom levels — and when the level ends either way it keeps pulsing for a moment after the result screen goes up, then goes out. `Game/Effects/LaserGrid.cs` owns the net, `Testbed/Content/Shaders/LaserGrid.fx` draws it (Game-only: registered in the Game's `.mgcb` alone, the `Fireworks.fx` precedent), and `GameplayScreen.UpdateLaserWarning` owns the trigger.

- **The trigger is geometry, read off the very walk the loss is decided on.** `CheckLevelLost`'s scan over the live body poses tracks the minimum Y rather than stopping at the first offender, and the warning is `lowest ≤ CEILING_DEATH_Y + 3 × CEILING_DESCENT_PER_STEP` (−3.7) — the loss's own comparison, three descents early (two until #110; playtesting asked for it a bit sooner, and at the set's cadences of 4–9 shots per descent the extra step buys roughly that many shots of noticing it before it matters). Geometry rather than shots, deliberately: a level whose ceiling never steps still warns when attaching balls grow the cluster down to the line, and a ceiling-driven countdown would sit silent through exactly that. `LASER_WARN_HYSTERESIS` (0.3) is on the way *out* only — a shot shoves the structure, and a lowest ball bobbing across the exact threshold would otherwise arm and stand down the net with every swing. An empty field's `MaxValue` stands it down for free.
- **A drop cinematic holds the level's endings, never the warning** (`CheckLevelLost`'s `mayLose`). The release that engages a cinematic is exactly the release that rescues a low cluster — six or more balls cut off its bottom — so a warning frozen behind the cinematic gate kept blazing across the player's reward for the whole slow-motion dive down the drain, seconds after the danger it warned about was gone. The walk and the arm/stand-down now run on every frame of a built session; only `LoseLevel` and the budget test wait for the cinematic to let go.
- **Everything time-driven is stamped in wall-clock seconds and evaluated in `Draw`, and that is the load-bearing decision.** A lost level pushes the result screen over the session, and the session stops running its *rules* from that moment — the covered frame it does get is the world's alone (#241) — while `Draw` keeps running (`DrawsUnderlying`). So a fade advanced in an update would freeze at full brightness at the exact moment the player is being told what it warned about. Instead the envelope is a pure function of the clock (the fireworks' contract with their sparks' age): `SetVisible` and `NoticeLevelEnded` stamp transitions, `Draw` evaluates them, and the linger (`LINGER_SECONDS`, 2.5) expires *in Draw*, the one place still running under the result page. The linger is stamped in `ShowResultScreen` — the one funnel both endings come through — and not where the level logically ends: a clear's page arrives `LEVEL_CLEARED_BEAT` after the field empties, plus a whole cinematic when one is running, and a linger stamped at the clear was spent before anyone saw it. Under a pause the net keeps pulsing, like the ball heartbeat and the city's windows — everything alive runs off the wall clock.
- **The beams billboard about their own axes (ShotTrail's trick), and here it is load-bearing rather than nice.** The play camera stands at Y −7.9, barely below the grid's plane at −6.0 — a flat quad lying in that plane is seen edge-on and vanishes. Billboarded, every beam holds its thickness from any lens, including precise aim's.
- **The red is the ceiling flash's own** (`CEILING_FLASH_COLOR`, passed into the constructor so the two cannot drift apart): the plate says "the glass stepped", the net says "and this is where it ends". The channel has to be far over 1 for the glare's sake — the bright pass keys on *luminance* and red's Rec. 709 weight is 0.2126, so a pure red needs a channel near 2.6 before it clears `GLARE_THRESHOLD` (0.55) at all.
- **The white core bleached the whole net at every peak, and the screenshots caught it.** The hot filament inside each beam lifts the channels red does not have (the cluster ripple's lesson) — but the play camera sees the grid near edge-on, so the beams stack additively across the frame, and at `CORE_WHITE` 2.5 the net turned white at exactly its loudest moment. A red warning that stops being red at its peak is the failure mode; at 0.9 the peak blooms red-pink and the trough stays pure laser red.
- **The pulse is a raised cosine squared at 1.6 Hz** between a floor of 0.35 and 1 — sharp peaks, wide troughs, an alarm's shape rather than a breath. At the floor the beams' luminance sits just under the glare threshold and at the peak far over it, so the pulse visibly breathes the bloom itself, not just the brightness.
- **The net spans the field footprint (+0.75 bleed), which keeps it entirely inside the drain's mouth.** Every authored footprint's half-diagonal is well under `ArenaIsland.FUNNEL_TOP_RADIUS` (14), so the beams can never cross the gold bead at the rim, and the gun — whose orbit's lower bound already clears the footprint — never stands in them. One static buffer per level, rebuilt in `Fit` from `FitFieldToMap` (the ceiling renderer's cadence), one draw call, three per-frame uniforms.
- **Drawn in the trails' states — additive, `DepthRead`, `CullNone` — but between the two glasses, and the position is load-bearing.** The net lies between the drain and the ceiling in Y, and neither translucent draw can depth-test against it (the net writes no depth), so the order has to encode the geometry: *after* `DrawSettingGlass`, because the funnel is entirely below the net and a drop cinematic looking down the throat has the net in front of the glass — drawn earlier, the cone wrongly dimmed it — and *before* the ceiling plate, which is above the net, so an overhead shot seeing the net through the glass still gets the plate composited over it. The island, the gun and any ball in front occlude the beams either way. (There is a third translucent surface in the frame since the barrel's window was glazed, and it needs no such reasoning: the gun's pane is drawn after everything, being nearer than all of it — see "The gun's hardware, in one copy" in `docs/testbed.md`.)
- **`lasers` on the command line pins it on** while a level is played: reaching it honestly means playing to within two steps of losing, which can no more be scripted than clearing a level can — the `celebrate` reasoning, for a session-owned effect.

## The in-play HUD (Game)

What the scoring rules (see "Scoring" in `docs/game-session.md`) look like while a level is being played (#60), and how they *move*. It lives in **`Game/Screens/PlayHud.cs`** — its own class, for the reason `ScoreKeeper` is one: this is the feel of the thing, the part most likely to be argued about and retuned, and it has no business being another few hundred lines inside `GameplayScreen` where the dials cannot be seen together. It owns no score; it is handed the keeper each frame and animates what it reads there. Drawn in one `SpriteBatch.Begin/End` **after the tonemap resolve**, in display space, like the crosshair and for the same reason — a HUD downsampled with the scene would be soft.

**Not Myra**, and that is not only about input arbitration (Myra's `Desktop.Render` also consumes the mouse and the keyboard, and the game captures the cursor while playing): Myra has no animation system to reach for. It lays widgets out, and every motion below would have to be driven from outside it frame by frame anyway. And **a shader over the text is the wrong tool** — `SpriteBatch.Begin(effect:)` will happily put a pixel shader over the glyph quads, but each glyph is its own quad, so anything that reaches *outside* the glyph (a blur, a smear) is clipped at the quad's edge and stops dead at the letterforms. What is used instead: FontStashSharp's own `FontSystemEffect.Blurry`, which rasterizes a blurred variant into the **atlas** (so a glow costs one extra atlas variant per window size, not a pass per frame), and repeated draws along the path for the motion smear.

- **The corners, because they are the only space free in *both* poses.** The centre of the frame belongs to the cluster, the bottom to the gun, and precise aim leans the lens in over the barrel and fills more of it still. Score top **right** (the FPS line owns the top left, and `InfoRenderer` draws after this in `base.Draw`), balls left bottom **left**, the magazine strip bottom **right** since #236 — so all four corners are now spoken for, and the strip's own bullet below records why it ended up in the one it did. Verified in the overview, in ADS and at 21:9 fullscreen (3840×1600) — everything is placed off `Viewport.Width/Height` and scaled by `Viewport.Height / 2160` through the menu's own `Scaled()`, so a wider display only moves the two columns apart.
- **The magazine strip, bottom right (#236).** The loaded queue as flat discs, next shot first and largest, with a ring around the head. It exists because **the gun cannot answer "which colour fires next" from the pose the game plays**: `CannonRig`'s own note records that drawn opaque, the pane fills the whole slot from this camera — the barrel hides its own muzzle end, so the round in the notch shows only as the small ellipse of its cap and the four behind it are read through glass keeping about 0.38 of what is behind it. Naming a colour off a small dark ellipse is the difficulty, and no palette change makes an ellipse bigger; #246 measured and fixed the palette half and could only fix that half.
  - **It took two moves to land, and the left edge is why.** The issue asked for the bottom **left**, which is the busiest edge on the frame: `DrawClusterProfile` holds its vertical middle (`Scaled(1300)` of 2160, ~542 px at 900) and `DrawBallsLeft` the bottom, leaving about **74 px** between them at 1600×900 — enough for a disc row at that one window height and not reliably at others. So it first went *beside* the count on the free horizontal axis, which needed a second constant once the owner saw the first halo sitting almost on the "balls left" caption. Then the owner moved it to the bottom **right**, which settles it: that corner was empty in both camera poses, nothing there has to be cleared, and the strip is anchored to the frame's own margin like every other corner readout.
  - **The row runs rightwards in FIRING order — head leftmost, the tail in the corner — and it is the HEAD whose origin is solved.** The order is the owner's, asked for after playing it the other way round; what the layout owes it is that the head does not move, because the row shortens as the shot budget runs down and a head placed by measuring back from the right edge would slide along the bottom of the screen over the last five shots — exactly when the player is reading it. So the head's origin is solved for a *full* magazine ending flush with the margin: a shorter queue empties from the far end and leaves a gap at the corner, which is the truth about what is happening, and the disc that matters stays put. The corner still has to clear the head's *full* reach, ring included, for the reason `HUD_MARGIN`'s own comment gives about a halo drawn hard against the frame's edge — that is what fixes the row's vertical middle.
  - **`HUD_MAG_GAP` measures what is DRAWN, and re-basing it was the fix rather than raising it.** It was the space between the *fills*, so each disc's dark halo ate `HUD_MAG_RIM` off both ends of it — at 24 that left 10 units of daylight between two rounds and the four resting discs read as one run of touching blobs. The head was not merely tight but wrong: its ring stands `HUD_MAG_RING_GAP + HUD_MAG_RING_THICKNESS` outside the halo, 18 units past the fill the gap was measured from, so the mark that says "this one, right now" **overlapped the next round's halo by a unit**. The constant is now the clear space between one disc's outermost drawn edge and the next's, at 32, and every step is written from each disc's own reach — which is why the first step is longer than the others by construction rather than by a fudge. The right edge is unchanged: it was already anchored on `rest + rim`, so the strip grew leftwards and the tail stayed where it was.
  - **The colours come through `TypeColor`**, which bakes from `BasicEffectParamsProvider.GetDiffuseTintByType` — so the strip tracks the balls by construction. There is deliberately **no second palette** here to drift out of step with them.
  - **A dark halo behind every disc and a light outline inside each fill**, and the second one is not decoration: `TypeColor` keeps each type's real darkness on purpose (#153 refused peak-normalising), so the 8-ball prints at about (22, 20, 16) and a filled disc of it inside a dark halo is a hole. Outlined, it reads the way the ball itself does — by its light gores against the black. Verified with a queue of three black rounds.
  - **It never shows more discs than there are shots left.** The magazine holds five whatever happens, so at a budget of two a five-disc strip is a lie about the level, and the last shots are exactly when the player is counting. (Off `ScoreKeeper.ShotsRemaining`, which includes the loaded rounds. Reaching a budget under five needs no argument at all — any level's own budget gets there on its last few shots; #262 fired down `One`'s 30 to confirm the strip and the barrel agree at four.)
  - **The physical bore agrees with it (#262).** The strip's own clamp shipped with #236, but the loaded balls actually drawn *inside* the barrel kept the old unconditional five — a two-shot budget still showed a full five-ball queue in the tube the strip sat next to, the two readouts disagreeing about the same gun. `GameplayScreen.Draw.cs`'s `CollectMagazineBalls` now takes the identical `Math.Min(Magazine.SIZE, remaining)` clamp; see "Neither may the barrel show more loaded rounds than there are shots left" in `docs/game-session.md`.
  - **⚠️ `DrawDisc` had a latent half-pixel bug and this is what found it.** The primitive rounded each scanline's `y` with `MathF.Round`, which rounds a **.5 to even** — so a centre landing exactly on a half-pixel mapped consecutive rows to `y, y+2, y+2, y+4 …`, drawing half the rows twice and **never drawing the other half at all**. The strip's first build came out combed: solid horizontally, one-pixel stripes vertically with the scene showing between them, at ssaa 1 and 2 alike. Sampled a column to see it (`38,38,145` alternating with the stone behind), because at a glance it reads as a shader or a blend-state fault rather than as arithmetic. It is a `MathF.Floor(cy + dy + 0.5f)` now — contiguous for every centre, and different from `Round` only at the .5 that was broken. The cluster profile's markers go through the same primitive and were one lucky centre away from it. (The scanline arithmetic itself has since been retired — see the antialiasing bullet below — but the incident is kept for what it taught about `MathF.Round`, and because its whole-pixel discipline survived into the texture draw.)
  - **The discs are antialiased, from cached coverage textures (the owner's ask).** The strip's rough look was the scanline primitive's nature: a one-pixel bar is either drawn or not, so every rim was a staircase, and at the strip's sizes — and the profile's, a third of that again — the owner read it as rough. `DrawDisc` now draws each disc and ring as ONE quad from a small procedural texture generated per whole-pixel geometry and cached for the HUD's life, whose texels carry the disc's exact **area coverage** (8×8 supersampled, premultiplied white — the `Crosshair` colour precedent, so the batch's default `AlphaBlend` composes the tint correctly at every fraction). The texture objection the scanlines were built on was to a *content asset* — something to premultiply, ship and keep in step; a disc baked in code from its own radii is none of that, and it is **cheaper to draw**: one quad per disc where the scanlines cost one per row, which on a full cluster profile was hundreds of quads a frame for its markers alone. The profile's marker radius breathes with the orbit (its pixels-per-unit follows the field's turning half-depth), so the fraction its rounding drops is spent on the sprite's **scale** rather than thrown away — the discs grow continuously where whole-pixel steps would tick once a turn. Verified by 4× capture on both: the rim carries a ~1-source-pixel transition band on the strip's fills, its head ring and the profile's markers alike.
- **The muzzle round's coloured halo, and the white mark it replaced (#236).** #175 marked the round that fires next by breathing a **ripple** on it, and the ripple's positive branch adds mostly-**white** light — so the one round whose colour most needed reading was the one continuously washed towards white while the player aimed. It said "this one" by spending the very thing it existed to reveal. The breath is now a halo **around** the ball, in the round's own colour: `BallGlow` + `Testbed/Content/Shaders/BallGlow.fx`, one additive camera-facing billboard, depth-read, in linear radiance over 1 so it blooms through the glare like the emissive balls.
  - **The hole in the middle is the depth buffer's doing, not a figure in the shader.** The quad passes through the ball's centre, so the ball's own front hemisphere is nearer the lens and the depth test discards that part — what survives is the annulus outside the silhouette, a halo around the ball rather than a wash over it. **That is the whole reason this mechanism works where two others failed**: both of those add light *to the ball*, and a halo puts colour where there was none — the dark bore, the sky past the muzzle.
  - **The same test is what makes it read as light escaping the loading window.** From the game camera the barrel is in front of the round, so the tube rejects most of the halo and what comes out of the notch reads as the gun lit from inside by the colour it is about to fire. Screenshotted on `level=One` (a red round glowing against a blue sky, the strip in the corner agreeing) and on `level=Reel` (cyan spilling onto the skyline behind).
  - **Two mechanisms were refused first and both are measured, so neither is worth retrying.** A same-hue flare through the instanced shader's positive `RippleStrength` branch was tried at full strength (0.97) and "could not be seen on screen at all", because it piles energy into a channel already at the top of the ACES curve. The negative branch, which *replaces* a ball's shading with a flat colour, is a single `float3` **uniform per draw call**, so it cannot carry one slot's own hue without per-instance data.
  - **The cadence did not change**, deliberately: the rate (`MUZZLE_MARK_HZ` 1.6, off neither the cluster's 1.1 Hz heartbeat nor the ghost's 2.2 Hz blink) and the gate (`_previewBeamVisible` — exactly "would a shot leave the barrel this instant") are the ones #175 tuned. What was wrong with the mark was the channel, not its timing.
  - **⚠ Every figure above was judged against the OVERVIEW, and precise aim takes away the two things that made them safe (#321).** The sentence a few lines up — *the tube rejects most of the halo* — is a defence of the *camera*, not of the halo: `PreciseAim` puts the lens behind and above the muzzle and narrows the field 1.19×, so nothing rejects anything and a ring four ball radii across becomes a blooming, breathing disc over the very cells the mode exists to read. The owner's report was exactly that. **Two figures now come down with `_preciseAim.Blend`**, because the report has two halves: the reach (`BallGlow.Draw` takes it per call now, 4 ball radii to `MUZZLE_GLOW_ADS_RADII` 2 — a dimmer disc of the same size still sits over the same cells) and the strength (to `MUZZLE_GLOW_ADS_STRENGTH`, 30 % — a floor, never off, because #236's ruling leaves the halo as the only 3D statement of the next colour). **And the breath is damped out entirely rather than scaled**: what reads as loud over a field being aimed at is the *movement*, so leaned in the halo becomes a steady ring. The HUD's magazine strip carries the same colour in 2D throughout, which is what lets it come down this far. **Measured through the leaned-in lens, red excess (R−G) on the sky above the round: 43/24/26/23 codes at four heights before, 13/8/3/−50 after** — a hundred pixels up the halo is simply gone. **The overview is untouched by construction, not by luck**: `PreciseAim.Step` snaps `Blend` to exactly 0 when the button is released, so both lerps are identities there. The halo is the third member of the group that consults the mode, after the aim beam's opacity and the crosshair's, and was the last one that did not.
  - **The breath's depth was set by argument, not measured, and that is deliberate.** 0.24…1.00 on the halo's strength: full at the peak because the owner asked for a *strong* pulse, and never near zero because #175's rule stands that the round must not be briefly unmarked. **Measuring the on-screen swing was attempted and abandoned** — scheduling `shot=` captures against a 1.6 Hz sine measures the sampler, not the effect. The game runs ~21 FPS on this level at `High` and a shot-taking frame costs ~0.1 s, so eight requested times yielded three captures, and two attempts at half-period spacing gave 4 % then 2 %, i.e. the wrong direction. The strength is a linear multiplier on the added radiance, so the swing is what it says it is; do not quote a screen figure for it without a frame-accurate capture path.
  - **And the loaded balls do not breathe at all any more (#252)** — the other half of the same ruling, in the owner's words once the halo was in: *it is enough that the cannon's tip glows*. No ball in the barrel carries a ripple, and none of them takes the cluster's emissive heartbeat either, so they radiate their colour **steadily** while the cluster breathes and the pulsing is the gun's, in one place.
    - **It had to be a different DRAW, not a value on the ball.** `EmissiveStrength`, `PulseDepth`, `PulseSpeed` and the rest are **per-renderer uniforms**, pushed once per draw call — so "this ball is still" cannot travel with the instance. `BallRenderSet` keeps **two planes** of (type × LOD) buckets since #252, and `BallDrawFrame.Add`'s `still` flag routes a ball into the second; `Draw` then makes one pass per plane, the still one at `PulseDepth` 0. Both passes **state** the depth they draw at rather than one inheriting what the other left on the renderer — the same trap the shot trail's two callers already paid for once. The alternative was a sixth float on `ModelInstance` for the shader to multiply the emission by: a vertex-stream change and every producer of instances touched, for five balls a frame.
    - **It costs the still plane's own draw calls** — at most one per type actually loaded, so up to five, each a five-instance call. The buckets are lazy, so a frame with no magazine (the map editor's, the menu backdrop's) never touches a renderer for that plane at all.
    - **Measured, six captures 0.5 s apart on `level=Reel` at `High`, as the spread of a region's mean luminance.** The magazine's deep slots went **10.70 → 1.41**; the cluster's red block **4.61 → 5.14** (unchanged inside run-to-run), and two controls confirm the instrument was reading the pulse and not the frame: the cluster's *black* block sat at 0.58 → 0.59 (the 8-ball is documented as the one ball that does not pulse) and a patch of stone floor at 0.23 → 0.15. What is left of the magazine's 1.41 is most likely the muzzle halo's own pulsing bloom leaking into that region, which is the intended pulse in the intended place. Note the *before* figure: at 10.70 the loaded rounds were the **most conspicuously breathing thing in the frame**, ahead of the cluster, because they sit close to the lens and fill their region.
    - **The Testbed still breathes its magazine**, deliberately: it shares the gun and the queue (#76) but has no halo and no HUD strip to carry the signal instead, and it is not the product.
- **It is authored LOUD, and that is the brief.** A HUD in a game is not a data readout: the score and the ball count are two of the three things the player is tracking, they have to land while the eye is in the middle of the frame on the cluster, and the corners are empty in both poses so size costs nothing. The first pass was tuned like an overlay — small type, a 0.34 pop, a single-pass halo — and read as barely-there at a glance, which is the failure mode to check for. Type runs 140/112/76 design units (score/award/label), the score's pop is +0.7 of its own size, and the halo is drawn `HUD_GLOW_PASSES` times because **one pass of a blur is always fainter than the text it came from** — a blur spreads a glyph's energy over its own area, which is the wrong way round for a flash meant to be the loudest thing on the frame. Compounding alpha gets a dense core and a soft edge, which is what a bloom looks like and what a single pass at any alpha cannot be.
- **One primitive carries "this number just changed": `Pulse`.** A scalar resting at 1, **displaced** by a kick and sprung back. A spring rather than a keyframed curve for the reason `PreciseAim.Blend` is a blend and not a state machine — a second kick landing mid-settle adds to the one in flight, so a burst of scoring shots reads as one rising swell instead of an animation restarting under the eye. The kick moves the *position*, not the velocity: a pop that has to accelerate first reads as a wobble, not as a hit. ω ≈ 17.9 rad/s at ζ ≈ 0.45 is one visible undershoot of about a fifth of the kick and then done, and it is **snapped to rest under an epsilon** exactly as `CameraShake`'s decay ends — an exponential settle never quite arrives, and a permanent sub-pixel tremble on a number read every second is worse than no motion. It is **sub-stepped at 1/120 s**, because an explicit spring this stiff goes unstable as the step approaches 2/ω ≈ 0.11 s and this game really does run at ten frames a second on weak hardware until the adaptive tier steps in. Kicks **saturate** (`MAX_OFFSET`) the way `CameraShake`'s trauma does — at this amplitude two awards inside one settle would otherwise throw the score off the top of the frame, and `HUD_FONT_SCORE` is chosen against that clamp: the score is pivoted on its vertical centre at `HUD_MARGIN + height/2`, so the fully-kicked number must still clear the frame.
- **The halo must be centred by its own measurement, not the text's.** A blurred glyph is a larger bitmap with its own render offset, so drawing it from the plain text's origin puts the halo down and to the right of the number — on screen it looked like a stray glow *beside* the score rather than the score glowing. `MeasureString` takes the effect and amount; measure with them and centre the two boxes. `HUD_MARGIN` also has to clear the halo, not just the text: a glow drawn hard against the frame's edge is a glow cut in half by it, which reads as a rendering fault.
- **The direction of the kick is the information.** Score and streak grow (they went up); the ball count shrinks (it went down). They are deliberately not one shared figure. Each is pivoted on the corner it is aligned to — the score on its right edge, the ball count on its bottom-left — so the margins hold rock-steady and only the figure moves.
- **The awards fly into the score, and landing there is what pays it.** A popup is born on the cell it was earned in, held `POPUP_HOLD` (0.5 s) to be read, then flown to the corner over `POPUP_FLIGHT` (0.55 s) — and the readout's count-up starts on the frame it *lands*, not a second earlier when it would mean nothing. `_scoreShown` therefore chases `score.Score` **less the points still in the air**, recomputed from the list rather than carried as a running total, so the two agree by construction once the last popup lands, whatever happened in between (a level torn down mid-flight, a completion bonus with no popup at all — that one calls `FlashScore` so it is not the one award the score takes without being hit).
- **The flight is a lerp from a source reprojected every frame**, so at the handover the blend weight is zero and nothing snaps — the one-reversible-scalar idiom again. The ease is a *back-in*: it pulls backwards first, then accelerates, arriving at its greatest speed, which is where the smear should be longest and where the corner is about to be hit. The last valid projection is remembered, because a drop cinematic routinely swings the cell out of frame mid-flight.
- **The motion blur is sampled off the curve, not off the last frame.** The path is closed-form, so looking back a fixed distance *in flight parameter* gives a trail whose length is the curve's own local speed — nothing to store, nothing to go stale, identical at any frame rate, and zero cost while the number is not moving. Ghost alpha falls as `t²` behind the head, which is what a shutter integrates and what stops the trail reading as a row of copies.
- **Colour: one accent, and it means *gain*.** The HUD sits over thirteen scene palettes whose colours are nothing alike, so a hue that reads as the game's own over one of them fights the next — the same constraint the menu solves by going greyscale. **Amber** (`HUD_ACCENT`) survives them all because it is the sun's own colour and every scene already has it, and it is spent on the score's arrival flash (tint + `Blurry` halo, the halo squared so it marks the hit and is gone before the spring settles), on the streak, and on the last two balls. The **only** other colour is a popup's at birth: the colour of the balls that were actually cut, as the lit ball actually shows it — `TypeColor` bakes each type's tint once through the pipeline's own transform (the pattern shader's linearization, a nominal lit level, the tonemap's ACES curve and its sRGB encode, calibrated against a screenshot of the real cluster; #153). It used to be the raw tint peak-lifted the way the launch smear does it, which kept the hue but printed every colour lighter than the ball it stood for — the 8-ball's 0.045 grey came out almost white. It turns to amber as it flies, so the gesture literally says *those balls became those points* — colour that is never decoration because the cluster is always there to justify it.
- **Balls left escalates in three steps and only the last is a colour.** At `HUD_LOW_BALLS` (5) the number grows a step (`HUD_LOW_EMPHASIS`) and starts to **breathe** — motion is a stronger alarm than hue and costs nothing over thirteen palettes; at `HUD_CRITICAL_BALLS` (2) it takes the accent as well. That first step was a heavier *weight* until the HUD moved to Anton, which has only one: a bold slot on a single-weight face resolves to the very same glyphs, so the step would have vanished in silence and left a documented three-step alarm with two. It is pivoted on its bottom-left corner, so growing it cannot push it off frame the way the centre-pivoted score would be. Breathing, not blinking: a slow swell reads as urgency, a hard flash reads as a fault. Both run off the **play** clock, so they stop when the game does.
- **⚠ Every readout is also backed by a blurred black copy of its own glyphs, because an offset shadow darkens only ONE side of a letter.** The HUD had one dark copy at `HUD_SHADOW_OFFSET`, argued for over a *bright sky* — where it is right, a sky being smooth enough that one offset separates the figure from it. The bottom-left ball count is not over sky. It is over the **island's own concrete deck**, in all thirteen scenes, and there the three sides the offset does not cover are near-white on near-white with a texture running under them. The owner reported it as hard to read and it measured worse than that: over the meadow, in the caption's own bounding box, the **p95 luminance was 149.8 — the concrete's level, not the caption's**, because `MENU_TEXT_DIM` at grey 146 was *darker than the deck it was drawn on*. That colour's own comment says what it is for — asides "always on a dark plate" — and there is no plate within a thousand pixels of this one. It has its own `HUD_CAPTION` (206) now, and the box reads p95 206 with the contrast range up from 118.8 to 182.0.
  - **The backing is FontStashSharp's `Blurry`, the same effect the gain flash uses**, so it costs one more cached atlas variant per font size and nothing per frame — two passes, because one pass of a blur is always fainter than the glyph it came from, which is `HUD_GLOW_PASSES`' own arithmetic in the dark direction. It lives in `DrawString`, so score, streak, caption, count and the flying popups all take it and cannot drift apart, and it is premultiplied by the text's alpha exactly as the offset copy is, so a fading popup fades its backing with it.
  - **It is deliberately TIGHTER than the flash** (`HUD_BACKING_BLUR` 12 at the text's own scale, against `HUD_GLOW_BLUR` 30 at 1.14 of it) and that is the line between the two: a flash is light coming off the glyph and grows around it, this is the ground behind the glyph and has to hug the letterform. Let it spread and it becomes the backing plate the corner readouts refuse to have. Centred on its **own** measurement, since a blurred glyph is a larger bitmap with its own render offset — the identical trap `DrawGlow`'s comment records for the halo, and it fails the same visible way, as a smudge beside the number rather than under it.
  - **Over a dark scene it costs nothing and shows nothing**, which is the point of putting it under everything rather than only under the count: black on the Moon's black sky is invisible, and the same frame's count on the Moon's *sunlit* deck — the brightest ground in the game — is the case it was checked against.
- **The streak warms as it climbs and is held when it breaks.** ×2 → ×5 ramps `MENU_TEXT` → `HUD_ACCENT` over the whole range `ScoreKeeper.MaxMultiplier` allows, so the ramp keeps meaning the same thing if the cap is retuned, and the badge on a popup is the same colour as the number in the corner. It is still held back at ×1 — it reads as something the player is carrying rather than furniture — but its *loss* is the event, so the broken value is held `STREAK_BREAK_TIME` (0.45 s), sliding down and fading. A thing that vanishes between frames is a thing nobody saw.
- **The ceiling warning is the cluster profile, not a counter — and the reason a counter was the wrong shape.** The 3D glass coming down at the cluster *was* meant to be its own warning, and a number counting to the next descent says a second time what the player can already see — so for a long time there was none, and `ScoreKeeper.ShotsToNextCeiling` sat unused. What that left unspoken is *where the glass stands*: the 3D plate sliding against a sky is nearly invisible (see "The ceiling announces itself"), the floor laser net says only that the end is near, and neither shows the glass's height against the cluster and the death line. A counter would name *when* but never *where*. The answer is a **side-cut panel** down the left edge (`DrawClusterProfile` in `PlayHud`, the only strip free in both poses between the FPS line and the balls-left readout): the glass as a bar at its true height, the cluster's balls as round markers in their own type colours at their true spacing, and the death line at the bottom — so a descent reads as the bar closing on the balls.
  - **A side cut, ISOTROPIC, drawn directly over the scene with no backing plate.** The cut is a true section: the vertical axis is world Y, the horizontal axis is each ball's projection onto the **camera's right axis** (`forward × up`, the same basis the audio listener is posed with). Both axes share ONE scale — `pixelsPerUnit = min(panelHeight/span, width/(2·halfDepth))` — so the cluster keeps its real proportions and the balls sit at their actual spacing (a ball is 1 unit across, levels 1/√2 apart). `halfDepth` is the field's half-diagonal (`FieldHalfDiagonal`, the same arithmetic `FitCannonAndGameCameraToLevel` clears the corners with). There is no border, no label, no dark fill behind it — the balls and bars draw straight over the scenery, which keeps the panel from reading as a box plastered over the world. The markers carry their own contrast (their colours as the lit ball shows them — `TypeColor`'s baked display-space palette, shared with the award popups since #153; the small baked-in sheen is what keeps the 8-ball's dark marker visible instead of the almost-white dot the old peak-lift printed).
  - **A ball marker is a procedural disc, antialiased.** `DrawDisc` draws each dot as ONE quad from a small texture the HUD generates from the disc's own radii and caches — its texels carry the disc's exact area coverage as premultiplied white (see the strip's antialiasing bullet above; the markers are the same primitive, and a staircase rim at this size read as noise). The old scanline build's objection was to a *content asset* — something to premultiply and keep in step — and a disc baked in code is neither. The marker is sized at the ball's true world diameter (`pixelsPerUnit`, since a ball is 1 unit across), so a dense cluster's dots sit edge-to-edge the way the real cluster packs — and because that radius breathes with the orbit, the fraction its whole-pixel rounding drops is spent on the sprite's scale, so the dots grow continuously rather than stepping once a turn.
  - **The panel is FIXED size and frames the whole field.** It spans from the glass's resting place at the top (`FIELD_TOP_Y + CEILING_CLEARANCE`) to the death line at the bottom, and does not resize as balls are shot and matched — a panel that grew and shrank read as something wrong with the HUD, and it shifted under the eye every shot. The glass's descent is what moves; the frame that holds it does not.
  - **`PROFILE_WIDTH` is the only lever on how big the cut reads, and #89 ("the panel reads as tiny") was the width alone.** The scale is the `min` of two terms and they are nowhere near each other: the field spans **13.16** world units vertically against a half-diagonal of **9.2–12.7**, so at the old width of 200 the width term gave **10.9 px per world unit** where the height term offered **98.8**, and the cut was drawn nine times smaller than the panel holding it. A ball came out **11 px** across at 2160p. At 480 it is **26**, and the whole cut 344 px tall instead of 143. Raising `PROFILE_HEIGHT` does nothing whatever: it feeds only the losing scale term and a centre that works out to the viewport's own middle regardless — it survives as the cap it always was. Making the height term bind would need a width of ~2 500 design units, two thirds of a 16:9 frame, so the two are not meant to meet. Design units scale off viewport **height**, so the panel holds its proportion of the frame at every aspect (12.5 % of the width at 16:9, 14.6 % at 4:3) and the `panelHeight <= width` guard cannot trip: `maxHeight` is 0.915·H against a width of 0.194·H.
  - **A ball still in the AIR is drawn too, as a RING.** `BuildClusterProfile` used to walk only the hanging structure, so a shot was invisible on the panel for its whole flight and appeared only once it had attached — which is exactly the stretch worth watching (#89). It now also walks `_shotBalls` and `_fallingBalls`, the two lists the cluster array by definition does not hold, and marks them `InFlight`. The ring is the same `DrawDisc` with an inner radius: the hole is baked into the same coverage texture, so there is one primitive rather than two that could drift apart, and a ring costs exactly what a disc does — one quad. A ring rather than a disc because the panel's subject is where the glass stands against the *cluster*, and a ball that has not attached yet must be visible without being counted by the eye as cluster. **Held to the panel's window**: the gun stands on the island well below the death line, so a shot spends the first half of its climb under the panel's floor — drawn unclamped it would sit under the death line, which on this panel means exactly one thing, *lost*. A cluster ball can never fail that test, having ended the level if it did.
  - **A ball that crosses the death line dissolves rather than popping (#134), and the two things under that line are told apart by which way they are moving.** Cutting a falling ball dead on the frame it crosses was the pop: it is still falling for another 36 units before `KILL_PLANE_Y` takes it. It now goes on being drawn past the line, its colour multiplied down to nothing over `PROFILE_SINK_FADE` — 2 world units, like everything else the panel measures, so it is the same fall at every resolution (~22 px at a 1600×900 client, 52 at 2160p, both inside the space under the panel). The rising shot above must *not* get this, or every shot would show a ball under the line for the first half of its climb, and the discriminator cannot be which list the ball came from — a shot that missed is still in `_shotBalls` on the way back down. It is the **body's own velocity** (`BallMarker.Falling`), read fresh each frame, so the panel stays what it is: rebuilt from scratch, remembering nothing. A distance and not a duration for the same reason — a timed fade would have to keep per-ball state — which also makes the dissolve last as long as the ball's speed says it should. **Measured** from the draw itself: a missed shot fell back out at 7.4 units a second and the alphas ran 0.73 down to 0.04 in even steps, **16 frames** of dissolve at 60, with the climbing shots below the line still culled throughout. (Verified numerically rather than from a capture: the film grain animates every pixel under the panel, so frame-to-frame differencing cannot pick a marker out of it — the same reason the grain goes off before judging any shader change.)
  - **The glass bar is drawn at the plate's OWN thickness, and that was the one mark that used to lie (#133).** Everything else on the panel is a literal isotropic read of the world; the bar was a fixed design-unit figure (16) with no relation to `CeilingPlate.THICKNESS`, so at the 1600×900 client it came out **7 px where the plate is 11**, its underside floated 2 px above the glass's real underside, and the topmost balls read as hanging with a gap. There is no gap: at rest the cluster hangs **flush**, the ball's top surface and the plate's underside agreeing to **0.001 of a unit** (measured on One — 7.156 against 7.157). `CeilingPlate.THICKNESS × pixelsPerUnit`, floored at 3 px so the mark the whole panel is read against cannot vanish on a short window, and the gap measures 0 px after it. The death line keeps a design-unit thickness on purpose: it is a **threshold**, not a thing, and has no world thickness to be drawn at. **A trap worth recording**, because it made the first probe of this report a liar: `CLEARANCE` places the plate 2 units over the field's topmost level, but that is the layout's *grid* position, and the constraints then pull the cluster **0.272 up** to hang off the glass. Sampled on the first frame the gap reads 0.273 and no player ever sees it — sample after the cluster has settled.
  - **The glass bar is NEUTRAL at rest and red only on the flash; the death line is ALWAYS red.** The glass is the ceiling the balls hang from — a bar that is red at rest read as a warning when none was due. It takes the alarm's red only for the flash, squared exactly as the 3D `EmissiveTint` is scaled by `_ceilingFlash²`: unmistakable on the frame the glass steps, back to neutral before the slide finishes. The death line at the bottom is the one place the red is permanent — it IS the warning, the line a ball must not cross.
  - **The cut reads the GAMEPLAY camera, not the cinematic lens.** `_gameplayCameraRight` holds the right-axis of the pose *before* the drop cinematic's blend (`UpdateCamera` resolves it before lerping towards `_cinematic`), and the profile reads that vector instead of the live camera. A cinematic swings the lens to follow the falling cluster, and a profile that turned with it would read as the HUD shaking rather than as the cluster turning — so the cut holds still while the camera flies.
  - **The balls are the live bodies, this frame.** `BuildClusterProfile` walks `_physicsBalls` exactly the way the loss check and the instance collection do — same null-check, same XZLevel sizing, the drawn position render-offset included — so a ball gliding into its cell glides in the cut too, and a swaying cluster sways in it. They are written into one reused backing array (`_profileBalls`, sized to the field's cell count) and handed to the HUD as a span — no per-frame allocation on the gameplay path, the same discipline the instancing buckets keep.
  - **Drawn first, inside the corner readouts' own `Begin/End`, and hidden with them.** It is the first call in `PlayHud.Draw` so the corner numbers and any incoming award pass over it; it lives in the same default-state `SpriteBatch` block as the readouts, in display space after the tonemap, so it adds no render pass. And it stops when the level does — the `if (_pendingOutcome == LevelOutcome.None)` that gates the whole HUD gates it too.
- **The cap is its own tier, and below it nothing changed (#180).** The streak used to warm from the neutral grey towards the one accent and that was the whole of it, so a ×5 read as a slightly hotter ×2 — and it could not have read as anything else, because `climb` is normalised 0…1 against `MaxMultiplier` and so the ramp has **nowhere higher to go by construction**. What the top of the range needed was not a hotter amber but a different *state*. At `ScoreKeeper.MaxMultiplier` the badge now changes **face** (the loud label, so the multiplier stops being the smallest figure on the HUD at the moment it is the most worth reading), takes a **travelling colour sweep** instead of a flat fill, and **flares once** on arrival. Everything under the cap is untouched.
  - **A sweep here does not reopen the palette-clash problem the single-accent rule exists for**, and the distinction is what the rule is actually about: amber is what the HUD *wears*, continuously, over thirteen scenes, so it has to survive all of them. This is two glyphs in one corner, only at the cap, only while a perfect streak is being held, and gone the moment a shot misses — the rarest state the in-play HUD has, and the one worth spending colour the HUD does not otherwise own.
  - **The ramp is a hot arc and NOT a rainbow, and that was measured rather than chosen.** It ran the full wheel first — red, orange, yellow, green, cyan, violet — and the cool half failed exactly as the rule predicts: photographed over the meadow, the cyan stop was very nearly invisible against the sky, so the badge blinked out for a third of every sweep. It runs red → orange → gold → white-hot → pink → magenta now: every stop holds against a blue sky, the meadow's green, grey stone and the Moon's black, and the white-hot stop is what makes it read as *heat* rather than as decoration.
  - **The stagger has to be small enough that neighbouring glyphs are neighbours on the ramp.** At a third of the ramp per glyph the two characters of a badge sat two full stops apart and read as two unrelated colours; at a sixth they read as one sweep passing through. The badge is drawn glyph by glyph to do this, which drops kerning between the pairs — under a pixel on a two-character string, and the price of the effect.
  - **The glow needs a floor of its own.** The pulse's heat *ends*, because it is the "this number just changed" signal; the cap does not. Without a floor a held ×5 stopped glowing between shots and dropped back to looking like an ordinary readout in a bigger font.
  - **Cost: not measurable.** Level 1, `quality=high`, 1600×900 at ssaa 2×, vsync off, paired repeats of a pinned ×2 against a pinned ×5: **1.705 / 1.720 ms** against **1.717 / 1.722 ms**. The difference between the means is 0.007 ms and the spread between the two *identical* ×2 runs is 0.015 — twice that — so it is inside the noise, which is what three extra blurred-glyph draws in one corner should be.
  - `streak=<n>` pins what the readout **shows** — the display only, never the scoring, because a lever that altered the thing it exists to look at would be worse than no lever. The capped state takes `MaxMultiplier` consecutive scoring shots to reach, i.e. somebody actually being good at the game, so without it the top tier could be neither photographed nor compared against the tier below it. `celebrate`'s reasoning again.
- **It stops when the level does.** The result screen states the score itself, so the readout behind it is the same figure said twice — and the corners would be the only thing left pinned to the screen while the camera is released and swings out around the arena. An award caught mid-flight is the visible half of that: it ages on the play clock, which the covered frame deliberately does not step (#241 runs the world under the page, not the readouts), so it would hang frozen and be reprojected against a moving camera, sliding across the frame towards a score nobody is playing for.
- **The score chases rather than jumps** (an exponential chase with a floor rate so a big award still lands promptly). A number that snaps 0 → 2 960 is not read as a number at all.
- **Every score-like figure goes through `ScoreText`** (`Game/Screens/ScoreText.cs`, #284), which groups thousands with a **space** — `12 340`, never `12,340` — and does so independently of the machine's locale; the strings are rebuilt only when their *number* changes. Both halves of that have an incident behind them. What `"N0"` groups with is a property of whatever culture the machine runs under, so with one site invariant and another left to the locale a popup read "+2 960" beside a corner score reading "2,960" — one number written two ways in one frame. Pinning the culture fixed that and left the comma, which is what #284 changed; the separator is now chosen in one place rather than inherited from either culture, so no call site can disagree with another. Formatting three of them per frame would also be three managed allocations per frame on the gameplay path, which the render-hygiene rules rule out, and the count-up means the value genuinely does change on most frames of an award.
- **Testing it needs the game actually running, and the harness traps cost a round of screenshots each.** A popup lives ~1.05 s and the flight leg is the second half of that, so a capture taken a second after the shot shows nothing and reads as "it does not work" — capture in a burst, and note a full-window `CopyFromScreen` at 1600×900 costs ~90 ms, which is about as fast as a scripted burst can sample a 0.4 s spring (crop to the corner to go faster). **Drive the menus with Enter alone and never with Down**: Enter is edge-driven and has no repeat, and pressing it with the focus cursor down only *raises* it, so three Enters reach level 1 whether the cursor started down (raise, Play, level) or up (Play, level, a no-op in play) — whereas a held Down flirts with `NAV_REPEAT_DELAY` (0.42 s), intermittently steps twice, opens the **Scene page** instead of Play and silently voids the run, changing the scene under the very measurement being taken. (A key must still be held across a frame to be seen at all; the keyboard is polled once per frame.) Confirm the window actually took focus before sending anything — unfocused, `UpdateMenuChrome`'s `IsActive` block never runs and every press is dropped. And a scripted `rmbdown` for the ADS pose that is never released leaves the **button held at the OS level** after the process is killed: Windows then treats every later click as part of a drag and the next run's menu clicks vanish silently. Release it in the harness's exit path.

## The sound (Game)

The first audio the game has (#46): the shot leaving the barrel and a ball landing in the lattice, both **synthesized from raw 16-bit PCM at startup** in `Game/Audio/ProceduralAudio.cs` and played back as plain `SoundEffect`s. `SoundEffect`'s constructor takes a PCM buffer directly, so there is no asset file, no `.mgcb` entry and no pipeline step — the same "procedural, nothing to install" line the meshes and the surface textures already take.

- **The synthesis is a small signal chain, not a tone**, and that is what separates it from a beep: layers of additive harmonics, low-passed noise transients and a sub-bass weight are mixed on a float buffer, run through a **Schroeder reverb** (four parallel comb filters into two series all-passes — the Freeverb topology) for a sense of space, normalised, and only then quantized to PCM. Baking once at load means the runtime cost of a shot is a buffer play.
- **The shot is layered like a discharge**, because a cannon is not one sound and each part of it lives in a different band: a **boom** (a 150→60 Hz pitch drop that crashes in 30 ms and then rings, plus a rumble low-passed to 190 Hz), a **zap** (a short additive sawtooth sweep, the charge's own whine), a **crackle** (noise band-passed to 1.5–5 kHz under a ~45 Hz tremolo) and a **muzzle thud**. The crackle is there for a specific reason: that band is where the ear is most sensitive, so it is what keeps the shot present and cutting **at the volume a game is actually played at**, which is far quieter than a real cannon.
- **A landing is one note per ball type** — thirteen steps up a low register, adjacent colours exactly a whole tone apart (2^(1/6) per step, 150–600 Hz; the step is the design constant since #152, so another type extends the ladder rather than squeezing every step below what the ear can tell apart) — fronted by a filtered contact click and underpinned by a sub thump. The landing's random pitch nudge is bounded **under half that step** (±0.06 octave against the 1/6-octave tone), because at the old ±0.1 two neighbouring colours' wobble ranges overlapped and a low green could land under a high red — the per-play variety must never be able to swap two colours' notes. Shorter and duller than the shot on purpose: a ball meeting its own kind should read as solid, not explosive.
- **And it is one voice per MATERIAL as well as per colour (#314).** `BallStyle` says what a ball is made of and the ear was the last part of the game not told: a glass bubble, a ball of wool and a cooling lump of lava landed with the same clack for a given colour, on the sound that fires on every single shot. A `LandedMaterial` row per style now moves everything in the bake except the colour's note and the arena's reverb — **how long it rings** (wool is dead at 58, metal sings at 6), **where its two partials sit** (harmonic 2 and 3 for stone and ceramic; **inharmonic 2.76 and 5.40 for the metal**, a struck bar's own, which beat against the fundamental instead of thickening it and are what no envelope alone can imitate), **how much sub** it has under the tone (lava 0.88, the bubble 0.14 — a film around nothing has no mass to make one), and **how bright and hard the contact click is** (the metal's 12 kHz tick against the wool's 850 Hz "pff"). A material also transposes the whole ladder — lava 0.62, the bubble 1.90 — but never its **step**, which is the one figure that must not move: the thirteen have to stay countable under every material. Each row is read off that style's own doc comment on `BallStyle` rather than invented, so the sound cannot drift from the look. **The vinyl's row is the shipped constants unchanged**, arithmetically identical down to the buffer length. The thirteen buffers for a material are baked when a level names it (`ProceduralAudio.PrepareLanded`, called where `GameplayScreen` states the render style) rather than as the whole 10 × 13 cross product at startup: that would be 130 buffers and 390 XAudio2 voices of which one level can sound thirteen, and baking on the first landing instead would spend the synthesis inside the one frame in the game that must not stall.
- **A release answers separately (#46): the lattice's snap, then the freed balls popping away in a quick rising run.** The landing tone stays blind to what came loose, deliberately; the release sound carries that half, scaled by the count the way the firework burst scales by size — bigger is louder and a shade deeper, capped at 15 balls where louder stops meaning anything. The six pops are **band-passed noise, not tones**: the theme transposes itself per pass, so a melodic run in any fixed key would land wrong against half of them, where a noise pop's "pitch" is a gesture the ear reads against nothing. The run rises and accelerates slightly — release reads as reward even though the balls fall — and a plain attach plays only the thunk.
- **Nothing plays twice the same.** Every play takes a small random pitch, and a landing is **placed where it happened** and attenuated with distance to a floor rather than to silence, so a hit on the left of the field is heard on the left and a far one is quieter but never lost.
- **The five sounds that have a place in the world are put there by the hardware (#75)**, through `AudioEmitter`/`AudioListener`/`Apply3D` rather than by a pan computed here: the shot leaves the **muzzle** (which it did not before — it was centred, and the muzzle was treated as the lens's own work), a landing sounds at the cell it stuck to, a release at the cell that broke, and a shell whistles and bursts where it actually is. The four that have no emitting object — the party popper and the three UI sounds — never reach an emitter at all, which is a stronger form of the rule that already governed them.
- **Direction from the hardware, distance from the game, and that split is the whole design.** X3DAudio's own attenuation is a `1/d` curve that would put a landing 28 units out **29 dB down** — precisely what the floor above and the fireworks' near-flat term were written to avoid, and it cannot be replaced, because XNA's `AudioEmitter` exposes no custom curve. So it is switched **off** instead: `SoundEffect.DistanceScale` is set once to 10 000, at which the output matrix sums to exactly 1.0000 from 0 to 500 units in every direction (measured, stereo and 5.1 alike). The platform then contributes placement and nothing else, and every distance law in the file survives verbatim. It is a plateau radius, not an off switch — the curve does bite, at 20 000 units — and varying it per source would quietly put an inverse-square curve back on top of the game's own.
- **`Apply3D` needs a handle, and `SoundEffect.Play` is fire-and-forget** — so each spatial buffer gets a `VoiceRing`: a fixed ring of `SoundEffectInstance`s built once at load and reused forever (4 shot, 3 per ball type, 3 release, 12 launch, 32 burst — 90 voices at thirteen types). Bounded by construction because **the framework will not bound it**: MonoGame only recycles the instances it pooled itself, and `MAX_PLAYING_INSTANCES` is `int.MaxValue` on WindowsDX, so a create-per-play design leaks a source voice per sound with no exception and no symptom. Every `Take()` **stops** the slot it hands back, and that is correctness rather than tidiness: `SoundEffectInstance.Play()` returns *early* when the instance is already playing, so a still-sounding slot would silently swallow the new sound instead of replacing it. The two firework rings are sized by the **opening barrage**, not the steady state — at the start of a display every shell slot is free, so shells go up at ~14 a second until they run out (a simulated peak of ten concurrent whistles), and all 32 shells report inside one 2.6 s window, which is why the burst ring is the shell count itself. That last one has **no margin**: a shell slot can free and refire in 2.52 s, already inside the report, so a longer burst bake or a shorter shell life starts clipping tails silently.
- **The call order around `Apply3D` is load-bearing, and each way of getting it wrong is silent.** It writes the voice's output matrix **and** its frequency ratio, so the pitch must be set *after* it or every random nudge and every size-encoded offset is wiped. `Volume` is a separate setting and is safe anywhere. `Pan` writes the **same matrix** `Apply3D` does, so it must never be touched on a placed voice — which is exactly why the no-listener-yet fallback uses it deliberately: before there is a listener there is nothing to place against, and Pan is the only way left to address that matrix. And nothing resets a reused voice's matrix between plays, so `Apply3D` is re-applied on every take rather than only when the position moved: a slot replayed without it would sound at the **previous** event's position.
- **A NaN reaching X3DAudio silences the whole audio graph** — every voice, permanently, with no exception raised and no way back. Both ends are gated on being finite: a bad camera pose leaves the last good listener standing, and a bad world point falls back to the listener's own position (a coincident emitter is measured safe, and comes back as an even spread). The listener is also posed with a **normalised** up vector crossed from world up, because X3DAudio neither normalises nor validates the orientation it is handed: an up vector of the wrong length silently skews the image and a degenerate one collapses it to dead centre.
- **The bearing is an angle now, which is why one width constant replaced two.** The old law divided a *sideways distance* by a full-width — 18 units for the cluster, 90 for the sky, because a sideways distance is scale-dependent and the two live at completely different scales. An angle is not, so the sky needs no constant at all: the honest bearing up there is already **more than twice as wide** as `SKY_PAN_WIDTH` made it. The near field is the opposite case and keeps one exaggeration (`NEAR_WIDEN`, 1.9): a cluster ten units across seen from thirty subtends about twenty degrees, so the *truthful* image of a landing is narrower than the mix has ever had, and at 1.9 the edge of the field lands within a fraction of a decibel of where it has always sat. Set it to 1 for the plain truth — it was tried, and it reads as the stereo collapsing.
- **Overhead is folded towards the middle, and without that a bang comes out of one speaker while the player looks straight up at it.** A stereo image carries a horizontal bearing and nothing else — measured: a source at a fixed ground bearing returns an *identical* matrix at heights 0, 10, 25, 52, 80 and 130 — so a shell nearly above the lens has its side decided by a few units of horizontal offset, and about four shells a display would crack hard over. The sideways leg is scaled by the cosine of the elevation, which is what every height channel's downmix does and which leaves anything near the horizon exactly where it is. The old `lateral/90` law damped this case by accident; an angle law does not.
- **What "positional" does *not* buy, so nobody oversells it.** There is **no height cue** on stereo *or* on 5.1 — X3DAudio's law is the azimuth in the horizontal plane, measured identical at every elevation on both. **Doppler is refused** rather than merely unused (`DopplerScale` 0): no velocity is written, but the lens is not a head — the ADS lean moves it at ~140 units a second, 40 % of the speed of sound, and a listener velocity would detune everything sounding the instant the player leans in. `SoundEffect.SpeedOfSound` is **dead code** on WindowsDX and setting it would be a wrong "why" left standing. And surround placement is real — a source dead ahead goes to the centre speaker, one behind to the rear pair — but it is **unverified on this machine**, which is stereo.
- **The move cost 2.92 dB at the centre of the image and it could not be avoided.** X3DAudio's matrix sums to 1 in every direction where the pan law it replaces wrote *both* channels at full for a centred sound: a centred sound is exactly 6.02 dB quieter, of which only 3.10 dB is recoverable, because `Volume` throws above 1. `BASE_VOLUME` went 0.7 → 1.0 to take that back. A sound at the edge of the field loses nothing (both laws write [0, 1] hard over), so the shift is **position-dependent** and no single constant can undo it — the wide sounds gain relative to the centred ones, which mostly means the celebration reads a little louder and much more even across the sky. The popper and the three UI sounds still play through the centre-hot pan law, so they are trimmed by exactly the old `BASE_VOLUME` (`NON_SPATIAL_TRIM`, 0.7) to leave the menu's mix precisely where it was. If the effects end up sitting too low under the bed and the theme, the honest fix is the other end — `AMBIENCE_VOLUME`, `MUSIC_VOLUME`, `MENU_VOLUME` and `FANFARE_VOLUME` each times ~0.71 — and that is an ear's call, not arithmetic, so it has deliberately not been made here.
- **The listener is the host's, posed once a frame from the one camera there is** — never the session's. Sound is made with no session standing: a whole celebration of it over a frozen gameplay screen, and the menu's clicks over an orbiting backdrop. It is posed from the pose the *previous* frame was drawn from, which is exactly the staleness the panning it replaces already had (half a unit at sixty frames a second). Its up axis is crossed from **world** up rather than taken from the camera, which costs hundredths of a decibel even under the drop cinematic's dutch tilt and buys two things: the soundfield can never be left tipped by a roll the camera is still carrying from a screen the player has left, and the basis stays the one the cluster profile is drawn on. On the very first frame of a run the camera has not been posed yet — it is the *stack* that poses it, further down the same Update — so the fallback plays centred. With the `celebrate` argument that is the display's first launch whistle; the party popper beside it is centred by construction rather than by the fallback, never having reached an emitter at all. The latch is set once and never cleared, so a bad pose later leaves the last good listener standing rather than dropping back to centre.
- **The menu clicks (#46), and one wrapper covers every input device.** `MenuButton` wraps each entry's action, so the press sounds whether it arrived through Myra's `Click` (the mouse) or through the `Tag` that `ActivateNavEntry` invokes (the pad and the arrow keys) — an entry added later cannot forget its click. One 90 ms buffer serves all three UI sounds: the press plays it straight, the focus cursor's step plays it pitched up (`PlayUiTick` — faster playback is also shorter, which is what a step wants, and far quieter, because a held direction walks the list and the tick repeats with it), and backing out plays it pitched down, so leaving sounds lower than entering. The click itself is a dry "thock" — a low-passed knock over a fast 900→420 Hz drop with a touch of 150 Hz weight — with **no reverb and no pan**, the party popper's reasoning: the UI lives at the player's hand, not out in the scene. A screen change restores the focus cursor by assignment rather than through `StepNavFocus`, deliberately, so arriving on a page does not tick.
- **The effects stay MONO, and that is a constraint rather than an omission.** #119 gave the music a stereo image and deliberately stopped there. These buffers are placed by the hardware through `Apply3D`, which takes a mono source — a positional sound has no left and right of its own, because where it is *is* what the matrix is about — so baking them in stereo would not widen them, it would take the placement away. The ambience beds were the open question the music's change did not settle — they are not spatialized, so they *could* carry width — and #146 answered it (see "The ambience"). It needed a **different technique** rather than the same one applied again: a bed is noise, and noise is widened by decorrelating the two channels, not by panning a layer that would only move the point it comes from.
- **The star cue is the one UI sound that is a TONE, and it is not on the click's buffer (#139).** The result screen's rating lands one star at a time, and each landing plays `PlayStarEarned` — a small struck chime over a 2 ms noise tick for the hammer. It could not be the click repitched like the tick and the back are: the click is a dry knock, and a knock four times over reads as a stutter rather than as a reward. It could not be a **noise** gesture the way the release's pops deliberately are, because a noise "pitch" has nothing to read against. And the run **rises**, which is what makes a rating something the player hears being counted; the last star is lifted slightly (`STAR_FINAL_LIFT`), the only thing separating a four-star clear's fourth star from a three-star clear's third.
- **It does NOT play into silence, and that wrong sentence is what #158 cost.** This entry used to say the chime was played over "a stopped world with no music under it" — which was **never true**: `CheckLevelCleared` starts the victory fanfare the instant the field clears, *before the result screen exists*, so every star has always landed inside its ~9 seconds. Three consequences followed from the one bad premise, and all three were reported as the chime sounding cheap. It was baked on a **free bar's inharmonic ratios** (1, 2.76, 5.40, 8.93) — right for a bell struck into silence, dissonant against tonal harmony in any key. It sounded at a **fixed 880 Hz**, so it agreed with the piece only when that piece happened to roll A. And it stepped **~2.3 semitones** a star, which is an interval in no key at all.
  - **The fix is that the chime asks the fanfare what it is.** `ProceduralMusic.TryGetFanfare` reports the piece's root and tempo, the chime is retuned to that root's own **major triad** (0-4-7-12, consonant against every chord of its I–V–vi–IV by construction), the reveal takes **one beat a star** from its BPM, and the partials moved to a **tubular bell's** near-harmonic 1:2:3 with one inharmonic 4.2 on top for shimmer.
  - **The key is answered when the fanfare is REQUESTED, not when it is heard**, and that is the part that took two attempts. The piece is baked on a background thread — **over three seconds on the weakest machine**, measured — while the result page opens on the frame the level cleared, so a first version that read the key at `Enter` got "there is no fanfare" every single time, and a second that *waited* for the sound held the rating longer than any player would sit through. The roll moved out of the baker into `RollFanfare` on the calling thread, so the key is known immediately and only the beat grid needs the audio; when the audio is not there yet the row keeps the authored cadence and is still in tune.
  - **`SoundEffect.Play`'s pitch is in octaves over −1…1**, so the whole four-star run has to fit inside one octave of shift. The root is therefore placed in the octave *below* the baked A5 (an offset of −12…−1), leaving the triad room to reach +11 at the top star. Placed above instead, the fourth star of a four-star clear would silently clamp to the wrong note. Verified across the rolled roots: A gives −12/−8/−5/0 and C gives −9/−5/−2/+3, both an in-key major triad and both inside the range.
- **The buffers are the host's, not the session's**, built once in `LoadContent` and disposed in `UnloadContent` — the same split as the camera. A level is torn down and rebuilt per play; the sounds outlive that, and re-baking fifteen buffers per level would be work for nothing.
- **`SoundEffect.Play`'s pitch is an offset in OCTAVES, not a frequency multiplier**, and getting that wrong made every sound in the game wrong at once. It takes −1…1 where **0 is the sound as baked** and 1 is a full octave up; `NextPitch` returned `1 + jitter`, written as if it were a multiplier, so the shot, every landing, the whistle and the report all played **an octave above** what they were synthesized as. Everything came out thin, toy-like and squeaky — and no amount of retuning the synthesis could have fixed it, because the synthesis was never what was wrong. It is centred on zero now. **If a procedural sound here ever sounds wrong in a way the maths does not explain, check this first.**
- **Peak normalisation is exactly wrong for an explosion, and that is the second reason the bang was not a bang.** The loudest sample of a report is its *crack* — a few milliseconds, tall and thin — so scaling the peak to 1 scales the **body** down by whatever that transient happened to reach, leaving a tick with a quiet thud behind it. `Loudness(signal, targetRms, ceiling)` drives the RMS instead and rounds the transient off through a `tanh`, which is what every explosion effect ever made does; the burst, the popper and the cannon's own shot all take it, and `Normalize` stays for the sounds that are a tone rather than a transient.
- **The roll is what puts a firework over a landscape**, and a reverb cannot do it: `RollingEcho` lays down slap-backs, the report arriving again off every building and hillside around. A reverb alone gives a smooth wash and no roll. **But its spacing and its level both have to be right, and both were wrong first:**
  - **Over about 40 ms apart the ear resolves each repeat as its own event.** Four taps at 60–85 ms made the burst come out as "bum-bum-bum-bum" — a flutter echo, the sound of a corridor. Nine taps starting at 23 ms and stretching by an **irrational** ratio fuse into one event with a tail; the irrational spacing is what stops the repeats reinforcing into a pitch.
  - **The roll must sit UNDER the direct report, not double it.** At a high feedback the first taps land 20–60 ms behind the crack and add a second wavefront on top of it: measured, the envelope stopped peaking on the first sample and climbed to its maximum a tenth of a second in, which the ear hears as a soft "whoomph" instead of a hard "BUM". `RollingEcho` takes a `mix` for exactly this. With it at 0.42 the very first 10 ms window is back at 89 % of the sound's loudest — an immediate attack, which is as fast as a 33 Hz body can physically get there.
- **A vibrato is a shimmer, not a wobble.** The whistle's spin ran at 7 Hz and 2.8 % depth, and at that rate the ear tracks each swing as its own note: it came out as "vu-vu-vu-vu" rather than as a whistle. Shallow and fast (0.6 % at 16 Hz) is what a spinning shell actually does to a tone.
- **The launch is a SPARKLER, not a siren, and its pitch was got wrong once in each direction.** It began at 620→1950 Hz, was reported as squeaky and taken down to 430→1320 — but the squeak was never the synthesis, it was the octave bug above, and with that fixed the lowered tone sat in the register of a **horn**. It runs 900→2600 Hz now, over **0.55 s** rather than 1.15 (at over a second the ear has time to hear it *as a tone*, and a slow rising tone is a siren by definition), and the balance is pushed hard onto the **fizz** — a firework leaving the ground is a burning fuse and a jet of gas before it is a tone at all. It plays at **0.08** against the report's 0.85: with a shell going up every fraction of a second, anything audible enough to identify turns the display into a chorus of kettles, so it is a texture rather than a sound the ear stops on. Measured against its predecessor: half the length, **rough spectral centroid 2 134 → 3 036 Hz**, and the burst untouched at 270 Hz — which was the point, since the report and its crackle were what worked.
- The shells' visual rise came down to 0.62–0.95 s to match: a shell still climbing a second after its own hiss has finished reads as two unrelated things.
- **Measured rather than guessed at** (the bakes were dumped to WAV and analysed, then the dump removed): the burst runs 2.6 s at a **crest factor of 3.64** with **94 % of its energy below 150 Hz** and still a third of its opening level 800 ms in — a compressed, deep bang with a tail. The cannon's shot is 76 % low at a crest of 4.04. Those are the numbers that say "bang" rather than "click", and they are cheap to re-check if it ever regresses.
- **The volume is the player's now (#46), and the split is authored-mix × player-gain.** The mix keeps its tuning in constants (`BASE_VOLUME` here; `MUSIC_VOLUME` and `FANFARE_VOLUME` in the music; `AMBIENCE_VOLUME` in the beds) and four settings rows — Volume, Effects, Music, Ambience — scale it in quarters, "Off" at zero. Each subsystem takes one product through a `Gain` property (`master × its own row`), written only by the host's `ApplyVolumes`, so the two cannot disagree about what the master row means. The effects apply it on the **next** play (the `FireworkDuck` reasoning — nothing there sounds long enough to hear the difference), while the music pushes it onto the instances **already sounding**, because a two-minute pass and a nine-second fanfare are. The rows cycle *downwards* (the reason to click one is almost always "quieter"), `mute` on the command line starts the master at zero for the screenshot/benchmark harnesses, and like every setting none of it is persisted (see "Adaptive quality" in `docs/game-shell.md`).
- **The celebration adds three more, and they are the reason the noise source grew a seed.** A **launch** (below); a **burst** (a crack, a 110→38 Hz boom under it, and a *crackle* of burning stars gated by a fast random stutter — the crackle outlasts the boom by half a second, and that is what separates a firework from a gunshot); and a **party popper**, deliberately close and dry where the shells are big and wet, which is what makes the shells read as distant. `Noise(i, seed)` and `MakeNoiseArray(samples, seed)` exist because these mix **two** noise layers each: the source is a function of the sample index, so two unseeded layers are the *same* sequence differing only in how they were filtered, and summing two filtered copies of one sequence is correlated — it thins out and stops sounding like two separate things.
- **A firework's pan is not a ball's**, and that finding outlived the code that carried it. `PanFor` took one fixed full-width sized to the cluster (18 units); a shell bursts a hundred up and as far out again, and at the cluster's width every one but the shell straight overhead would pan hard to one side — so the width became a per-source argument (`SKY_PAN_WIDTH`, 90). Both constants are gone with `PanFor` itself (#75, above): a *sideways distance* is scale-dependent and needs one constant per scale, an *angle* is not and needs none. The half of the bullet that was never about the pan is untouched — the burst's distance term is still near-flat, because a firework *is* far away and rolling it off the way a landing does makes every burst a whisper.

## The ambience (Game)

Every backdrop sounds like itself now (#46): one looping bed per scene — surf for the sea, a wind for each terrain, a distant traffic rumble for the cities, a near-subliminal drone for space, an ethereal two-band shimmer breathing in opposite phase for the dream, hollow air with sparse water drips for the cavern (the campfire's crackle gate slowed to a few soft taps a second), the sea's wash at a calm remove under a light warm breeze for the tropical beach — synthesized from raw PCM in `Game/Audio/ProceduralAmbience.cs` and crossfaded when the scene changes. The host owns it beside the music, `SetScene` (the one scene writer) retargets it, and it runs off the frame like the clouds: the scene is on screen whether or not a session stands, so its sound is too, **pause included**.

- **Filtered noise, never a tone**, and that is the design rule of the whole file: a bed sits under the music and the effects for the entire run, and anything with a pitch would either fight the theme (which transposes itself per pass) or be noticed — and a bed that is noticed is a bed that is too loud. The one near-tonal thing in the set, the neon city's electric hum, is a narrow **band of noise** around 100–150 Hz, not an oscillator.
- **The loops are sealed by construction, and the seam was measured.** Every envelope runs a whole number of cycles per 16 s loop, and each bed renders a second past the loop point that is folded back onto the head under an equal-power ramp — continuous play of a loop is exactly the head plus the previous pass's ring-out. Measured at the wrap: the sample step across the seam is **0.1–1.5× the mean step** of the bed itself, i.e. indistinguishable from any other pair of samples; no click.
- **Levels are RMS targets per scene** (sea 0.16 down to space 0.05), set at bake so the mix is authored, and the band splits confirm each bed is what it claims: the cities put 52–56 % of their energy under 200 Hz (rumble), the forest 69 % above 2 kHz (rustle), space 82 % low (drone), the sea spread across all three (wash and foam a quarter-cycle apart, the hiss of a wave arriving after its weight).
- **The savanna carries its campfire** — the scene visibly has one, so its bed audibly does: band noise gated by a fast random stutter (~47 re-rolls a second), the firework crackle's trick at a hearth's scale, each snap opening the gate at once and the gate falling with a tail.
- **The crossfade is 1.5 s, equal-power, and only ever two instances live** — the bed fading out and the one fading in; an instance is made per arrival and disposed once it has fully faded. A retarget mid-fade lets the outgoing bed go where it stands, audible only when scenes are clicked through faster than the fade — the scene picker, not play. Volumes are written only on the frames the fade or the settings actually moved them.
- **They are in STEREO since #146, and for NOISE that means decorrelation rather than panning.** The obvious approach — give each layer a pan, the way an instrument is seated in a mix — does not work here and is the trap this was written around: a bed is filtered noise, and panning noise does not widen it, it moves the point it comes from, because two channels carrying the same sequence at two levels are still one source. So each layer draws a **shared** sequence and one of its **own per side**, mixed constant-power (`left = shared·cos θ + uniqueL·sin θ`, `θ = width·π/2`), which puts the inter-channel correlation at exactly `cos²θ` and — the three sequences being independent and unit-variance — leaves each channel's power at 1 for *every* width. Width is therefore a dial on the image alone and not on the level, which matters more here than anywhere else in the game because these levels were settled by ear once already. It costs almost nothing: `Noise(i, seed)` is a pure function of the index and two seeds are uncorrelated by construction, which is the property `BakeScene` already spent a seed per layer on. The per-side offsets are **+5101/+9173 and not +1**, or a layer's side would be drawn from the very next layer's own sequence.
  - **Width is per layer and per scene, and "wider" is not the goal for all thirteen.** The rule inside a bed is that **low bands stay nearer the middle** — a very low sound gives the ear almost no direction anyway, so decorrelating it buys little image and costs the most on a mono downmix. Across beds it is a judgement about the *place*: the mountain's gusts and the forest's canopy are fully decorrelated because an exposed basin and a canopy genuinely surround you; the sea keeps a middle for the swell's weight and opens out on the foam; the cities hold their rumble near the centre and widen the murmur above it; the dream puts the width on the contrast its two antiphase bands already had, so the image opens and closes as the pair breathes. **Space and the Moon stay nearly a point on purpose** — an enveloping void is a contradiction, and this file's own rule is that a bed the player notices is too loud whatever its level says. So is the `default` arm, which is meant to be conspicuously featureless.
  - **The crackles really are panned, because a snap is a point event.** A burning knot and a falling drop each *have* a place, so each snap rolls its own side from its slot index and holds it for its whole tail (a drip that slid across the room while it rang would be worse than a centred one). The savanna's fire is kept to a **narrow** spread — a hearth a few feet across, where a wide scatter would read as a ring of fires around the player — and the cavern's drips take the **widest** in the set, which is the thing that turns a hollow noise into a room with a roof on it.
  - **Measured, all thirteen, through the private baker with no playback path.** Every bed's RMS is unchanged to four decimals (sea 0.1600, space 0.0500, Moon 0.0350 …), so the authored mix is exactly where it was. Correlation lands on the maths: **0.907 and 0.904** for space and the Moon against a predicted `cos²(0.1π)` = 0.905, **−0.005 and 0.014** for the mountains and forest where both layers are fully spread, and the cities at **0.49/0.59** with their low bands held in. Side/mid runs **0.22** (space, Moon) to **1.01** (mountains). Summing to mono costs **−0.21 dB** on the narrow beds and at most **−3.03 dB** on the widest — which is the theoretical floor for fully decorrelated material, i.e. **nothing cancels anywhere**. The seam still closes in **both** ears (wrap step 0.19–2.63× the mean step, L and R alike) — worth stating, because folding one channel and not the other opens a seam *in one ear only*, which is markedly harder to notice than one in both. The one blemish: the cavern sits **0.40 dB** left, because ~48 randomly-panned drips in a 16 s loop are too few a sample to average to centre; it is well under the ear's threshold at this level and chasing it would make the drips mechanical.
- **The beds have a volume row of their own** (`master × ambience`, through the same `Gain` idiom as everything else): how much atmosphere sits under the music is a taste, and chaining it to the effects row would turn the shot down with it. The authored level itself was retuned by ear after the first build — from 0.16 to a quarter of it — because at 0.16 the beds sat *over* the theme and buried it, and an ambience the ear picks out over the music is too loud by definition.
- Verified in the running game with the keyboard-driven walk: boot fade-in on the pinned scene, four picks on the scene page — one retargeted mid-fade at 400 ms — then Escape back, with the bed continuing under a played level and its pause throughout; no errors, clean stderr. Re-run after #146 for the case the stereo bake actually put at risk — a **crossfade between two stereo beds**, cavern into sea — which needed nothing: the fade writes instance `Volume`, which knows nothing about channels.

## The music (Game)

`Game/Audio/ProceduralMusic.cs` — one file, and **five fully authored pieces** (#229): each is synthesized from raw PCM on a background thread at the splash, once, and then played over for as long as levels are being played. No tracker file, no asset, no pipeline step: the scores are a handful of arrays and the instruments are oscillators, the same line the sound effects, the meshes and the surface textures all take. It plays while a level is being played and stops with it.

There are **five compositions** — `MusicTheme.Pulse`, `Bohemia` (#120), `Nocturne` (#162), `Mural` (#264, which replaced #164's Dechovka in the same slot) and `Ember` (#163) — separate pieces, not one piece with a mood switch, sharing only this file's instruments and its one rule — that a piece is authored to the last ornament and always sounds the same way. The bullets below are Pulse, the ~2:30 eurodance track the game was built on and the one it opens on; the named parts further down cover what each later piece had to be different in to be worth having. Which one a level plays is "The rotation" at the end of them.

- **The front end has its own piece now (#46), and it is looped by the framework where the themes are fed through a chain.** The menu is visited for moments rather than minutes, so its own arrangement is small — and authored like every other piece here (#229; it rolled its tempo, key, progression and line direction per run until then): two rounds of held pads — chords over the theme's diatonic progressions, their root an octave under for the warmth a bass would bring — under a quarter-note line and an every-other-bar sparkle on the lobby's own **`Keys`**, an electric-piano voice; then the **groove** (a four-on-the-floor kick, off-beat hats and the theme's own off-beat bass figure — no clap, no ride, a step under the theme's levels, so the lobby stays a lobby), then the **refrain** — `MENU_HOOK`, one syncopated chord-tone motif stated identically across three chords and resolved onto a held octave on the fourth, played twice over the running groove while the line and the sparkle stand aside (two keys lines at once is mud, not counterpoint) — and the groove again to walk it off. The ~77 s loop means the hook returns now and then rather than nagging, and the wrap from full groove back to bare pads reads as a breakdown rather than a restart. Measured: intro RMS ~0.055 at 26 % below 200 Hz, groove and refrain ~0.117–0.118 at 58 % — the bass's arrival is real, and the refrain's identity is deliberately melodic rather than a level step. The line sat on the theme's square-wave `Arp` first and **read as a touch-tone phone**: a bare square with a fast decay is a texture inside a full mix and a beep on its own, which is what a slow exposed line makes it. The keys are a fundamental-heavy body with a bright *inharmonic* tine partial that fades several times faster (the attack says "struck", its quick fade is what stops it beeping), a ring long enough that each note runs a step past the next one's start — a phrase, not four plinks — and a shallow tremolo so the ring stays alive. The loop's **seam is closed by construction**: the piece renders a bar of room past the loop point and folds whatever rings into it back onto the head before the cut, so the join carries the same overlap every other bar boundary does. **Which music the moment wants is the stack question**: the host plays the loop exactly while no `GameplayScreen` is on the stack, and the theme's lifecycle stays the session's — with the one ownerless gap closed: leaving to the main menu keeps the session but must not keep its music, so the host fades the theme out there (#211) and re-wants it on Continue (which comes back *without* a `BuildLevel`; a fresh build's own `Play` is the "already sounding" no-op, so the two writers cannot fight). A pause deliberately keeps the theme running — the game is stopped, not left.
- **Every piece is authored, and nothing is rolled (#229).** The rule here used to be *random parameters, never random notes*: a pass drew its own tempo, key, progression, arpeggio direction and ornament densities out of the piece's own bands, so no two passes were the same piece of music and no two runs of the game sounded alike. The owner ended it — *"a single composition varied per pass sounds weird"*, and *"we should always pick what sounds best"*, which a roll cannot do by construction, since four passes in five are then not the best one. What replaced each roll is a decision: one tempo (the middle of the band it used to roll inside, which is also what each piece's documented length is now measured at — Pulse 128, Bohemia 116, Nocturne 96, Mural 108, Ember 134 notated / 67 felt, the menu loop 100), the piece's own key, one progression named in its `Score`, and for every ornament a **placement** instead of a probability — the ghost snare as a two-bar figure, the bass octave closing the round's second and fourth bars, the timpani's flam at the top of each round, Mural's glide alternating with its step-home tail. A probability is only meaningful against a roll; a placement is a phrase the ear can learn. Variety comes from **more pieces in the same style** now — every level file names its theme (#194), so a chapter can be given a piece of its own without a line of code in the player.
- **What that bought besides the intent.** A level boundary costs **no synthesis at all** where it used to start a fresh two-minute bake (Bohemia's is 4.7 s of arithmetic on the development desktop, Nocturne's and Ember's 2.2–2.3, Pulse's and Mural's ~1.1, the menu's 0.5 — all five run at once at the splash and are ready several menus before the first of them is wanted). The float-to-PCM conversion of thirteen million samples moved off the frame that submits the buffer and into that one render. And the five pieces are held as 16-bit PCM rather than as floats: **~26 MB each**, half of what the same five cost before.
- **The melodies are written as CHORD TONES, not as pitches**, and that is what lets a piece be written over any progression in its pool and re-keyed by one number. A melody written as absolute pitches only fits the chords it was written over; written as degrees of whatever chord is underneath, the same motif transposes itself and is consonant by construction — which is also the literal form of "one motif moved across the chords", the thing that made it a hook in the first place.
- **The outro was why the handover could be left frame-costed — until #212 took the cost away.** A piece is submitted as a buffer that plays **once**, so it has to be put on again when it ends. That used to be a per-frame `State` poll in `Update`: a finished pass was only noticed the frame after it ended, and starting a fresh voice added its own latency on top — a frame or two of dead air, which is #212's whole complaint. The pieces now play through one `DynamicSoundEffectInstance` whose queue is fed the next buffer while the current one is still sounding (#212), so XAudio2 starts buffer N+1 the sample buffer N ends and the join costs nothing at all. The outro keeps its job regardless, and it is the bigger half of the join: the arrangement *ends*, fading over its whole last section, so the join lands in silence — measured, the last two seconds of a piece are at an RMS of **0.0016–0.0019**, about −55 dB, and three pieces rest in authored silence for 1.7–2.5 s past the fade (a rest that is part of the outro's last steps, not padding). The prelude is the same idea from the other end (no kit in it at all, first two seconds at **0.073** against a chorus's 0.23) so the piece arrives rather than crashing in. All six baked pieces were probed at their seams after the change (through the private bakers, no playback path): every join steps by less than the piece's own internal p999 sample delta — the menu loop's hardware wrap by 0.001 against 0.105, and the then-fourth piece's chain join by 0.137 against 0.293, which was the downbeat's own attack out of an authored rest, not a cut. (#264 later replaced that piece; Mural's outro measures an RMS of **0.0000** over its last two seconds, so its join lands in literal silence.)
- **A switch fades; only an ending stops dead (#211).** Every place one piece merely REPLACES another used to cut: leaving to the main menu cut the theme mid-beat, starting a level cut the lobby loop mid-groove, a retry from the pause menu cut the sounding pass, and clicking through a result screen cut a still-ringing fanfare mid-chord. Each takes a fade now — the theme 0.9 s, the loop 0.5 s, the fanfare 0.4 s — applied **squared** (the outros' own curve) as a multiplier beside `Gain`, so a fade and a settings change cannot fight over one `Volume`; volumes are written only on the frames a fade moved them, the ambience's discipline. **Only the outgoing side ever ramps**: every arrival here is authored soft (the theme's prelude, the loop's held pads — and a fanfare is an announcement, correct arriving hard), so fading the leaving piece alone already is the crossfade. The level endings keep their dead stop: there the silence is the message, the fireworks' reports land in it, and a dance track carrying on over a loss is the wrong feeling entirely. `Stop` is the endings' call and `FadeOut` every switch's, which is the whole rule — and `Stop` takes any still-retiring chain with it, or "dead" would be a half-truth on the one call whose point is the silence. The ambience never had the fault: its 1.5 s equal-power crossfade predates this and is untouched.
    - **The chain made the theme's half simpler than the design it was ported from** (the patch preserved in #211's own comment, cut against the pre-#212 `_instance`). That design faded the sounding instance in place, which needed a second fade of its own and a special case so a `Play` arriving mid-fade did not resurrect the piece being left. Here the leaving chain is **retired into a slot** — `_retiring`, faded and disposed at silence — and `_voice` is nulled with the handover, so the new piece's chain opens underneath it and the two genuinely overlap for the fade's width. The sounding voice's volume is therefore a constant (a pass plays at full or it is not the pass any more), the second fade is not needed and neither is the special case. Only the retiring chain is fed nothing: `Update` submits passes to `_voice` alone, so a retired chain plays out what it already holds and no further.
    - **Verified by driving the real switches** rather than by reading the calls: three temporary probe keys on the always-running update (removed before the commit) took the leave-to-menu, the return and an ending, with the fades printing their own envelope. Leaving retired the chain and disposed it at silence; the return walked the loop's squared level down to 0.000 over its window and stopped it there; **the ending printed a dead stop with the voice still in hand** — the negative control the issue asks for, that the theme still cuts into the fireworks' silence. Interrupting a fade a second in (leave, then return) came back clean. What no probe can answer is whether 0.9 / 0.5 / 0.4 s *feel* right, which is the one judgement this issue leaves to the ear.
- **A level comes in on the piece's verse, not on its prelude (#201) — and the prelude is not cut, it is just not what a level opens on.** The complaint was that the music "only starts playing quite a long time after the level begins", and the envelope agreed with it on every piece: each one opens on a prelude with no kit in it, and measured two seconds at a time, the first 14–20 s sit **9–13 dB under the piece's own loudest**. That is right at the top of a *piece* — it is how a piece arrives rather than crashes in, and the outro is the same idea from the other end — and wrong at the top of a *level*, where the player is already shooting. So a `Score` names the section a level enters on, `EntryOffset` turns it into a byte offset, and the chain's **head** is submitted from there; every repeat after it is the whole piece from the top, so the arrangement is heard entire the moment the piece comes round. Measured, entry against the envelope reading it lands on: Pulse **30.0 s → −1 dB** (was −10), Bohemia **33.1 → −3** (−13), Nocturne **40.0 → −1** (−10), Mural **35.6 → −2** (−9), Ember **14.3 → −6** (−11). Ember is the one that enters a section earlier than the rest and the one that lands lowest, both for the same reason: its verses are **half-time** by design, so −6 dB is the piece playing its verse and not a piece still gathering itself; entering a section later would have opened levels on a chorus.
    - **The entry is `Advance`'s and nowhere else, because everything that reaches `Advance` is a level opening** — a build, a retry, a switch of piece, a Continue out of the menu — while everything that is not goes through the feed, which submits the piece whole. A pause does not stop the music, so a Continue does not re-enter it: `Play` returns at once while a voice exists.
    - **One rounding site, or the entry would miss the downbeat.** `SamplesPerStep` is now the only place a step's length is rounded to whole samples, and both the bakes and `EntryOffset` take the grid from it — computing it twice is how an entry ends a fraction of a step off the boundary it is supposed to open on. Verified in the running game rather than in the tool: `play level=Heart` submitted its head at **35.556 s of 162.222 s**, the section boundary `MusicBake` prints for Mural, with the whole-piece repeat queued 14 ms behind it.
- **Every piece is in hand, all the time** — one slot per composition, filled at the splash and never refilled (#229; it was one *pass* per composition from #120, refilled as each was consumed, see "The second theme"). The chain holds at most one buffer queued ahead of the sounding one, and the feed puts the piece on again the moment the queue has room, so a repeat is literally the same buffer. The old instances are disposed only once the replacement exists, so a failure part-way through leaves the previous chain intact and playable.
- **Measured, all six pieces, through `Tools/MusicBake`** (which renders them to .wav and prints these numbers; there is no playback path in it): Pulse **150.0 s**, Bohemia **198.6**, Nocturne **182.5**, Mural **162.2**, Ember **145.1**, the menu loop **76.8** — each within a few seconds of the length its section below documents, because the frozen tempo is the middle of the band those lengths were measured across. RMS **−14.9 to −15.2 dBFS** for all five themes (the menu loop −19.5, a lobby under a dance floor), peak −0.8 to −1.6 with no clipping, channel balance within 0.1 dB and mono fold within 0.5 dB. Rendered twice, every piece comes out **bit for bit identical** (SHA1 over the .wav), where three runs of the seeded build produced three different SHA1s — which is the check that says "authored" is a property of the code and not of a lucky seed. The point of the row itself is that **freezing the scores moved none of them**: the same six numbers off the last seeded renderings were 150–201 s and −14.9 to −15.1 dBFS. A switch between two pieces still cannot be heard as a level step.
- **It is in STEREO since #119, and the width is a seating plan rather than a widener.** Every piece here was one mono buffer every voice summed into, so a dozen instruments played back from a single point; the buffer is interleaved stereo now and each instrument has a seat. Two rules decide it, and the second is where the width actually comes from. **Low stays centre** — kick, bass, timpani and the snare are panned nowhere at all: below ~200 Hz the ear takes almost no direction from level difference, so panning them buys no width, and it costs, because a hard-panned low end loses half its power the moment anything downmixes to mono, which is every laptop and phone this will be played on. Since 61–70 % of these pieces' energy is below 200 Hz, that rule is most of the mix by energy and none of it by width. **Width comes from what is already detuned**: the lead is two saws twelve cents apart and the string section is seven, written to beat against one another, and pulling those apart makes the beating sweep *across* the field instead of wobbling at one point — genuinely different signals on the two sides rather than one signal at two levels, which is why it is far wider than moving a finished mono note off centre. Their **notes** stay where they were; only the detuned voices move. The rest is a kit seated as a kit (hats one side, ride answering from the other), the arp ping-ponged on alternate sixteenths — it never stops, so it is what keeps the image alive through the melody's rests — and the pad and brass chords opened out across their voices.
  - **Seat by the voice's INDEX, never by its pitch**, and this one was measured wrong first. Pitch-derived seating looks equivalent and is not: `note % 12` rotates with the piece's key, so the same chord shape lands somewhere different in every piece, and each piece leans to whichever side its key happened to put the chord tones on. The index is a property of the arrangement, which is what the seating should follow. (It was measured against a harder version of the same fault: while the key was rolled per pass, the image moved between two playings of ONE piece.)
  - **A part that can ever play ALONE must not take half of a symmetric pair's seat.** The defeat fanfare's harmony is conditional on the run's intensity, so seating its melody as one of a pair left the tune singing from one speaker on every quieter loss — **measured at 1.41 dB of lean** before the melody went back to centre and the *harmony* became the part that moves. The general form: centre is for whatever may be the only thing sounding.
  - **The limiter reads the interleaved buffer as one signal and drives it with a single number, and that is load-bearing.** Measuring and driving the two channels independently would turn every loud moment on one side into a level change on that side alone — the image pumping, the classic way to wreck a mix while leaving each channel individually correct. If it ever grows a real envelope follower the gain must stay linked.
  - **Measured through the private bakers, three seeds each, no playback path involved** *(before #229 froze the scores; the seed spread is what the piece could then roll)*. Everything the rows above record is unchanged — Pulse 134.5–138.5 s at RMS **0.1778–0.1779** *(as #119 left it; #186 rewrote Pulse's arrangement and its figures are in that part below)*, Bohemia 194.4–200.7 s at **0.1765–0.1767**, **zero clipped samples** anywhere and DC 0.0000–0.0016 — because constant-power panning moves energy without changing how much of it there is, and the limiter still sets the loudness. What is new: **channel balance** within 0.15 dB on both long pieces (Pulse −0.14, Bohemia −0.06, victory +0.11, defeat −0.04, the menu loop +0.3 — the lobby's keys carry that piece almost alone, so their seat is deliberately shallow); **side/mid above 200 Hz**, where the image actually lives, of **0.30 (Pulse), 0.26 (Bohemia), 0.32 (victory)**, against 0.17–0.18 for the menu loop and the defeat, which are pads and a single line and have less to spread; and **mono compatibility of −0.07 to −0.19 dB**, i.e. summing to mono costs a fifth of a decibel and cancels nothing. Pulse's peak reads **0.836–0.856** where this file recorded 0.83 for the mono bake — the one figure the change moved, and still nowhere near the ceiling.

- **It is ARRANGED, in ten eight-bar sections** since #186: prelude, intro, verse, chorus, **breeze**, verse, chorus, build, chorus, outro. A sixteen-bar loop with no sections is a ringtone; what makes a track worth hearing for two minutes is that it keeps arriving somewhere. Measured across three seeds (full-band RMS, per section, seed to seed within 0.002): prelude 0.073, intro 0.111, verse 0.215, chorus 0.228, **breeze 0.112**, verse 0.215, chorus 0.228, build 0.196, final chorus 0.231, **outro 0.050** — a range of **13.1 dB** where the nine-section version spanned 10.4 (its row read intro 0.094, verse 0.205, chorus 0.220, breakdown 0.067, build 0.190, outro 0.071, and the figures before *that* — intro 0.130, breakdown 0.041 — predate the breakdown fix the bullet below describes). The pass's *overall* RMS has barely moved through any of it: what changes is the distribution between sections, which is what every one of those fixes moved.
- **The hook is ONE rhythmic motif transposed across the chords**, not four different phrases. Repetition with variation is what a hook *is*; a melody that never repeats is one nobody can hum. The chords are one a bar, four bars round; the verse is short notes with gaps in them and the chorus is **simpler, higher and longer-noted**, because a chorus is usually *less* busy than its verse and that contrast — not volume, and not more notes — is what makes it a chorus. The last bar of every section is a descending fill that hands the phrase on.
- **A breakdown is EMPTIER, not quieter, and the measurement said so.** At its first setting the section fell to a fifth of the verse's level and a *ninth* of its low band, which is a gap rather than a breakdown; almost all of that was simply losing the kick and the bass, which carry most of a mix's energy. The parts that remain are pushed up to compensate. #186 finished the thought: the section is the **breeze** now, and it keeps the sustained floor while losing the kit, so what is emptied is the drums and not the bottom of the piece — measured, its low band went from 22 % of the section's energy to 78 %, i.e. from *no* low end to the same share the verses carry.
- **The parts are arranged around the kick, not laid over it.** Four on the floor; clap on two and four; eighth hats with the off-beats opened; **bass on the off-beat eighths**, because a bass on the beat doubles the kick and both go to mud, while a bass between them is what reads as a pump. The sixteenth arpeggio never stops inside a section, so the track keeps moving through the melody's rests — it is the *sections* it rests in (the prelude and the breeze), not the bars. Under all of it since #186 there is a **sustained floor** that ducks to the beat rather than doubling it, which is the same argument one octave down: see that part below.
- **PolyBLEP, and it matters more here than anywhere else in the project.** A naive saw or square has energy at every harmonic, and at 44.1 kHz everything past Nyquist folds back as *inharmonic* tones — on a lead playing an A5 that is a chorus of wrong notes moving the opposite way to the melody, which reads as cheapness rather than as a bug. Two lines of polynomial correction at each discontinuity remove nearly all of it and, unlike summing band-limited harmonics, cost the same at any pitch.
- **The lead is two saws detuned about twelve cents apart.** That beating is the whole "supersaw" the genre is built on, and two oscillators are enough — the width comes from the detune, not from the count. Much less and they sound like one flat oscillator; much more and it is an out-of-tune chord.
- **A whole number of samples per step, and the track's length taken from that** rather than from the nominal tempo. Rounding each step and then trusting the nominal length leaves a fraction of a step of silence at the end of the pass.
- **Measured** (baked, dumped to WAV, analysed, dump removed): peak **0.826–0.830** with **zero clipped samples** and a DC offset of 0.001 — it read 0.83 for the mono bake, 0.836–0.856 after the stereo one (#119), and #186's floor took it back down. Earlier, on the fixed-tempo version, the low-band envelope per sixteenth read `9542 7442 7442 7442` — a nine on every beat and a four between them, i.e. exactly the four-on-the-floor-against-off-beat-bass the arrangement calls for. Synthesis takes a few seconds per pass, which is why it happens on a background thread rather than during load.
- The track stops **dead** when a level is cleared, so the fireworks' reports land in silence — a bang competing with a four-to-the-floor kick is a bang nobody hears, and the sudden quiet is itself part of winning. It stops on a loss too: a dance track carrying on over a result screen that says the player ran out of balls is the wrong feeling entirely. It is authored as Level One's theme and is still exactly that — level one plays it, and so does every fourth entry after it (see "The rotation").

**Pulse was reworked in #186**, which reported it as primitive, aggressive and aimed at small children, and asked for dignity, a "summer breeze" quality, real bass and a rare climactic shock. Three of those are arrangement and one is a voice, and the measurement disagreed with the report on exactly one point — which is the useful part.

- **The dynamics were already real; the rhythmic density was what was uniform.** The report read the `Level` column (0.62 to 1.00, about 4 dB) as the piece's dynamic range, and it is not — measured, the nine sections spanned 0.067 to 0.222, i.e. **10.4 dB**, because what a section *has in it* sets its level and the dial only trims (the lesson Bohemia's own figures record twice). What was genuinely uniform is what the report heard: the kick played in **eight sections of nine** — every one but the breakdown — and the sixteenth arpeggio in all nine, so the track ran at full rhythmic density for almost its whole length whatever its levels did. The fix is therefore air and not a dial — and the levels moved anyway, to **13.1 dB**, because taking parts away is what moves them.
- **The floor is a new voice, and deliberately not a change to the old one.** `Bass` is a **pluck** — `Exp(-t * 6.5f)`, gone in about 150 ms — so the piece had a pump and no bottom. Re-enveloping it, which is the obvious fix, would have re-voiced **three other pieces**: Bohemia, the lobby loop and the victory fanfare all play that same voice. `SubBass` is a separate sustained note, one a chord, an octave under the root, held **past the bar line** so one chord's floor is still releasing while the next one's speaks (ended *on* the line it leaves a hole every bar, measured at a quarter of the level in the sections with no drums to cover it — the bass audibly pulsing once a bar).
- **The pump stays an octave up, and that is the whole design.** The report's sketch proposed writing the recurring off-beat hit at the true root instead of `+12`; that would have doubled the new floor and turned both to mud. A floor *under* a pump is how the genre is actually arranged — the pump is a mid-bass rhythm part and the sub is the bottom, and they are two jobs rather than one part in the wrong octave.
- **It ducks to the beat, and the duck is the fix rather than an effect.** A sustained sine in the kick's own octave meets it at whatever phase it happens to and either doubles or cancels — the classic way a mix loses its low end with every part individually correct. Ducking 55 % on each beat and recovering over ~110 ms is both the cure and the pump the ear expects. Where no kick plays it holds dead flat, which is what gives the prelude, the breeze and the outro a bottom.
- **A sine is the correct sub and is silent on the hardware this is played on.** The fundamental here runs 37–82 Hz, which a laptop or a phone reproduces not at all; a second harmonic at 0.24 and a third at 0.08 are audible on those speakers and the ear reconstructs the missing fundamental from them. The ladder is audibility, not colour.
- **Two sections carry no kit at all, and they carry a second tune instead.** A dance track with one tune has only volume to make a quiet section out of. `BREEZE` is a four-bar sentence on the **keys** — state, the same cell a step higher, an answer that falls, a held note to rest on, the shape Bohemia's theme proved — sitting an octave under the chorus and moving by step where the chorus leaps. It opens the piece (a **prelude** with no drums in it), it is what the breakdown plays instead of hats, and it walks the **outro** off, so the ear meets it three times and the outro ends rather than fading with a kick still going. The kick now plays in six sections of ten and half of a seventh.
- **The shock is one event in one place: a stop, and one chord from outside the piece.** The build's last bar cuts dead three beats early — measured, **six sixteenths of literal digital silence** — and what lands in the hole, off the beat, is **E major**: the harmonic-minor dominant, whose G♯ is the one note two minutes of strictly diatonic A minor has not played. It resolves onto the Am the final chorus opens on, guaranteed because *every* progression in the pool starts there, so the surprise is a cadence and not a wrong note. It is scored for **brass**, the one voice this piece never otherwise uses: a part heard once is an event by construction, where the same chord on the lead would just be the piece getting louder. This is what the report meant by rare — the per-bar `Embellish` and `Ghost` rolls fire in nearly every bar and are therefore texture.

**The second theme: Bohemia (#120).** A level set with one piece in it is one piece heard all evening: one `ARRANGEMENT`, one hook, one chord pool. **The third theme: Nocturne (#162).** Smooth jazz — a trio playing sevenths on a swung grid, nine sections, 3:02 at the set's slowest tempo (96). Where Pulse and Bohemia differ in **mode over one shared dance floor**, this one differs in **harmony and in time**, which is what makes it a third language rather than a third accent.

- **The genre's identity is chords and placement, both of which are tables** — which is why a whole third piece cost almost no new synthesis. Every chord is a **four-note seventh** where the other two use triads-plus-octave (the seventh is not a decoration here, it is the chord), the five progressions are all built on **ii–V motion**, and it is in plain C major on purpose: the colour comes from the extensions, and a piece whose whole point is its harmony should not also be fighting a key. Two of the five end on the dominant instead of the tonic, so a four-bar round can circle rather than land.
- **The swing is worth more than any of the chord tables.** The same notes on a straight grid read as a ballad. Only the **off-beat eighths** move — steps 2, 6, 10, 14 — and the sixteenths between them are left alone, because swinging those makes a shuffle, and the walking bass on the beat must not move at all or the time itself wobbles. `SWING` is 0.62 rather than a mechanical 2:1 triplet, which reads as a shuffle.
- **The walking bass is written as an algorithm, not as a figure**, because that motion *is* the backbone: beat one is always the root so the harmony is never in doubt, beats two and three take chord tones in the voicing's own spacing, and beat four is the **chromatic approach a semitone under the next chord's root** — the one note that makes a walk sound inevitable rather than merely busy. It was also **silent from #162 until #218**: `UprightBass` guarded its envelope with `break`, and an envelope with an attack ramp is zero *before* its peak, so the loop ended on the first sample of every note — the piece played three months as a trio missing its stated backbone, and nothing caught it because every figure this file records (RMS, peak, balance, correlation) looks healthy with a voice missing behind the limiter. The low-band figure below is the one that cannot hide that fault, which is why it is recorded now; the guard is a `continue`, as every other voice's always was.
- **One new voice, and the reason is the attack rather than the pitch.** `Bass` is a saw held open through a filter that sweeps *with* the note — a dance bass, and what it does is sustain. `UprightBass` is plucked: a **triangle** (nearly all fundamental with odd harmonics falling off as 1/n², the string's own spectrum) under a filter that **closes** as the note decays, over a few milliseconds of finger noise. A bass with no finger noise reads as a sine rather than as a player. **The fifth theme: Ember (#163).** A rock ballad — power chords through a distorted guitar, half-time under the verses and full time in the choruses, ten sections, 2:25, in E minor notated at 134 and felt at 67. The other four differ from one another in mode, in harmony, in time, and in grid and register; this one differs in **gain**, and three of the decisions below follow from that one fact rather than from taste.

- **The chords have no third in them, and that is acoustics rather than style.** Distortion is a non-linearity, so notes played into one do not merely add — they multiply, and every pair's sum and difference tones land back in the signal. Root-fifth-octave is 2:3:4 and all of those products fall on notes already in the chord; a major third is 5:4 and its products do not, which is why a driven triad turns to porridge and a driven fifth turns to a wall. **Rock's harmony follows from its amplifier**, and this piece is written that way down to the code: `Guitar` renders the whole chord itself instead of the arrangement calling it three times, because summing three separately-driven notes is a mix of three guitars and a completely different sound. What the guitar therefore never states is the third — the pad, the keys and the tune are what make the harmony minor, and they are clean.
- **The build is the gain and not the fader.** Every section carries a `Drive`: 1.1 for the picked verses, 4.5 for the pre-chorus, 9 in the choruses, 11 under the solo. The waveshaper is normalised at its output (`tanh(drive·x) / tanh(drive)`), so pushing it changes the **spectrum** and not the level — which is the same lesson every other measurement in this file arrives at, reached from the other end: a chorus here is louder because a harder-driven guitar has more harmonics in it and because more parts are playing, and the dial that would have said so is not the one that was turned. Measured, the ten sections span **0.038 to 0.241 full-band RMS**, a range of **16 dB** — the widest in the set, against Pulse's 13.1 — while the pass's overall RMS sits at the set's own 0.1797.
- **Distortion is compression, so a driven note sustains.** The voice's decay falls with the drive (`2.6 / (1 + 0.5·drive)`): a picked note is gone in a couple of seconds and a saturated one holds through the bar. It is one line, and it is most of why the choruses feel bigger rather than merely louder.
- **The time halves and doubles, and the low band says so.** The verses put the snare on the third beat alone, so a bar notated at 133 is felt at 67; the choruses put it on two and four. Measured, the low band per sixteenth through one bar of each — a verse `1852 1103 0840 0800 | 0832 0821 0855 0830 | 1148 0938 1661 1154 | 0845 0812 0802 0721`, i.e. **two** low-end events a bar, against a chorus `2258 1431 1144 1109 | 1175 1077 1907 1384 | 2388 1389 1172 1090 | 1057 0986 2267 1309`, which is **four**. Neither row ever falls below about a third of its own peak, so the floor is under both — the thing #186 had to fix on Pulse is designed in here. Notating at 128–140 and feeling it at half is also what keeps sixteenths available for the fills and the solo: a ballad written at 67 would have a sixteenth two thirds of a second long, which is too coarse a grid to play a fill on.
- **Two takes hard either side, and seating them symmetrically is NOT enough.** The image is a double-tracked rhythm guitar — #119's rule (width from genuinely different signals) arriving as the idiom's own studio practice, and it makes this the widest piece in the set. What it also does is lean, and the reason took three measurements to find: the piece read **0.2 dB left** while the wall on its own measured **0.3 dB right**, which is arithmetic until the cross-term is written down. The bass and the floor play the guitar's own root, so each take is correlated with what is in the middle, and the two correlations differ because the takes are detuned to either side of the pitch and beat against the centre at different rates. **What leans a mix is that cross-term, not either take's level**, and no amount of care over the seats can reach it. Swapping which take sits on which side twice a bar cancels it — inaudibly, since both sides carry the same chord either way. It has to be swapped **by energy and not by count**: alternating the picked guitar note by note left its two loud thumb notes on the same take and measured 0.34 dB of lean in the verses, which folding the half-bar into the alternation removes.
- **Measured, three seeds, through the private baker** *(before #229; it renders 145.1 s at 134 BPM now)*: **146.4 / 143.4 / 142.3 s** (2:22–2:26) at **132.8 / 135.6 / 136.6 BPM**, RMS **0.1796–0.1797** against Pulse's 0.1794 on the same harness (and 0.1795–0.1796 against Mural's 0.1744–0.1750 on #264's re-run), so no level step at a boundary; peak **0.824–0.849** with **zero clipped samples**, DC **0.0009–0.0010**, channel balance **−0.04 to +0.10 dB** with no section outside 0.17, and **57–61 %** of the energy below 200 Hz (the cabinet's own high-pass at 110 Hz is what keeps a wall of guitars off the bass rather than on top of it). The last two seconds sit at **0.0003**, so the join to the next pass lands in silence like the other four. It is the **widest and the least mono-safe** piece here — side/mid above 200 Hz **0.433–0.446** against Pulse's 0.277–0.284 and Mural's 0.153–0.157, correlation 0.77, and summing to mono costs **0.5 dB** where the others cost 0.02–0.11. That is the double-tracking, it is the point of the piece's sound, and half a decibel is the price.
- **A piece costs 1.0–4.7 s to render, once, and holds ~26 MB** — measured for #229 through `Tools/MusicBake`, one Release run on the development desktop: Pulse 1.0 s, Mural 1.1, Nocturne 2.2, Ember 2.3, Bohemia 4.7, the menu loop 0.5. All six run at once at the splash, so what a session waits for is the longest of them and nothing after that: rendering is over before the player leaves the first menu. Earlier machines read Pulse 1.6–1.7 / Bohemia 6.2–12.6 on the same code, so none of the runs are comparable across machines, only within one. The memory halved with #229 as well — a piece is kept as the 16-bit PCM the voice is fed rather than as the float mix it was rendered into.
- **Not claimed: the tune.** Everything above is a measurement, and none of it says whether the hook is worth humming — that is a call on feel, and the piece was arranged against the numbers rather than at a monitor. What is claimed is that the levels, the balance, the loudness and the handover are the set's, so what is left to judge is the music.

**The rotation.** `ProceduralMusic.ThemeFor(named, index)` answers which piece a level plays, and `GameplayScreen`'s install calls it before anything else is put in place — including when the level fails to load, since the fallback map is still level `index` and should sound like it. With nothing named, **the level's position in the set picks the piece**, alternating: level one is Pulse (the piece it was written for), level two is Bohemia, level three Nocturne, level four Mural, level five Ember, and so on, so a set has variety without a single file having to say anything. Adding a fifth piece therefore changes what an unnamed level plays at every position — and changed nothing in the shipped campaign, because since #194 **every level file names its theme** (re-checked at #264: all 45). A level may pin one with a `"music"` field (see "Levels and serialization" in `docs/formats-and-tools.md`); it is matched case- and whitespace-insensitively, and an **unknown spelling falls back to the rotation rather than throwing**, for the reason the scene names do — it is a hand-editable file. A negative index is folded back into range rather than indexing off the end.

**The victory fanfare was tightened in #185**, which reported it as forced rather than punchy — and read against the report that produced it in the first place ("old, cheap, nothing to dance to", of a lone trombone), the two together **bound the target between bare and busy** rather than cancelling out. So the eurodance drop stays and everything round it is cut: **three drop bars instead of four**, the extra build bar reserved for a genuinely big win (intensity 0.75 rather than 0.35 — at 0.35 nearly every non-trivial clear got it), the pad's sustain down from 15.5/22 steps to 10/14 and **struck rather than swelled** (a 0.12 s attack against the shared voice's 0.35, which is a parameter now because all four pieces share that voice and only this one wants the hit), the close's lead hold 14 → 9, the sparkle run 12 steps → 8, and the layer thresholds spread from 0.2/0.35/0.45/0.5 out to 0.3/0.5/0.7/0.8 — at the old spacing seven simultaneous voices arrived by a *below-average* score, so the fully stacked version was the ordinary case rather than the exceptional one.

- **The shortened drop plays I–vi–IV, not the first three of I–V–vi–IV.** Taking bars 0-1-2 would drop the IV — the one chord the vi exists to set up, and the lift the whole progression is written for. Home, the minor turn, the lift.
- **The two fanfares need different room past their last bar, and one constant for both is a constant that fits neither.** Cutting the shared tail to 8 for the victory truncated the *defeat's* closing pad, which is held 26 steps from the top of its last bar: measured, its final five milliseconds sat at **37–39 % of the piece's own peak** — cut off mid-note, a click, the exact fault the constant exists to prevent. `VICTORY_TAIL_STEPS` is 8 and `DEFEAT_TAIL_STEPS` 14; both pieces now end at **0.0 %**.
- **Measured across the intensity range** (seed 1234, so the tempo is held): an ordinary win **10.6 → 8.1 s** and a big one **12.3 → 9.9 s**, i.e. about a quarter off each, with zero clipped samples throughout and RMS climbing 0.165 → 0.202 with the score, which is the layering still doing its job. The defeat was left untouched at 17.5 s — long, and worth its own look, which it got as #190 below.

**The defeat fanfare was shortened in #190**, which is #185's finding followed up rather than a new complaint: the defeat ran **four bars whatever had happened**, and at 62–74 BPM that measured 17.5 s over a screen that had just told the player they lost — nearly twice the victory's own length once #185 had shortened that. Only the bars could pay for it. The **tempo is the piece's whole character** ("it has to feel like it is running out", and half the theme's tempo is what does that), and the **tail cannot be touched** — the closing pad is held 26 steps from the top of the last bar, and #185 measured what a shorter one costs: the final five milliseconds at 37–39 % of the peak, i.e. a click. So the length became the intensity's, like the density already was: **three bars for a loss, four only above `DEFEAT_FULLER`** — the very threshold that already put the harmony under the melody, so "the run deserved better" grows the piece in one step rather than by two figures that could drift apart.

- **The third bar is the one that goes, chord and melody note together** — a three-bar **i–VI–i**. The iv is a step on the way home, where the VI before it is the sag the whole piece is written round and the i after it is the landing, so the short version still sags and still lands, and the melody still falls (3-2-0 or 2-1-0 against the four-bar 3-2-1-0). Dropping the VI instead would leave a plain plagal cadence with none of the piece's colour. It is #185's own rule for the victory's I–V–vi–IV applied to a falling progression: keep the chord the progression exists for. The four-bar arrays stay the canonical statement and a bar index is mapped onto them, rather than a second set being authored per length.
- **Measured, through the same private baker** (no playback path; it reproduced #185's figures exactly before anything was changed, which is what says the two measurements are comparable): the ordinary loss **17.52 → 13.92 s** at seed 1234, and the near miss deliberately **unchanged at 17.52**. Across eight spread seeds the three-bar piece runs **13.2–15.0 s** and the four-bar **16.7–18.9 s**, the spread being the rolled tempo. **RMS does not move at all** (0.0933 → 0.0933 at intensity 0.1, 0.1321 → 0.1321 at 1.0) because `Limit` normalizes each bake to a target; zero clipped samples throughout, and the last five milliseconds still sit at **0.0 % of peak**, so the tail arithmetic is independent of the bar count as it should be. The only figure that moved is the **peak**, 0.397 → 0.415 at the low end: the same RMS driven into a shorter buffer puts the crests a touch higher, and at a 0.9 ceiling that is headroom nobody hears.
- **Not claimed: that this is the right length.** The issue said as much when it was filed — it is a call on feel, and the number was simply large enough to be worth an ear. What is claimed is that the tempo, the tail, the mode, the fall, the density thresholds and the loudness are all untouched, so what is left to judge is the length alone. If it wants to be shorter still, the next honest step is a two-bar version for a poor run — but at the current gate the last melody note is withheld below 0.35, so two bars would leave a single trombone note in ten seconds, which is the "dismissive" failure the issue warns about rather than a shorter piece.
- **A caution left standing from when the shapes were rolled** *(#229 froze them: the victory is always C4 at 135, the defeat always G2 at 68)*: three hand-picked small seeds (1234, 7, 99) all rolled the **same key** and a tempo inside 0.8 BPM of each other — `Random(int)`'s first draws are correlated for nearby small seeds. Across eight spread seeds all four roots (E2/F2/G2/A2) and the whole 62–70 BPM band appeared. The lesson outlives the roll: held seeds are right for comparing two builds and wrong for asking what a range is, which is the trap in any harness that draws numbers from a seed.

**The fanfares.** Into that silence goes a piece announcing the result — `PlayVictory`/`PlayDefeat`, in the same file because they are played by the same instruments. Both are rendered like the pieces: baked on a background thread (this fires on the exact frame a level ends, which is also the frame the camera is released, the fireworks start and the result screen is built — the last moment to spend on synthesis), from an authored shape rather than a rolled one since #229 — the victory C major at 135, the defeat a fifth under it two octaves down at 68, inside the resting heart's own band — and on their **own** `SoundEffectInstance` so `Stop()` silencing the theme does not cut off the piece announcing the outcome.

- **The victory is a eurodance DROP now, not a herald's call** — the first version was a lone trombone rising over a pad, and it was reported as exactly that: dignified, old, cheap, nothing to dance to. The game's musical language is the theme's eurodance, and in that language a celebration is the drop: a snare-roll **build** with a climbing arpeggio ladder in front (once the win is worth announcing), then four bars of **I–V–vi–IV** — the most euphoric progression pop owns, its one minor bar making the IV lift after it — with a four-on-the-floor kick and the off-beat bass pump running under a **supersaw hook in the 3-3-2 tresillo** (hits on 0, 3, 6 / 8, 11, 14 — the most danceable eight counts there are), closing on the held tonic with the sparkle run climbing away.
- **They scale with the score, and the scaling changes how much of the floor is moving, never the tune.** `intensity` is the score against `FANFARE_FULL_SCORE` (6000 — there is no natural ceiling to a score, so the reference is a stated constant, and it is the one dial to turn if the fanfares stop matching how the player feels). **The kick, the bass and the hook are always there** — the beat is the celebration now, not a garnish a modest win goes without; past 0.2 the claps arrive, past 0.35 the hats and the build bar, past 0.45 the sixteenth arpeggio, past 0.5 the hook's octave doubling, past 0.6 the sparkle run, past 0.75 the higher held finish, past 0.8 a tom run into it. Measured across 0.1 → 1.0: RMS 0.160 → 0.203, energy below 200 Hz at **59–61 % throughout** (the old build's low band only *arrived* with a good score — now the floor is under every win), length 10.6 → 12.4 s. The player hears what kind of win it was before the result screen has said a word.
- **Defeat is the inverse in every dimension that matters** — mode, direction, tempo and register: minor rather than major, falling rather than rising, 68 BPM against the victory's 135, and low rather than high. It has **no percussion at all**, because a beat would give it momentum and momentum is the one thing it must not have. It takes the same intensity from the other end: a good score that still lost gets a harmony under the melody and a resolution at the bottom, while a poor one **does not play its last note at all** and simply stops, unresolved — the bleakest thing in the piece, and it costs one condition. Measured 0.1 → 1.0: RMS 0.093 → 0.132, and note onsets *falling* 22 → 12, because the fuller pad holds the sound continuously instead of leaving separate attacks.
- **The closing chord needs room to ring out past the last bar** (`FANFARE_TAIL_STEPS`). Both pieces hold their final chord for well over a bar, and without the tail the buffer simply ends mid-sustain and the fanfare finishes on a **click** — a poor way to be told anything. Verified: the last 150 ms of all six measured variants peak at exactly 0.0000, so they decay to silence rather than being cut.
- **The DEFEAT is played by a TROMBONE, not by the track's synth lead** (`Brass`), and four things make it one. The load-bearing one is a **filter envelope that opens with the note**: brass gets brighter as it gets louder, so the cutoff sweeps from a muffled 550 Hz to wide open over the attack and settles back as the note holds. A fixed filter over the same oscillators is a synth pad; that one line is most of the difference. The rest is a **slow speech** (~55 ms of attack with a touch of breath noise across it — a trombone does not start instantly), a **sub an octave down** which is the whole of the depth, and **two filter poles rather than one**, since 6 dB an octave leaves the top end hissing through and that is exactly what stops a saw sounding like an instrument. The victory used the trombone too, once — that is the version that read as a parade ground — and plays the theme's own supersaw `Lead` now: the instrument the player has been dancing to is the instrument that congratulates them.
- **Register belongs to the instrument.** Up at C4–G4 the *trombone* fanfare was a whistle with a chord under it, and dropping it an octave was the fix — a lesson that stays true for the defeat (E2–A2, 73–86 % of its energy under 200 Hz). The supersaw victory goes back **up** (roots A3–D4, the hook an octave over them), because the theme's own chorus already proves that voice carries that register over a full mix — it was the trombone that could not.
- **Legato, and the arithmetic of it is the fix.** A melody whose notes die before the next begins arrives as a row of separate events — it read as choppy. Every line here overlaps: the victory's hook notes run a step past the next hit's start, and the defeat's seventeen against a sixteen-step gap (its holes are bigger, being slower), so the voice releases across the join and each phrase is one gesture.
- **The fireworks duck under the fanfare** (`ProceduralAudio.FireworkDuck`, to `FIREWORK_DUCKED` 0.35). Both arrive on the frame a level ends, and a report is broadband and loud enough to bury a tune under it — the bang is an event, the fanfare is the point. It is a gain on the *next* play rather than on the ones already sounding, and the reason for that changed with #75 even though the behaviour did not: the burst voices now *do* have handles that could be turned down mid-flight, but walking 32 of them every frame to write a volume that is almost always the same one is the per-frame work `BestPractices.md` forbids, for a difference that lasts as long as one report. The host reads `IsFanfarePlaying` per frame rather than latching it, so the ducking lifts by itself when the piece ends.

## The victory fireworks (Game)

Clearing a level sets off a display: shells rise from a ring around the island, whistle, burst over the arena in colour and rain down, with the report arriving as each one goes off. `Game/Effects/Fireworks.cs` owns it and `Testbed/Content/Shaders/Fireworks.fx` draws it; the sounds are three more procedural bakes (see "The sound").

- **It lives on the HOST, not on the gameplay screen, and that is what makes it work at all.** A cleared level pushes the result page *over* the session, and the session runs only its world under that page (#241) — so a celebration owned by the session would freeze at the exact moment the player is meant to be watching it, and would go with the session the moment Main Menu tears it down besides. Owned by the frame it keeps running whatever is on the stack, exactly as the clouds and the wall clock do, which is why `BS3DGame.Update` advances it above `_screens.Update` and `FinishSceneDraw` draws it. It is started from `CheckLevelCleared` (not from the result screen), so the first shells are already climbing while the last of the cluster is still falling — the celebration overlaps the moment it is celebrating instead of following it — and `BuildLevel` stops it, or the next level opens with the last one's fireworks still bursting over it.
- **One static vertex buffer and ONE draw call for the whole display**, the idiom `Snow.fx` and `Spray.fx` established: every shell and every spark is animated in the vertex shader from a handful of per-shell uniforms (`ShellOrigin`/`ShellBurst`/`ShellColor`/`ShellShape`, `MAX_SHELLS` = 32), so nothing is rebuilt or re-uploaded per frame. The C# side owns only what the shader cannot know — when a shell goes up, where it bursts, what colour it is and when to make a noise. A dead slot is `ShellColor.a == 0`, which collapses its quads to a degenerate triangle: an idle display costs the vertex work of the buffer and **not one pixel**.
- **The same buffer is the rocket on the way up and the burst on the way down.** While the age is negative the sparks are strung along the flight path by their trail rank, which draws the rising comet and its tail for free; at zero they are released. A spark's whole path is a **pure function of its age**, which is precisely what lets the buffer be static — and why the burst directions are baked in at build time rather than hashed in the shader, since a direction that changed between frames would make every spark jitter.
- **It waits for the fanfare, and it outlasts the result screen.** The display hangs back `CELEBRATION_DELAY` (2.2 s) before its first shell, so the victory fanfare gets its opening statement to itself — the level ends, brass announces it, and only then does the sky start going off; everything at once is nothing heard. And it runs for a **minute**, because it is meant to still be going while the player reads their score and the released camera swings around the arena. At nine seconds it ran out while the result screen was still being read, and a celebration that stops before its audience does reads as the game losing interest. `TearDown` stops it, or a win followed by "Main Menu" would go on bursting over the front end.
- **The brief is a sky FULL of them.** 32 concurrent shells, a launch every 0.17 s once the opening barrage settles (0.07 s during it), bursting between 44 and 122 up and spread right across the sky rather than clustered over the arena — which puts about twenty shells in the air at any moment. Past `RELAXED_AFTER` (16 s) it **eases off** to a shell every half-second: a full minute at the barrage's density is exhausting rather than celebratory, and the point of the later stretch is to keep the sky busy without shouting. Verified alive and varied at 4, 12, 25 and 45 seconds in. How long the opening lasts is a **per-display** value since #184 rather than the constant it reads by default — a finished block of levels takes nearly three times as much of it, which is what makes that display read as bigger; see "Finishing a block of levels is its own moment".
- **The party popper fires when the display STARTS, not when the celebration was asked for** — it is the fireworks' own opening crack, announcing them rather than the win, which the fanfare has already done. It is flagged rather than hung off the delay counting down to zero, because at a delay of nothing that branch never runs at all and the `celebrate` test argument asks for exactly that case. The ceiling on `MAX_SHELLS × SPARKS_PER_SHELL` is the **16-bit index buffer**: times four vertices it must stay under 65 536, so raising either without checking that is how far triangles quietly start referencing the wrong vertices (the lesson `CreateGridMesh`'s grids taught, from the other direction). At 32 × 200 it is 25 600.
- **The ceiling's glass must not WRITE depth, or most of the display is thrown away (#299).** The whole scene pass runs under `DepthStencilState.Default`, stated once in `BeginSceneDraw`, and the ceiling plate simply inherited it: it blended correctly on the frame and then wrote its depth like a wall. The display goes in afterwards under `DepthStencilState.DepthRead` and additive blending, so every shell bursting beyond the glass was depth-tested away — and from the play camera, low behind the gun with the plate filling the top of the frame, that is most of the sky the shells use. The bursts appeared only once the released post-win camera had swung out to a vantage with no glass in the way, which is exactly how the fault was reported. `BS3DGame.DrawCeilingGlass` is now the one place the state is stated, for the session's plate and the front end's preview plate alike; reading depth is kept, so the glass still goes behind the island, the cluster and the gun. **The drain's glass and the gun's window pane still write depth** — the same latent shape, left alone because neither is ever between the lens and a burst.
  - Measured by contact sheet rather than by eye on one frame, because the shells are random and four frames prove nothing: twelve captures of the plate's silhouette from a fixed play camera in each build (`play level=One celebrate`, a shot every two seconds). Before, the silhouette is clean in all twelve while bursts are plainly going off around it — and several frames show the glow **cut along the plate's own diagonal edge**, which is the occlusion made visible rather than inferred. After, rays, shell trails and whole bursts cross it in five of the twelve.
  - The `celebrate` and `confetti` levers had to be repaired before any of that could be photographed: both fired at the end of `LoadContent`, where `BuildLevel` — which stops both displays on purpose — silently swallowed them a frame later, so `play` plus `celebrate` gave a level and an empty sky. They are consumed from `Update` after the startup level now (`StartStartupCelebrations`), which is the only way to see either display from the play camera at all.
- **Measured cost: nothing worth a number.** City, in a level, 3840×1600 fullscreen, `quality=high`: 7.66 ms with no display, 7.35 with the first (14-shell) version and **7.63 with the shipping 32-shell one** — all three inside the run-to-run spread, and the worst second stays above 100 FPS. Space measures 5.77 ms. One additive draw call is simply not what this frame is made of.

Three things that were wrong first, all worth keeping:

- **A burst has to be BIG and DENSE, and the arithmetic is not obvious.** At 120 sparks the display read as three or four lonely glints: a burst subtends about 19° from the play camera, so a hundred-odd sparks spread over that disc land a couple of hundred pixels apart. It ships at **320 sparks** and a **1.55-unit** spark — the size is in *world* units and a burst is 40–90 units up, so it has to be far larger than it sounds. Spark count and spark size both have to scale with the burst radius, or a bigger shell looks *emptier* than a small one, which is exactly backwards.
- **A SPARK IS A LINE, NOT A DOT, and this is the single thing that makes it read as an explosion.** A burning star crossing the sky faster than the eye or a shutter can resolve is seen as a streak, and it is those streaks radiating from a point that say *something is blowing apart*; a cloud of round dots, however many and however bright, only ever reads as a swarm. The billboard is stretched along the spark's own **screen-projected velocity** (`SparkStretch`), which falls out in closed form because the position already is — `d(reach)/dt = (1 − INITIAL_SPREAD)·DRAG·e^(−DRAG·t)`, fastest at the flash and gone within a few tenths of a second. So the shot is a *line*, then a break-up, then a drift, without a stage or a state machine anywhere. Projecting the velocity is what keeps a spark coming straight at the lens round instead of stretched along an arbitrary axis, and the pixel shader tapers the streak's brightness towards its leading end so it reads as a comet rather than as a stick.
  - It also disposes of the flash problem the first version had. Every spark being coincident on the burst frame used to stack additively into one blown-out sample that the glare pass drew as its six-armed star — a burst that read as a flaw in the lens. `INITIAL_SPREAD` was raised to 0.12 of the radius to fix that, and it introduced a worse fault: it *pre-arranged* the shell into a formed sphere that then inflated rigidly, which is a bottle brush on a wire and not an explosion. It is 0.015 now, and the streak spreads the flash's energy instead — on that frame every spark is nearly coincident but moving at its fastest, so each is drawn at its longest.
- **A Fibonacci sphere is TOO regular for this.** Even coverage is right for placing sparks, but expanded uniformly the lattice holds its pattern and the eye picks the spiral out as a rigid structure inflating. A quarter-radian of scatter on each direction keeps the coverage and destroys the lattice; and the speed spread was widened hard (`0.34 + 0.66·rand^0.6`, from a tight bunch near the full radius), because a shell whose sparks all reach the same distance stays a clean hollow surface — real, but not a thing coming apart.
- **Two colours per shell, split per spark** (`ShellColorB`, chosen a random step round the palette so it is never the same one twice). A real shell is one chemistry and one colour; a *display* is not, and a burst that comes out half magenta and half gold reads as far more of an event than either alone. The split is hard rather than a blend, so the two are seen as two.
- **The hot core is mostly WHITE**, for the reason the cluster's ripple whitens: these are driven to about 4 in linear radiance, far over `GLARE_THRESHOLD`, and a saturated hue at that level just clips one channel and reads as a flat coloured blob. A spark is white at its brightest and only shows its own colour as it cools, which is what makes it read as *burning*.

`celebrate` on the command line fires the display on the front end. Clearing a level is the only thing that normally starts it and clearing one cannot be scripted, so without it the fireworks can be neither screenshotted nor measured — the same reason `autoshoot` and `aimshoot` exist.

## Finishing a block of levels is its own moment (Game, #184)

The game marked exactly two things specially — a level cleared, and the *last* level cleared — and gave them the same treatment: `ResultPage` swapped its heading to `CAMPAIGN COMPLETE` over an otherwise identical page, the same fireworks, the same fanfare. There was nothing between "one level" and "the whole campaign", and nothing in the data that could have been: a `LevelSet` was a flat list and `TotalStars` a flat sum. #194 gave the campaign **blocks of five** — five of them then, six since #207 added the desert, seven since #182 appended the Nebula and eight since the Arcade — and #184 gives finishing one its own moment.

**What a block is lives in the set** (`LevelSetEntry.Block`, see "The campaign is nine blocks of five" in `docs/formats-and-tools.md`): a run of consecutive entries sharing a name. Everything below is gated on a set actually naming them, so a set that does not — every set authored before this — plays exactly as it did.

- **Finishing a block is EVERY level of it cleared, deliberately not "the last level of it cleared".** The picker gates on the campaign's star *total*, not on the previous level, so a block can be played out of order: six stars from the first two levels open the fifth. Asking whether the whole run is cleared is right however the player got there, and it also makes the milestone survive someone who leaves one level for later — that return clear is the one that completes the chapter, which is exactly when it should fire. A level counts as cleared when it holds at least one star, the same test the picker's tiles read.
- **It fires on the TRANSITION, not on the state**, so replaying the last level of a chapter already finished is an ordinary clear — which is what it is. `BS3DGame.WouldCompleteBlock` asks "would clearing this *now* finish it": every *other* level of the run cleared, and the run not already complete. That second half is the one that matters — without it the milestone would fire every time the player went back for a better rating.
- **The decision is taken ONCE, in `CheckLevelCleared`, and that is forced.** The fireworks and the fanfare start the instant the field empties; the result page arrives `LEVEL_CLEARED_BEAT` later, and the clear is written into the progress on the way. So a decision taken on the page would reach the effects too late to change either, and asking the question twice would ask two different questions (before and after the record). It is stamped into `_blockCompleted` and the three consumers read it — a page that says a chapter is finished over an ordinary barrage and an ordinary fanfare is three components disagreeing about what just happened.

The moment itself is distinct in **what it says, what it looks like and what it sounds like**, because the `CAMPAIGN COMPLETE` precedent — a different string over an identical page — is what this issue was raised about:

- **The heading becomes the chapter's own name**: `THE TOWER`, where an ordinary clear says `CLEARED`, with `Block 4 of 7 complete` on a line under it (the counts are the live set's). The name is the point — a heading reading "BLOCK COMPLETE" would have repeated the precedent exactly, and what is worth telling the player is *which* chapter they closed and how much campaign is left. Upper-cased at the point of display, so a set may name its blocks in prose. That second line is the only new widget on the page, held back (`Visible = false`) on every other ending.
- **A longer OPENING barrage, not a longer display** (`BLOCK_CELEBRATION_OPENING`, 8 s against the ordinary 2.2). This is the one dial that reads as *bigger* rather than merely longer: the launch interval is already at its minimum during the opening, the player is watching the sky in the first seconds, and by the time it has eased off they are reading their score — so a longer total changes the part nobody is watching. Eight seconds is about 115 shells at `INTERVAL_OPENING` against the 32 the shader carries at once, so the sky stays saturated for the whole of it instead of filling and emptying. `Celebrate` takes the **longer** of two openings for the same reason it takes the longer duration: a second call must not be able to cut a display short.
- **The fanfare plays at full intensity whatever the level scored** (`PlayVictory(score, grand: true)`). The milestone is the chapter and not the last level of it, so a chapter closed on a scraped two-star clear must not sound like a poor win. Nothing new is synthesised for it — the score scaling already tops out at 1, and this skips the weighing.
- **Never both milestones at once.** The last block completing *is* the campaign completing, and the screen has one heading, so `LevelResult` suppresses the block when `CampaignComplete` is set. The bigger of the two wins.

`blockdone` on the command line makes the `result` page a block milestone, and it is one step further along `celebrate`'s own reasoning: a single clear merely cannot be scripted, where finishing a block cannot be *reached* without playing five levels. Verified with it, against an ordinary clear from the same vantage: `THE TOWER` / `Block 3 of 5 complete` against `CLEARED` — the counts as the set stood at #184; they read off the live set and say `of 7` today — both legible once the frame goes out of focus (the milestone's second line is thin against a bright sky in the first seconds, which is the same trade every line on this page makes — see the section below). The `[level] Cleared …` log line now ends with `[block 4/7 'The Tower' COMPLETE]`, which is the only way a real play-through says whether it fired — and it names the block either way, so a clear that did *not* complete one still reports which chapter is still open.

**What is not machine-verified**: the five-levels-then-fire path end to end, because clearing five levels cannot be scripted. What is verified is everything under it — the set's block queries and the loader's refusal of a non-contiguous block were checked directly against `LevelSet` (including the case that caught a real defect: `null == null` made every entry of an *unblocked* set one run, so `BlockRange` returned the whole campaign and `BlockCount` said 1 where its own comment promised one per level; nothing live read it, which is exactly why it would have sat there) — and the presentation was looked at in the running game.

## The campaign ends in confetti (Game, #215)

Clearing the **last uncleared level of the set** starts a second celebration on top of the fireworks: paper confetti tumbling down around the camera for nearly two minutes. **It read "the last *entry* of the set" until #347, and that was a real difference rather than a wording one**: unlocks were gated on a star *total*, so a player banking four stars a level could open and clear entry 105 with earlier levels never played, and the banner and the paper fell on a campaign full of holes. The decision is `BS3DGame.WouldCompleteCampaign` now — every other entry cleared, and the campaign not already finished, which is `WouldCompleteBlock`'s question one scale up and makes a replay of the finale an ordinary clear. `Game/Effects/Confetti.cs` owns it and `Testbed/Content/Shaders/Confetti.fx` draws it, and it follows the fireworks' structure exactly — on the host, one static vertex buffer, one draw call, everything animated in the vertex shader out of a single clock.

**Why it is a new effect and not a bigger barrage** is the whole argument for it. #184 had already found the dial that makes a celebration read as *bigger* rather than merely longer — the opening barrage — and by the time a block completes that dial is at eight seconds of an already-maximum density, with `MAX_SHELLS` saturated for the whole of it. There is nothing above it. So the ending that deserves the most was the one with no room left to grow, and the answer had to be a different **kind** of thing rather than more of the same. The two run together: the sky keeps bursting and the paper comes down through it.

- **It outlasts the fireworks on purpose** — `CONFETTI_SECONDS` is 105 against the display's 60. The display *thins itself* after `RELAXED_AFTER` (16 s) because a minute of barrage is exhausting, so at the exact moment the player is reading the last score of the whole campaign the sky has gone quiet. Paper is the opposite kind of subject: it costs nothing to keep falling and it never shouts, so it is the one that can still be going when the fireworks have finished. It has no delay of its own either — the fanfare's opening statement is already protected by the display's 2.2 s, and paper starting to fall is not a sound.
- **The quad is NOT a billboard, and that is the only reason it reads as paper.** `Snow.fx` turns its flake in its own plane and keeps it facing the lens, which is right for a speck with no orientation to read. A chip of paper has one: it flips end over end, so it is broadside, then foreshortened, then edge-on as a thin bright line, then broadside again. That flashing between a wide chip and a line is what says *paper* rather than *dot*, and it falls out of building the quad on a real world-space basis rotating about the piece's own tumble axis — one cross product and one `sincos` over a billboard. The tumble axis is **biased horizontal** for the same reason: a chip spinning about a vertical axis never presents an edge to the lens and so never flashes at all.
- **The light is the effect's own, not the scene's.** A two-sided `abs()` lambert on the piece's own normal, over an ambient floor — bright broadside, dark edge-on, twice a turn. Two-sided because confetti is printed on both faces and seen from both, where a one-sided term would blink half the field to black for half of every turn. It carries its own direction rather than the scene rig's because this runs over any of the thirteen scenes and eighteen domes, and a celebration that went black in the cavern would be a hole in the party.
- **Alpha-blended, not additive, and that is the difference from a firework.** A shell is light and adds to what is behind it; a chip of paper is an object that hides it. Being tonemapped with the rest of the frame is what keeps it looking *lit* rather than luminous — the palette sits mostly under `GLARE_THRESHOLD` so only the brightest broadside flashes of the white and gold glint, which is what a foil chip catching the light actually does.
- **It moved OUT of the HDR pass in #242, and that reverses the sentence this bullet used to carry.** It was drawn after the fireworks and inside the same pass, so a piece crossing a shell covered it — and that pass is exactly what the result page's defocus reads, so the campaign's last celebration dissolved into bokeh along with the arena. The owner's words: it should stay sharp like the trophy. So it draws into the pipeline's **sharp foreground layer** (#225's, built for the cup) and is composited over the resolved frame, which is what the trophy already did for the same reason.
  - **It keeps the tonemap**, which was the whole argument for the HDR pass: the composite runs the layer through the same exposure, curve and grain, so the paper still reads as lit and its broadside flashes still feed the bloom through the layer's own bright pass. Only the softening stopped reaching it.
  - **Three things it gives up, all accepted rather than missed.** It no longer covers a firework it crosses — the shells stay in the scene, so they are behind the whole layer. It loses **scene occlusion**: out there the depth buffer is not the scene's, so the island and the cluster no longer hide a chip falling behind them (cheap at the moment it plays, the camera having been released onto its orbit and the frame going soft anyway). And it is drawn **before** the cup, so the cup still covers it — #225's ruling that the presented object reads as the nearest thing, which this issue did not ask to change.
  - **It had to become premultiplied to live there**, in both `Confetti.fx` and `Confetti.cs`. That layer is cleared transparent and composited by coverage, and `BlendState.NonPremultiplied` squares the alpha (`a·a + dst·(1−a)`), so every partly covered chip would have under-reported itself. Straight alpha was correct only while this drew over an opaque scene.
  - **Verified on the path #225's own regression hid in.** That change was tested at `High` only and a black result screen got through; this was captured across the defocus ramp at **both** `High` and `Medium` (the ssaa 1 + MSAA 8× path), plus a third run with the confetti falling and **no result page at all**, which must be — and is — the frame it always was.
- **The box follows the camera and wraps with `frac`**, `Snow.fx`'s trade taken for the same reason: a little translational parallax given up for never popping as the camera crosses a boundary. It is tighter than the snow's (58×48×58 against 70×55×70) because the density that matters is the density near the lens, and the near fade is far tighter too (1.15 against 7) — a big piece rushing past the lens is most of what sells this, and fading them as eagerly as snow does throws the best of it away.
- **The clock is never reset**, and that is deliberate: every piece's position is `frac()` of it, so a reset would teleport the whole field at once. A second celebration therefore picks the fall up wherever it happens to be, which is invisible — the pieces are already at every phase of their cycle — where a reset would be a very visible flicker. The fade in (0.7 s) and out (3.5 s) do the starting and stopping instead.
- **The decision is taken once, in `CheckLevelCleared`**, beside the block milestone's and for the identical reason #184 records: the confetti starts there, so a decision taken on the result page would reach it a beat too late. `ShowResultScreen` now **reads** that flag rather than deriving campaign completion a second time, which is what stops the page and the celebration answering two subtly different questions. It stays gated on `cleared` at the page, because a *loss* on the last level reaches that line having never taken the decision at all.
- **It shipped at 4 000 pieces and that was too few** — the fireworks' own lesson arriving a second time from the same direction. Photographed on the front end, 4 000 read as a pleasant confetti *fall* rather than as a celebration: the pieces were individually legible, evenly spaced, and the eye counted them. It is **9 000** now (36 000 vertices, still well inside the 16-bit index ceiling of 16 384 pieces), and what changed is that the frame became a mess of colour with no gaps to rest in. Density is what a celebration is read as big by, before size, brightness or duration.
- **Measured cost: nothing.** Meadow, level 1, `quality=high`, 1600×900 at ssaa 2×, vsync off, from the fixed play camera and in paired repeats: **1.722 / 1.737 ms** with no confetti against **1.734 / 1.738 ms** with all nine thousand pieces falling. The difference is 0.007 ms and the spread between the two *identical* no-confetti runs is 0.015 ms — twice that — so the effect is inside the run-to-run noise and there is no honest number to quote beyond "not measurable". One draw call of pure vertex work with almost no overdraw is simply not what this frame is made of.

Verified in the running game with `confetti`, and the shot worth keeping is the whole ending together — `result celebrate confetti` over the **mountain**, which is the one scene that already has falling white snow in it. Confetti and snowflakes in the same frame read as unmistakably different things, which is the billboard decision above being confirmed against the exact case that could have exposed it. The paper covers the additive shells where it crosses them, and once the page's defocus ramps the whole field becomes a soft coloured bokeh behind the score — the numbers stay perfectly legible, and the celebration is still visibly going on behind them.

`confetti` on the command line starts the fall on the front end. It is one step further along `celebrate`'s reasoning than `blockdone` is: a block milestone needs five levels played to reach honestly, where this needs the whole campaign.

## The cup the player just won (Game, #183)

The result screen presents a **trophy**, close to the lens and turning: a plain Bronze or Silver, or — the top two tiers, **with handles** — a Gold cup (since #232) and, above it, a **Diamond cup**, which since #228 is **crystal** rather than a fourth metal. `Game/Effects/TrophyPodium.cs` owns the presentation and `BS3DLibs/Prazsky.Core/Render/TrophyMesh.cs` builds the geometry, procedurally like everything else here.

It is the third beat of an ending that already had two, and it says something neither of the others does. The fanfare states *that* the player won and the fireworks answer it; what the celebration could not say was **how well**, in anything but a row of stars and a figure. A cup is the same information as an object, and an object can be handed to somebody.

- **It is placed against the FRAME, not against the world**, and that is forced rather than stylistic. The result page releases the camera onto a slow orbit of the island, so anything left standing in the arena swings out of shot within seconds and spends the rest of the ending behind the score panel. Anchored to the lens it holds its place while the whole arena turns behind it, which is what reads as the cup being *presented*. Where it sits is stated in **normalised device coordinates** and turned into a world offset through the camera's own projection — at distance `d` the frame's half-height is `d / M22` and its half-width `d / M11` — so it holds its place in the composition at any field of view and any aspect ratio, rather than drifting off the side of an ultrawide. Left of centre and low, and since #226 DELIBERATELY TALL ENOUGH TO REACH INTO THE PAGE'S OWN UI: the heading, the stars, the breakdown and the three buttons are all centred, and the cup runs its left edge up against them — a reward that kept its distance from the page it sits on read as exhibited in a case beside it rather than handed over (#226).
- **It holds focus while the frame behind it goes soft (#225, reversing this bullet's own first answer).** The cup used to soften with the arena, because it was drawn inside the HDR pass and the defocus takes everything the HDR pass holds. But #178's argument — the ending is watched first and softened afterwards — arrives at the one object it most obviously applies to: the cup is the thing being watched, and the blur's whole point is that what is being read stays legible over an arena that has stopped competing. So the cup now draws into the pipeline's **sharp foreground layer** (`PostProcessPipeline.ForegroundTarget`), and `CompositeForeground` tonemaps it onto the resolved frame after the blur: the arena, the fireworks and the confetti melt into bokeh *behind* a cup that stays crisp. Nothing about its light moved with it — the layer is linear radiance, the composite runs the same exposure, ACES curve and film grain as the resolve, and the layer's own bright pass is added into the bloom pyramid's head, so the metal keeps the glare its finishes were tuned against; the defocus is also built from a scene the cup is not in, so no blurred copy of it stands behind the sharp one. The layer is filled in `BeginSceneDraw`, before the scene target is first bound: MonoGame clears a `DiscardContents` target on binding it, so the first version's mid-frame swap of targets wiped the drawn scene and showed a black result page on the MSAA path — the placement is a fix, not a preference (see the defocus section in `docs/rendering.md`). What the move did flip is one layering call: the scene's weather and the celebrations were drawn in front of the cup when it stood in the HDR pass, and they are behind it now — nothing 3D is drawn after the composite. **And since #242 the cup is over the UI as well**, on the owner's word that it is fine for it to partially cover a panel: the composite moved from "first thing after the resolve" to the last picture the frame draws, past the Myra desktop. The sentence this bullet used to end on — that the HUD, the page and its panels belong over the cup — was a deliberate call and it is the one that was reversed.
- **A metal's diffuse is DARK, and the first version got that wrong in a way worth keeping.** Authored at the diffuse a painted surface would take (0.66 for the gold) with the sky reflected at full strength on top, every cup came out of the tonemap as flat pale plastic: a bright, even, shadowless shape with no highlight anywhere on it, because the diffuse alone was near the top of the curve and the reflection pushed it over. It also **hid the Diamond's handles completely** — the shape information was there and the saturation destroyed it, which is worth remembering the next time geometry appears to be missing. Metals have almost no diffuse; what colours them is their reflectance. So the diffuse is roughly a third of what it was and the specular carries the hue — and since #226 the reflectance is dialed further up (`SpecularAmbientStrength` 0.30–0.45, the specular powers near tripled): the owner's word was *shinier*, and the tier ladder still climbs from a cast bronze to a polished-to-a-point diamond, only every rung of it is a polish no prop gets.
- **The tiers differ by finish, and the top two by SHAPE, and the very top by MATERIAL too.** All four cups run the metal path (`Metalness = 1` — Diamond's own near-dielectric value is a separate later dial, see below), and the specular power climbs with the tier — a bronze cup is a cast thing with the broadest highlight, a gold one is polished. Bronze and Silver differ from each other only in *hue*, and hue is the first thing a dark scene takes away, so Gold and Diamond take the **handled** mesh: told apart by silhouette before any colour has been read. Diamond carries a small emissive term as well, so it is faintly a light source rather than only a reflector — small, because the first pass had it at three times the shipped value and the cup came out of the glare pass as a white blob with no shape left in it.
- **Gold's own handles are #232, and reverse a #183 call rather than fix a break.** The owner's playtest report was that Gold reads as a plain bowl where a trophy is expected to have handles; the class doc had always argued the opposite — one shape distinction, saved for the top. Diamond keeps sole claim on being crystal, but the *shape* argument now marks a pair of tiers rather than one, which is what the report asked for.
- **The cup's own reflection was never anything (#232), and that undercut every material bullet above it.** `InstancedModelRenderer.SkyColor`/`GroundColor` default to a flat `Vector3.One` — no image, no gradient — and nothing had ever set them here: the cup was never enrolled in the scene's `SkyLightRig` (the "one controlled finish" argument under "The front end" in `docs/game-shell.md`, made from `TitleWordmark`'s opposite choice), which is a deliberate choice, but the flat compiled default standing in for "an authored finish" was not. Reflecting a uniform white sky off a uniform white ground *is* reflecting nothing, so the Fresnel term every tier's material comment above already assumes — "the sky reflected... a metal's reflectance is its specular colour" — was, in play, a flat wash with no shape in it at all. Silver took the worst of it: with no hue to fall back on, a flat reflection over a near-white specular and a merely-lightened diffuse read as one undifferentiated pale surface — **"flat white and matte"**, the owner's report, verbatim. Silver's diffuse dropped separately, from a pale (0.40, 0.415, 0.45) — closer to its own near-white specular than a dark metal's reflectance ever gets — to a genuine near-black (0.075, 0.08, 0.09), so the body sits far enough under the highlight and a real reflection for both to read as light *arriving* rather than the cup's own paint.
- **The first fix gave it a small fixed "room" instead; the owner asked for the level's own dome, and got it (#232's second half).** A hand-authored `SHOWCASE_SKY`/`SHOWCASE_GROUND` shipped first — bright and warm from above, dark and cool from below, the same every time — and it worked, but the owner watched it and asked directly why the cup could not reflect the actual scene the way the cannon does: the cup stands over the exact level it was won on, unlike the wordmark's rolling menu preview, so matching THAT dome is the more fitting "one controlled finish" than a fixed one invented for the purpose. `TrophyPodium.Renderers` now enrolls all four tiers in `BS3DGame.SkyLitRenderers` the same way the cannon's own renderers are, and the showcase constants are gone.
  - **It very nearly reintroduced the exact bug this whole issue opened on.** The Moon's sky rig is near-black *by design* — the "sky" is airless space, so there is nothing to reflect (`SceneRenderer.TryGetLightRig`'s own note) — and the cavern's is "dim and cool" on purpose too. Screenshotted straight after enrolling: Silver on both came back as a near-silhouette, only a thin Fresnel rim still lit, because #232's own diffuse cut had just made the body genuinely dark and the reflection was now the *only* thing left to light it. A reward that goes unreadable on two of fifteen scenes is worse than one that never matched any of them.
  - **`SkyLightRig.ApplyToPresented` is the fix, and it LIFTS rather than SCALES.** Pushed onto the trophy's renderers instead of the plain `ApplyTo` every other enrolled renderer takes (the ceiling glass takes its own dedicated push for the same kind of reason, `ApplyToGlass`), it floors `SkyColor`/`GroundColor` to `PRESENTED_MIN_SKY_LUMINANCE`/`PRESENTED_MIN_GROUND_LUMINANCE` (0.35 / 0.05, linear) — but by *adding* a flat grey lift equal to the shortfall, never by multiplying up to it. Scaling a near-black `(0.001, 0.001, 0.002)` up to a 0.35 floor is a ~350× multiplier that amplifies any stray colour impurity into a tint and is undefined outright at exact zero; adding just enough neutral grey to clear the floor is well-defined at every input and costs nothing at all once a dome already clears it on its own — which every scene but the very darkest does, so the cup still matches the dome everywhere the floor does not have to speak.
  - **Verified on both directions of the fix.** Silver on the Moon and the cavern now reads as a legible, cool, dimly-lit metal rather than a black cutout, and Gold on the Moon came back warmer and clearly gold rather than muddy brown. And on a scene the floor never touches — Silver photographed under a dusk-leaning meadow roll came back genuinely violet-tinted, matching that dome's own mood exactly, which is the feature working as asked rather than a regression to chase.
- **And the top tier is CRYSTAL (#228)** — a little blue and genuinely transparent, so the defocused arena, its fireworks and its confetti go on moving *through* the bowl while the cup holds the frame. It costs nothing beyond its own draw, and that is #225's layer paying off a second time: the sharp foreground is composited over the resolved frame through **premultiplied** alpha, so a cup that writes an alpha below one simply lets the frame underneath through — which on this page is the bokeh the defocus was building anyway. A translucent tier could not have existed before that layer: inside the HDR pass the cup would have been transparent to a scene that was about to be blurred *with* it. Four things make it read as cut glass rather than as a faded decal, and none of them works alone:
  - **The alpha is per surface, and the depth WRITE is off.** Just under a third each since #231 (just over a third when #228 set it), so a look through the bowl crosses its near wall and its far inside and lands near half, while the one-wall-thick stem stays at a third and the far handle shows through the body. Glass that gets denser where there is more of it is most of what says glass, and it falls out of the geometry rather than being authored. With the depth write on, only the nearest surface of each pixel survives and the cup is one flat film; nothing else is ever drawn into that layer, so there is nothing for it to sort against but itself.
  - **It is a dielectric, not a metal.** `Metalness` 0.06 lerps normal-incidence reflectance from the shader's 4 % dielectric F0 to about 0.10 — between window glass and lead crystal, leaning towards the diamond it is named for (0.17, n = 2.42) without going there. So the face is nearly clear and only the edges mirror, which is Fresnel and is the entire shape language of glass; a metal reflects the same at every angle and would be a mirror with a hole in it.
  - **Its reflection is not attenuated by its own transparency**, which needed a new shader dial: `SpecularAlphaWeight` in `InstancedModel.fx` (1 everywhere else, and the two specular lines are then unchanged to the bit). Alpha says how much of what is *behind* a surface comes through, and a reflection is light coming off the *front* of it — exactly the argument the file already made for `EmissiveTint` on a glowing pane. Attenuated, a 38 %-transparent cup keeps 38 % of its sparkle and reads as a coloured film.
  - **The blue had to go somewhere other than the diffuse.** The diffuse is premultiplied by the alpha and *then* sRGB-decoded, and both crush it: 0.34 at the shipped alpha reaches the light as about 0.11, which decodes to 0.015 of linear radiance — a body colour that is, to the eye, black. The first crystal was authored at a sensible-looking blue and came out a **white ghost** for exactly that reason. So the diffuse is authored high (it is a colour at 38 % of itself, not a colour) and a small `EmissiveTint` carries the rest: that term is added in linear radiance, is neither premultiplied nor decoded, and is the only one on this material that survives at full strength. The reflected environment is also turned right down from the metals' (0.30 against 0.30–0.42 — but at a dielectric's F0, not a metal's): at a metal's strength even the level's own real dome would be a milky veil over everything the crystal is supposed to be showing through, rather than the glint the low strength keeps it as. Diamond is enrolled in the same real-dome push as the other three tiers (#232's second half) and takes the same brightness floor, so a level cleared under the Moon or the cavern still shows a glint rather than fading to a dark hole in the frame.
- **And it is CUT since #231, because crystal that is only transparent is a ghost.** The owner played #228's cup and reported it still read as an apparition rather than as glass — translucent and washed out, not a solid you can see through. Two things were missing, and neither of them was a number on the material, which is why #228 could not have reached them by tuning:
  - **The facets are gone — twice refused, and the second refusal is the one that matters (#271).** #231 gave the crystal tier a faceted body on the argument that cut crystal is *defined* by flats and nothing material makes a smooth cup read as cut: 24 segments, authored rings, a flat normal per quad. The rim came out a 24-gon, and the owner's first ruling was that a cup has a cup's shape — "crystal sharp" is an *optical* sharpness, not a visible polygon edge. The rework kept the cuts in the **normals** alone (the smooth 64-segment geometry, each ring normal's azimuth snapped to 24 directions — flat shading bands on a round silhouette), and the second ruling closed the question: **visible edges are edges whether they are geometric or shading.** Every tier now draws the one smooth revolve, and the crystal is entirely its material — the transparency, the Fresnel rim, the reflected environment that #231's second half tuned. What survives of the original idea is the lesson in `OmniWheelMesh`, where deliberately visible cuts on a roller are the point: on the trophy they never were.
  - **The balance between body and edge.** A ghost is bright and even; glass is mostly the background with bright edges. The body came down — the diffuse to about 40 % of #228's, the emissive tint to a third — and the reflected environment went **up**, from 0.30 to **0.85**, which reverses #228's own reasoning outright. That figure was turned down from the metals' on the argument that a metal's strength would veil what the crystal is meant to show through; true of a metal's F0 and false of a dielectric's. At `Metalness` 0.06 the material reflects 0.088 head-on and rises to 1 only at grazing angles, so the strength does not paint the face, it paints the **edges** — the silhouette, the lip, and now every facet edge — which is the shape language of glass and precisely what the report said was absent.
  - **What it does not have is refraction**, which the issue also asked for. The cup is drawn into the pipeline's foreground target, cleared transparent and composited by coverage (see "One layer is exempt from it" in `docs/rendering.md`), so it never sees the frame behind it: a real refraction would mean handing the composite a screen-space offset, which is an architectural change rather than a material one. The facets and the Fresnel edges were tried first for that reason, and they answered the report.
  - **The vertex budget went the friendly way, and every tier shares it now.** The smooth handled cup is 30 000 vertices against the builder's 16-bit ceiling of 32 767 — the constraint `PROFILE_SUBDIVISIONS` is written against. #231's faceted body banked headroom near 10 000; with the facets gone (#271) the crystal draws the same mesh as gold and sits at the ceiling with it, which is also the ceiling's answer to why the crystal could never have gone finer-chorded.
- **The handle is a quadratic Bézier, not the circular arc it was first built as.** A handle has to meet the bowl at *both* ends whatever the bowl's profile does between them, and struck as a circle it did not — the ends landed where the arc's own geometry put them, the upper one buried and the lower one hanging in the air off the side of the cup. A Bézier is stated by its ends, so both can be written inside the bowl and the control point is then free to say how far the handle bows out without moving either. It was also too small: clearing the bowl by a tenth of a unit read as a rib stuck on the side, where the shipped bow reaches 0.60 against a bowl of 0.35 and there is real daylight under it.
- **"Buried by construction" was a claim, and it was false (#228).** The owner reported the handles' lower ends standing outside the cup with their cut edge showing. Measured against the **densified** profile — the surface that is actually drawn, not the authored rings — the low end ring stood **0.020 outside** the body at its lowest point and **0.008 inside the bowl's hollow** at its highest. Nor was it a matter of moving the anchor: **there is no anchor anywhere on this profile that buries a full-thickness tube.** The bowl's wall is about 0.05 thick where a handle can reach it and its solid base is barely 0.04 deep under the floor, against a tube 0.084 across. So the tube **tapers** towards each root (0.042 to 0.026 over the last 12 % of the sweep) and the low anchor moved down into the bowl's solid base, `(0.225, 0.620)` → `(0.105, 0.548)`, with the bow carried out from 0.86 to 0.92 so the handle's silhouette came out a shade *wider* than the one that shipped rather than smaller. Both end rings are now at least **0.012 inside** the body and nothing on the tube comes within 0.012 of the hollow. The taper is spent before the tube leaves the cup — the centreline crosses the surface about 7 % along, where the radius is already back to 0.036 — so what shows outside is a handle of very nearly full thickness narrowing slightly as it enters, which is what a cast root looks like. The roots are **capped** now as well: they were left open while burial was an assumption and a cap there was triangles nobody could see, and the crystal made that a different question — everything inside the body is on show, and an open end reads through the glass as a pipe with the far side of the room visible down its bore.
- **The silhouette was refused by a photograph twice.** With one point at the knop and a single flat disc for a foot, the cup read as an eggcup on a washer — two straight runs meeting at a point give a hard bowtie, and a base with no step to it has no weight. The knop is four points now and the plinth two courses. No material change fixed either: a silhouette is read before a surface is. The second refusal was #226's: once the cup stood tall enough to reach into the page's UI, the authored profile's straight runs between creases read as CHORDS — a low-poly silhouette at presentation size, which no number of facets around the axis fixes, because the side-on silhouette *is* the profile. The smooth runs are now subdivided through a centripetal Catmull-Rom (`TrophyMesh.DensifyProfile`, three samples a span, the authored rings untouched and every crease left crisp), and the axis went to 64 facets and the handle's tube to 20 sides.
- **Where it sits, and why not where the owner asked (#233).** Left of centre, because the whole page is one centred column; **raised from −0.30 to −0.15** in NDC on the owner's word that it was unnecessarily low, and they asked for *vertically centred*. It is **not** centred, and that is measured rather than a judgement: at 0 the bowl is **cut by the top edge at the dolly's near end** — photographed against a dark sky so a clipped rim could not hide in a bright one. The cup is simply too tall to be centred there; `SIZE` 2.0 against the frame's half height at `DISTANCE − DOLLY_DEPTH` leaves nothing over and `LEAN_ANGLE` swings the rim higher still. **So #226's −0.30 was not arbitrary — it was protecting the bowl by cutting the plinth instead, and a cut bowl is worse, the bowl being what makes a cup a cup.** −0.15 is the highest that survives the whole 7.85 s dolly period, verified over six captures across it. Full centring would cost either a smaller cup or a shallower dolly, which is the owner's to spend. Vertically this is aspect-independent by construction — the anchor scales with the frame's half *height*, so an ultrawide only adds width.
- **The dance is a turn, a bob, a lean that PRECESSES, and — since #226 — a DOLLY.** The lean is applied about the cup's own Z and the turn about its Y afterwards, so the tilt travels around the axis instead of rocking like a metronome. A cup that merely spins reads as a menu prop. The dolly is a real approach and recede — the distance moves, the scale does not fudge it — and because the NDC anchor is derived from the live distance, the cup holds its place in the composition while its perspective changes, exactly what a dolly-in does: at the near end it is most of the frame's height with the handles swinging wide of the panel's edge, at the far end it is nearly half of it. #226 turned everything up on the owner's brief that it is a game and the reward may be extreme: the lean that was 5 degrees is 17, the turn half again as fast, the bob doubled, and the landing overshoots by over a third rather than a fifth — an object presented to a player is thrown up, not faded in.
- **Measured cost: ~0.24 ms for the whole presentation at #226's size** (meadow, the result page, `quality=high`, 1600×900 at ssaa 2×, vsync off, `stars=4` against `stars=0`): **1.60 ms** without against **1.84 ms** with. That is the cup filling half to two-thirds of the screen — fill rate, mostly, on top of the sharp-foreground machinery #225 measured at ~0.07 ms for a third-of-frame cup — and exactly 10 000 triangles against #183's ~2 800 (the densified profile and the 64-facet axis; 30 000 vertices against the builder's 32 767 ceiling, because it charges six a quad). **That figure is the OPAQUE tiers'**, which since #232 is Gold as well as Bronze and Silver: Gold draws the identical handled mesh this measurement already covers, opaque exactly as it was here, so its cost needs no new number rather than an unmeasured one. **#228's crystal has not been re-measured**, and that part still stands as it did: the top tier now blends instead of writing depth, so its bowl is shaded twice over where one surface used to reject the other, and the honest expectation is that four stars cost somewhat more than three. It could not be measured on this machine — uncapped runs (`nocap`, which is what makes a frame-rate reading a frame *cost*) take the whole system down and force a restart, and a vsync-capped run reads the cap and nothing else. What *is* measured is that it still holds the cap: 75 FPS steady on both `quality=high` (ssaa 2×) and `quality=medium` (ssaa 1× + MSAA 8×, the path #225's regression hid on). Still spent only on a page where nothing is being played.

`stars=<0..4>` rates the `result` page, and it exists because the cup is a function of the rating: three is the only tier a scripted run could otherwise reach, so the other three could be neither photographed nor compared. It is `blockdone`'s reasoning again — a state a real play-through reaches only by being good at the game.

`lost` makes that page a **FAILED** one, and it exists because until #238 the fail state **could not be looked at at all**: `result` hardcoded `cleared: true`, so `stars=0` produced a starless CLEARED page and nobody had ever seen the line the player is told they lost by. It implies `result` the way `level=` implies `play` — the first thing it did on its own was put the main menu up and say nothing. The failure text it uses is the real one `GameplayScreen.Rules` produces for the cluster reaching the line, not a placeholder: a stand-in of a different length is a different layout.

**And the first capture through it found the line was not merely small.** It was `FontBody` at `MENU_TEXT_DIM`, and the palette's own rule for that grey is *asides, always on a dark plate* — while this page, on a fail, has **neither a plate nor a scrim**. Over the lit arena and the ball cluster it came out the least legible thing on the screen, under even the score line beside it. It is the heading size at full white now, which the `FAILED` heading over the same backdrop already proves carries.

## The end of a level goes out of focus (Game, #178)

A level's ending used to be read through smoked glass. The result page dimmed the whole frame at `PAUSE_SCRIM`'s weight (alpha 176) on the pause screen's argument — a page over a stopped game — and that argument does not survive being looked at: a pause is a game *put down*, where this is the game's own ending playing out. The fireworks are climbing, the camera is letting go of the gun and swinging out around the island, the last of the cluster is still running down the drain, and all of it was behind a scrim at the one moment it was worth watching. So **`ResultPage.DimsFrame` is false**, and what holds the page's numbers legible over a lit, moving arena instead is the frame **going out of focus** a few seconds in.

- **The ending is watched first and softened afterwards, and the delay is what makes it an effect rather than a transition.** `BLUR_DELAY_SECONDS` (3.4) sits past both things this page does on arrival — the camera's release (`ORBIT_EASE_SECONDS`, 2.5) and the last star landing (`RevealTotalSeconds`, about 2) — so nothing is blurred while it is still arriving. Then `BLUR_EASE_SECONDS` (16) of smoothstep, whose rate is zero at *both* ends: a linear ramp starts with a visible lurch out of a still image, and the eye reads the moment a movement stops as sharply as the moment it starts.
  - **It was 4 s, and the shape of the ramp rather than its length is why that read as a switch (#200).** The blurred copy has fully taken over by `PostProcessPipeline`'s `DEFOCUS_MIX_IN` (0.3), so the part the eye reads as the transition — one image arriving in place of another — all happens inside the **first third** of the ease, and after it only the radius goes on growing. At 4 s that spent the whole visible change in about 1.2 s; at 16 s the takeover alone breathes for ~5 s. So the figure to reason about here is not the ease but 0.3 of it, which is the trap in slowing this by eye: three quarters of the constant are spent on a change that is nearly over.
- **It is driven from the page, like the camera release and the star reveal** — the defocus is this page making room for its own text, so it is timed from the page opening and belongs to the page; the session under it goes on running (#241) but knows nothing about being read over. `MenuPage.FrameBlur` is the whole interface (zero for every other page), the host reads it off the **active** screen at resolve time, and it is a pure function of one clock the page's `Update` advances — so nothing can write it from two places, and a retry that lands back here gets its own few seconds in focus.
- **The blur happens in LINEAR RADIANCE, before the tonemap curve, and that is the whole reason it is worth having.** `PostProcessPipeline` builds a blurred copy of the very HDR target the resolve is about to read and `Tonemap.fx` mixes it in ahead of the glare add (see "Color management" in `docs/rendering.md` for the stage itself). Blurring the *finished* picture instead would average tonemapped pixels: a firework spark sits clipped near white, so spreading it into the dark around it gives a grey smudge. Spread in linear light its energy goes with it and it stays a glowing orb — and the glare, added afterwards from the sharp scene, keeps every bright thing blooming through a frame that has gone soft.
- **A pause blurs too, and it starts at once.** The result screen's delay exists because its ending is worth watching; a pause has nothing arriving — the game stopped mid-move and the menu *is* the subject from the first frame — so `PausePage` ramps from zero over `BLUR_SECONDS` (0.35) and no further. It is still a ramp and not a cut, for the reason the title card fades rather than cutting in. The pause **keeps its scrim** on top of the blur: what is behind it is a stopped game rather than an ending playing out, and #178 explicitly allows the darkening to stay where it is the blur doing the work. Its clock is the wall clock rather than its own elapsed time, and that is not a style choice — see the `FrameBlur` bullet in `docs/game-shell.md`. Resume takes the blur off **at once** rather than easing it out, and the reason is structural: the ramp belongs to the page, and by the time the frame is sharp again the page has left the stack, so there is nobody left to ease it.
- **Precise aim blurs too, and it is the one moment that must NOT take the whole frame (#214).** Holding the aim leans the lens in (see "Precise aim" in `docs/game-session.md`), and with it the periphery goes out of focus — the gun's near body filling the bottom of the frame, the sky, the frame's corners — while the centre stays perfectly registered, because the centre is the whole point: the aimed cell, the landing ghost (whose display-pixel dissolve dither does not survive being averaged) and the crosshair all live there. The shape is the resolve's `defocusFocus` (see the DEFOCUS notes in `docs/rendering.md`); the amount rides `PreciseAim.Blend` directly up to `GameplayScreen.ADS_DEFOCUS` (0.5 — the result page's 1 is a page over a finished level, not a lens being aimed), so the lean and the focus are one gesture. The gates that clear the lean while the session stays active — a cinematic, a lost window — take the blur out with it on the blend's own ease; a screen pushed OVER the session is instead a **cut**, accepted rather than smoothed: a covered session stops updating, so its answer stops being asked and the incoming page's own ramp starts from sharp — the pause re-blurs whole-frame within its 0.35 s while its scrim and panel own the moment anyway, and a level decided mid-hold goes sharp until the result page's own delayed ramp, which is a camera-release moment where a lingering aim blur would be the wrong thing to keep. The session is the third `FrameBlur` answerer and the only one that is not a page — see the bullet in `docs/game-shell.md`. The laser net, the ceiling flash and the alarm ripple keep their legibility two ways: they live near the frame's centre at the angles that matter, and the glare — added after the mix, from the sharp scene — carries every emitter through the softness.
- **Looked at, not assumed** (`result celebrate` on the command line, which exists so this moment can be seen at all — see "Controls" in `docs/game-session.md`): at 8 % of the ramp the arena is barely soft and shows no ghosting, at 43 % it is plainly out of focus with the shells reading as soft bursts, and at 100 % it is a field of colour and glow. It costs nothing measurable — see the DEFOCUS notes in `docs/rendering.md` for the figures and for how the blur is built.
- **The page's own chrome is untouched by it.** Everything the player reads is drawn after the resolve, in display space — the Myra desktop, the heading, the star row, the breakdown's plate — so it stays sharp over the softened arena. That is also why the in-play HUD had to be gone already for this to work at all, and it is: `GameplayScreen.Draw` stops drawing it the moment the level is decided (see "The in-play HUD"), so there is nothing pinned to the corners of a frame whose camera has been released.

## The drop cinematic (Game)

The reward for a good shot. When one lands and cuts loose more than the player's **biggest drop of this level so far** — by `DropCinematic.MustBeatBestBy` (1.25×), and never fewer than `MIN_BALLS` (12) whatever the history — the camera lets go of the gun and follows the wreckage down: the fall, the run into the glass drain, and — where the scene has anywhere to fall to — the balls pouring out of the hole under the island. `Game/Effects/DropCinematic.cs` owns it; `GameplayScreen` owns the trigger, the subject, the running best (`_biggestDrop`, cleared per level by `BuildLevel`) and the fact that the gun stops answering.

- **The bar is a record and not a count, because a count cannot survive a new level.** The threshold was a flat 6, and on `One.json` that measured as three cinematics in ninety seconds — genuinely rare, because most shots there drop fewer. The pattern pack is built out of large primed groups (Pinwheel drops 102 on a good shot, Crown 72, Bullseye 150 — the first and third re-measured after #234's second pass), so the same 6 fired on essentially every shot that landed and the reward for a good shot became the tax on every shot. **Raising the number does not fix it**: any figure that keeps Pinwheel rare is one Mosaic can never reach, since Mosaic's best possible shot is 24 balls — and a level that never shows a cinematic is as wrong as one that always does. Nor does authoring a figure per level, which is a dial to get wrong in every future level for an answer the game can work out for itself. "The biggest thing you have done here yet" is what big means, whatever the level is made of.
- **The margin is what makes it rare rather than merely monotonic.** Without it a level whose drops creep upwards fires on every one of them; at 1.25× a record has to be *beaten*, not edged. The first qualifying drop of a level always fires, since there is no record yet — one early cinematic is how the effect introduces itself. Expected frequency across the pack: one on Pinwheel (its four vanes are 99–102, so nothing beats the first by a quarter), two or three on Bullseye (whose standing groups run 31, 49, 52, 62, 105, 150 — a ladder wide enough to be climbed in steps of a quarter more than once), three or four on Mosaic as its 8s climb to its 24s.
- **The record is raised by every release, including ones the gates refuse** (a landing during another cinematic, or as the level ends). A collapse the player watched has moved what counts as big here whether or not the camera went with it — and the `[cinematic]` line prints the bar it beat, because "why that one and not the last one" is now a question about the level's history rather than about a constant.
- **A blast counts towards it, and towards the release sound, on the same terms as anything else (#326).** `BallsReleased.Total` gained a third category — the balls a bomb destroyed by geometry — and both the cinematic's bar and `Audio.PlayRelease`'s scale read the total rather than the two named counts. It is the right reading twice over: a blast's victims *fall*, so they are balls coming away from the cluster like any other and the machinery that answers a collapse is exactly what should answer this one; and a bomb is the largest single thing that can happen to a level, so a cinematic that could not see it would refuse the one drop most worth watching. The `[cinematic]` line prints the split, so a bomb-driven record is legible in the log as one.

- **It is a pose, a time scale and a blend, not a mode.** The class holds no camera, no simulation and no input: it is handed the subject's position each frame and answers where the lens goes, how fast the world runs and how much of the frame is its. `UpdateCamera` then Lerps between the ordinary gameplay pose and that one — a **second** Lerp stacked on precise aim's, on the same one-reversible-scalar idiom and for the same reason: at a blend of 0 the three lines return the gameplay pose bit for bit, so a cinematic that ends, or is skipped halfway, cannot leave the camera anywhere the game did not put it.
- **The arc is driven by the balls, not by a stopwatch.** Every framing parameter is a three-key curve over how far the subject has fallen, measured against three anchors — the release height, the island's stone and the drain's throat — so the camera cannot run ahead of a slow collapse or lag a fast one. The legs are sequential by construction (each only starts once the one before it has finished), so averaging them gives one monotonic progress and there are no stages to snap between, only weights that move. `MAX_SECONDS` is the only clock, and it exists because a ball the game never culls can still come to rest — rarely now that the stone ring is a dish that rolls a landed ball into the drain (see "The drain funnel" in `docs/scenes.md`), but a pile propping itself up near the mouth still can, and the game only culls at the kill plane (see `RemoveFallenBalls`) — so "the subject is gone" is not a condition that always arrives.
- **Slow motion is applied to the accumulated time, never to the timestep.** `StepPhysics(elapsed * TimeScale)` — the fixed 1/120 s step is untouched, so a slowed world is exactly as stable and as deterministic as a full-speed one. It exists because a released group falls a handful of units under real gravity and is on the stone in about a second, which is too quick to read. It eases in after the release has been seen at speed and is let go once the balls are through the throat, so control is handed back at 1× and nothing lurches. The ceiling's descent is deliberately **not** scaled: that is a rule of the level playing out, not part of the spectacle.
- **⚠ That same scaling is why the shot juddered until #293, and the cure is render interpolation, not a different step.** Under slow motion the scaled time crosses a 1/120 s step boundary only every few rendered frames, so a ball drawn straight off its body — and a camera fed the raw subject centre — stood still and then jumped double. Measured on `One` at slow-mo 0.35, ~85 FPS: **half of all slow-motion frames moved the subject exactly 0**, the other half ~15 mU each (delta CV 1.15), and the lens inherited the ripple through its own 0.10 s pivot ease. Every drawn ball now interpolates between the pose before the last step and the live one by the accumulator's fraction (`PhysicsBall.InterpolatedPose`, snapshot per step in `StepPhysics`, `_renderAlpha` the factor), and `TryGetDropCentre` feeds the camera the same interpolated read — re-measured, **zero still frames and delta CV 0.53**, the camera monotone. The drawn world runs up to one step (8.3 ms) behind the simulation, which nothing reads — the rules, the aim and the landing preview all ask the simulation itself; a body *placed* rather than solver-moved (the landing's #265 placement) resets its own history so no blend ever straddles a teleport, the arrival glide owning that journey as before. Interpolation runs in normal play too — the same aliasing exists in miniature against any uncapped refresh — and costs nothing measurable (interleaved A/B on `Eleven`, high/nocap: medians identical at 47). The Testbed is untouched: its one variable step per rendered frame leaves no gap to interpolate across.
- **Skip is LMB / Space / gamepad A, and Escape deliberately stays pause.** Taking Escape would give one key two meanings *and* leave the player unable to pause during a cinematic; the skip belongs with the buttons that already mean "yes, go on", which are the ones the hand is on. `SKIP_LOCKOUT` (0.3 s) ignores it at the start — the shot that triggered the cinematic was fired with the very button that skips, so a player firing in bursts would otherwise never see one. That is the splash's `SKIP_AFTER` reasoning, and it is not hypothetical: the first verification harness fired every 1.6 s and silently skipped every cinematic it triggered.
- **Both level endings wait for it.** `CheckLevelLost` and the cleared countdown are both held while it is engaged, because either one puts the result screen over this one and a level that ends mid-collapse takes the collapse the player earned off the screen before they have seen it — the same reason `LEVEL_CLEARED_BEAT` exists at all. A shot that clears the field is the one most worth watching, so the trigger runs *before* `CheckLevelCleared`.
- **The subject is held by body handle, not by list index.** `ReleaseSameTypeCluster` appends the released balls to `_fallingBalls`, so they are its last `Matched + Orphaned` entries at the instant `OnBallLanded` fires — but the kill plane then removes them one at a time and the indices shift. Bepu recycles a handle once its body is gone, which would let a later ball inherit a dead subject's identity; that cannot bite **here and only here**, because nothing is added to the simulation while a cinematic runs — the gun is locked, so there is no shot to land and therefore no further release.
- **Variety is rolled per shot.** Distance, the swing around the drop, a slow arc, the elevation the arc starts and ends at, the tilt and how slow the slow motion goes are all drawn per cinematic; a third of shots stay level, because a tilt that happens every time stops reading as emphasis and starts reading as a broken horizon. The swing is measured from **the player's own bearing** and never exceeds a quarter turn, or the player loses which way the field is facing and has to find it again when control returns.
- **Everything else is bounded by one requirement: the balls stay on screen for as much of the drop as possible.** That is not a preference, it is what the whole shot is for, and it decides the two things below.
  - **`SceneRenderer.OpenBelow` splits the scenes in two, and the question is what is opaque under the island.** The two cities have nothing built under the arena any more (#275 — no camera here ever looked down the funnel far enough to see it) and the sea is water the lens can dive into — in both, the drain's glass is translucent, so from below the balls read right through the cone and then pour out of the hole; under the two cities that hole now opens straight onto the sky past the towers' feet, the same way it looks straight through onto the stars in space, and the same way it does in the storm, whose cloud cells all stand at least 105 units out so the shot looks past the island at cloud rather than through it (see "The drain funnel" in `docs/scenes.md`). The ten solid-terrain scenes are a flat clearing with the island's footprint cut out of it **and a near-black pit shaft hugging the glass**: the ground is a lid, the shaft is opaque, and there is no vantage down there that can see a ball at all. That is why `OpenBelow` is defined as the exact complement of `IsSolidTerrainScene` rather than as a second hand-kept list — the shaft those scenes need is precisely what closes the view from below (#75; see "The setting, in one copy" in `docs/scenes.md`). So those shots climb instead of descending — the elevation keys run to +48…72° rather than −38…−18° — and end looking straight down the throat.
  - **The mouth is the only hole in the stone, so a lens above it can see a ball below it only by looking through that hole.** The lens has to lie in the cone that runs from the ball up through the rim, and clamping its horizontal reach to satisfy that is what swings the shot overhead as the balls run deeper: the further down they are, the narrower the set of places that can still see them. It is the whole of how a solid-terrain scene is filmed and a safety net in the others. Two more guards go with it — while the balls are still *above* the stone the lens is too (the island's top is an opaque annulus, and from under it a shot looks at the underside of the platform with the drop hidden behind it), and the platform is treated as the **solid it is**, so a low swing is pushed out of the drum rather than driving through it. A first pass had only a per-scene floor on the lens Y, which put the camera inside the stone in the terrain scenes and left the balls invisible behind it.
- **The shot ends when the drop stops being one.** Waiting for the kill plane to take the last ball leaves the camera holding on a straggler — one wedged in a slow-draining pile, or (before the stone dished) resting on the flat ring where nothing would ever remove it (see `RemoveFallenBalls`) — the sea's final frames were an empty blue rectangle with one ball in the corner. `Stalled` ends it when the subject descends less than a unit over ~0.9 s, and that is measured in **simulated** seconds, not wall-clock ones: in slow motion a group that has only just been released covers well under a real second's worth of fall, and a wall-clock timer would call that a stall and cut the shot at the moment it began.
- **The sea's underwater murk came over from the Testbed for this.** `Tonemap.fx` already had it (the shaders are shared); the game had it pinned at zero because nothing here ever went below the island. Now the shot follows the balls through the surface and the frame has to say so: `FinishSceneDraw` ramps the amount by how far the lens is under `SceneRenderer.SeaLevelY` and hands it to the shared pipeline's `Resolve` (the two murk colours live on `PostProcessPipeline`, set once). The result is the drain's glass cone full of balls seen from underwater with the waterline across the frame — the best shot in the game, and the reason the sea is in `OpenBelow` at all.
- **⚠ For roughly the top quarter of the elevation roll, that murk used to hide the balls from the CAMERA rather than reveal them (#193).** The fall leg's elevation is positive by construction — the shot opens looking down at the release from above — so a subject that sank under the sea well before that leg finished left the lens floating metres above a surface the submerge fade was already, correctly, closing over the cluster: the shot filmed nothing for its opening stretch and the balls re-materialised mid-air once the schedule alone caught up. Not the fade's fault — with the eye above the water and the subject under it, the fade's answer (invisible) is the honest one; the framing was what disagreed with it. `DropCinematic.SubmergeElevationPull` fixes the framing instead: as the subject crosses `SceneRenderer.SeaLevelY`, the elevation is pulled towards the OUT leg's own diving angle over a `SUBMERGE_PULL_DEPTH` of 2 units, so the lens gives up the dry angle and dives after it rather than waiting for the schedule. **Measured with a throwaway per-frame log** (`pivotY`/lens `Position.Y`/pull, stripped before commit): the pivot crossed sea level at simulated `t=4.88`, `pull` engaged smoothly from 0 at the crossing to 1.00 a full 2 units under it by `t=5.45`, and the **lens itself crossed the surface at `t=4.95`** — 0.07 s behind the subject, down from the reported 3–7 units / whole seconds of lag. No snap: `pull`'s own ramp is what the log shows doing the smoothing, the same reversible-scalar shape as `Blend` itself.
  - **Pulls the angle, not the position.** A straight clamp on the final lens Y would crush it down to the surface while its X/Z stayed wherever the fall leg's wide, distant radius had already put them — a grazing, far-off shot rather than a dive. Pulling the elevation instead keeps the orbit's own radius/azimuth geometry intact and asks the lens to look more steeply down (and, once close enough, back up through the surface it just crossed), which is what `KeepBallsInSight`'s island logic already does for the opaque half of the same problem.
  - **The two cities are `OpenBelow` too but carry no water surface**, so `SubmergeElevationPull` gates on the scene being the sea specifically (`SceneRenderer.LensSubmergedAmount`'s own "the only scene with water a camera can get under") — asking it of a city's elevation would pull towards a diving angle for a canyon with nothing to submerge into.
- **`RecoilCamera` grew a `BaseRoll`** for the tilt — a deliberate roll of the base pose, as against the involuntary one the shake adds. It is added to the shake's roll rather than applied separately: they are the same degree of freedom, and rolling twice about an axis that moved in between is not a roll of anything. At 0 the camera is byte-for-byte what it was.
- **One `[cinematic]` line per shot**, in the manner of the `[level]` and `[score]` lines — a rare event, not a per-frame one, and since the shot is rolled it is the only record of what a badly framed one actually chose. ASCII and `InvariantCulture`: an arrow and a degree sign both came back as `r` on a Czech console and made the figures unreadable.
- **Testing it needs the game driven through its menus, and Myra reads its input once per frame** — so a scripted mouse press and release sent back to back is never seen as a click at all, exactly as a keyboard down+up is not seen as a key press. Both have to be **held across a frame**. Measured on `One.json` back when the bar was a flat 6: three cinematics in 90 s of firing, at 109, 46 and 34 balls. Note those three are a rising sequence only by accident — under the record rule that same run would have fired on the 109 and then on nothing else, which is the intended shape.

## The chapter intro (Game, #267)

The campaign's other camera takeover, and gameplay's answer to a gap the menu already closed for itself. `BackdropScreen`'s fly-in (#254, closer since #261) cranes the camera in among a previewed map's balls specifically so the player sees a level up close before ever pressing Play — but that is the *front end's* promise, over whichever map happens to be rolling, and nothing on the way **into** a level said anything at all: a level just starts, gun already in hand, and whatever changed about the backdrop has to be noticed while already aiming. When the level about to be built is the **first index of a new block** — a new scene, dome, music theme and style all at once (`docs/formats-and-tools.md`, "The campaign is nine blocks of five") — the camera now tours the arena first. `Game/Effects/ChapterIntro.cs` owns it; `GameplayScreen` owns the trigger, the block detection and the fact that the gun does not answer while it plays.

- **A pose and a blend, the drop cinematic's own shape — and, since the owner's ruling on the first cut, an arc of the SCENE rather than of the map.** Same idiom: no camera, no input, the level's own solved figures handed in once at `Begin`, a pose answered every frame, and `UpdateCamera` Lerps it over the ordinary gameplay pose on the identical one-reversible-scalar rule — at a blend of 0 the pose is the gameplay one bit for bit, so an interrupted or skipped intro cannot leave the camera anywhere the game did not put it. What is missing on purpose: no `TimeScale` (the cluster is hanging, not falling, so nothing needs slowing to be read). The first cut's shot was one smoothstepped wide-to-close orbit around the field — one subject, one look-at, the whole flight spent on the map — and the ruling on it was that a new chapter is a new *place*: the tour should fly the environment and only then come to the map and the gameplay view. The shot is now **four Catmull-Rom keys of position and look-at both** — the environment (wide and low, looking across the island's rim into the land behind it), the arena (nearer and higher, the bowl and the drain framed under the field), the map (the one proper look, from a high bearing), and the **arrival: the ordinary gameplay pose verbatim**, so the blend-out hands control back as a nudge between two identical poses rather than a visible glide across the arena. The sweep's bearing, direction and key figures are all rolled per opening.
- **The wide legs are scaled off the level's own solved stand-off, not authored per scene.** `GameCameraFit.Solve` has already picked a distance and an aim height that clears the arena for *this* field by the time `BuildLevel` reaches the trigger (`FitCannonAndGameCameraToLevel` runs inside `InstallLevel`, earlier in the same call) — so the tour's environment key stands at that distance × a rolled 1.9–2.4 and the map key at 1.25–1.45× it, with the environment and arena legs' look-ats placed off the island's own figures (`ArenaIsland.RADIUS`/`TOP_Y`), which is all the "where is the rim" reasoning the shot needs. A tall level's intro therefore stands back further exactly as its ordinary camera already does, with no island-clearance reasoning worked out a second time for it.
- **The trigger reads the SAME block query the milestone celebration is gated on, for the identical reason.** `LevelSet.HasBlocks` false (a set naming no blocks at all) refuses the whole feature outright: `BlockRange`'s "an entry naming no block is its own run of one" fallback would otherwise call *every* level the first index of its own block and open an intro before each one. On a chaptered set, `BlockRange(index, out first, out _); first == index` is the whole test — the same one `Game.WouldCompleteBlock` and the milestone page's `_blockCompleted` build on from the other end.
- **Shown once a run of the program, never persisted.** `_chapterIntroShown` is a plain `HashSet<int>` on `GameplayScreen`, keyed by the block's first index (a name is authored prose and not guaranteed unique across non-adjacent blocks; the first index always is) and never cleared — a retry of a block's own opening level must not replay its intro a second time in the same sitting, and `HashSet<T>.Add`'s own return value is both the test and the record in one call, so there is no window between asking and marking. Deliberately **not** `PlayerProgress`: a fresh launch of the program sees every chapter's opening again, which is the ask, not an oversight — the front end's equivalent has no memory of what a player has already seen either.
- **⚠ The release must not be gentler than the drop cinematic's, and that is measured rather than a style choice.** `Engaged` gates the gun strictly on `_blend > 0f`, and an exponential does not cross the 0.002 snap below until about 6.2 taus in. The first cut set `BLEND_OUT_TAU` to 0.5 on the reasoning that nothing here is racing a falling body, so the hand-back could be as gentle as the grab — which works out to a **3.1 second dead zone** after the tour visually looks over, during which a player who has already pressed the skip button is holding a gun that will not fire. Caught by testing the skip itself: two Space presses sent well after the skip and well apart moved the HUD's ball count not at all. `BLEND_OUT_TAU` is now the drop cinematic's own 0.16 — control is back within about a second of the tour ending, skip or not — and the grab (`BLEND_IN_TAU` 0.35, gentle on purpose, ungated since `Engaged` is unconditionally true for the whole `_running` span) was never the half with a bug in it.
- **Skip is Space / LMB / gamepad A, the drop cinematic's own buttons, through the same gate.** `GameplayScreen.CameraTakeoverEngaged` is `_cinematic.Engaged || _chapterIntro.Engaged`, read everywhere either one used to be checked alone — the aim preview, the ceiling's queued step, the quality probe's exclusion, both level endings — and `SkipCameraTakeover` tries both `TrySkip`s at once rather than needing a third flag to say which is live. That is sound because **at most one is ever engaged**: the intro blocks the very shot the drop cinematic needs to begin, and `BuildLevel`'s `TearDown` resets the drop cinematic before a new level's own intro could start. `SKIP_LOCKOUT` (0.3 s) is the drop cinematic's own reasoning again — the button that just advanced into this level (Next Level, a menu Play) is one of the buttons that skips, and a player who arrived with it still held would otherwise never see the tour begin.
- **One `[intro]` line per chapter opening**, in the manner of `[cinematic]` — nine times over the whole campaign, and the shot is rolled, so it is the only record of what a badly framed one actually chose. Carries the block's own name and its place in the campaign (`'The Gallery' (2/9)`), because "why this one, now" is a question about the campaign's shape.

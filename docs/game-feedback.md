# The game: feedback

Everything the game says back to the player — the HUD, the sound, the music, the fireworks, the light that runs through the cluster, and the camera that follows a good shot down.

Part of the BS3D documentation. CLAUDE.md holds the project overview, the build commands, the ball grid and the repo-wide conventions, and says which of these documents covers what.

## The ripple: the cluster answers a landing (Game)

Every ball that lands sends a wave of light out through the cluster — the balls touching the impact flare first, then the ones touching those, each fading as the next takes over. It is what makes the cluster read as a connected, living body rather than a heap of independent spheres: the shot does not just stick, the thing it stuck to answers. The resting heartbeat was turned down for it (`BALL_PULSE_DEPTH` 0.55 → 0.38): the cluster now has two things to say with its own light, and a breath swinging over half the emission drowns out the event.

- **It travels by connectivity, not by distance, and that is the whole of why it looks right.** `StartRipple` is a breadth-first walk over the balls that *touch* (`BallsMap.GetNeighboringCells`) outwards from the landed cell, so a ball's hop count is how many balls the light has to pass through to reach it — which is exactly its delay. A wave evaluated from a world-space radius in the shader would cross the holes a played cluster is full of as if they were not there; a walk goes **around** them, including around the hole the matched group has just left, which is the most satisfying thing it does. It is started *after* the release, so it walks the cluster that is left rather than the one that was.
- **The origin cell seeds the walk whether or not a ball is still standing in it.** A shot that completes a group is released along with it, so by the time this runs the cell it landed in is usually empty and the wave has to start from the balls around the gap. (Measured: a landing that matched reported `0 balls lit` from its own cell before this was handled.)
- **Only the resulting brightness rides on the GPU** — `ModelInstance.Ripple`, a `Single` in TEXCOORD7 beside `Dissolve`, zero meaning "not flaring" so nothing has to know it exists to opt out. The shader term is behind `[branch] if (RippleStrength > 0)`, a *uniform*, so it cannot diverge and the Testbed and map editor (which never set it) pay nothing.
- **The flare has to be mostly WHITE, and this is the trap that cost the most.** Adding light in the ball's own colour piles it into the one channel already near the top of the ACES curve, so a red ball taking a full-strength flare goes from bright red to *very slightly* brighter red. It was verifiably in the instance data at 0.97 and could not be seen on screen at all — a diagnostic that added to the green channel instead lit the cluster up like a Christmas tree. The hue is normalised to peak 1 and carried 65 % to white (`RippleWhiten`); lifting the channels the ball does **not** have is what "lighting up" looks like, and it settles the dark types for free (the 8-ball would otherwise sit the wave out entirely).
- **Three numbers decide whether it reads as a wave or as a flash**, and it is the ratio between them: the lit band is as many balls deep as the flare's length over the hop delay. The first build had a 0.36 s flare stepping every 0.045 s — nine balls deep against a reach of twelve, i.e. very nearly the whole cluster alight at once, which read as the shot flashing the lot. Keep the band around three or four balls (`RIPPLE_HOP_SECONDS` 0.09, attack 0.05, decay 0.22, `RIPPLE_MAX_HOPS` 14).
- **The amplitude falloff is squared, and that is a glare problem before it is a look problem.** The *count* of balls at a given hop grows as its square in a packed lattice, so a linear falloff leaves hundreds of them near full brightness a few hops out. At `BALL_RIPPLE_STRENGTH` 1.1 that flooded the glare's bright pass, whose six streak arms overlapped into a wash and — added before the ACES curve — blew the **entire frame** white for one frame (measured: a single sample at 174 mean against a 137 baseline). It looked like a rendering fault, not an effect. The published tuning holds the whole-frame mean flat to ±1 while the cluster itself clearly flares.
- **The same wave carries the ceiling's alarm, and the SIGN of the per-instance float says which.** Positive is the landing's own light; negative is the alarm — one channel, two meanings, exactly as `Dissolve` encodes its two directions. It costs nothing (a ball could only be in one wave anyway; the newest to reach it takes it over) and it means the shader picks the colour with a select rather than a second uniform.
- **The alarm REPLACES the ball's colour rather than adding to it**, and that is the whole difference between a warning and a wash. Added, a red flare on a green ball is yellow, on a red one a slightly brighter red, on black a pale grey — every ball came out a different pastel and none of them said "red". Blended (`RippleAlarmCoverage` 0.95, short of 1 so the balls still read as balls), the cluster momentarily *turns* red.
- **The state lives on `PhysicsBall`** (`RippleTime`, counting up from a negative delay, and `RippleAmplitude`), for the reason `Occlusion` does: a ball crosses between the structure and the falling balls in one step, and a group cut loose mid-wave should keep glowing on the way down rather than snapping dark. It is advanced from inside the shared `ClusterCollector` walk, through the one hook that component takes at construction (the Game's `AdvanceRipple`; the Testbed and the editor hand over none and pay a predicted branch on a readonly field per ball) — the one place that visits every ball exactly once a frame, the same contract the occlusion ease and the attach glide rely on, and one that `BallRenderSet.BeginFrame` now enforces rather than assumes. A ball the wave reaches again while still lit simply takes the newer wave: the nearest impact wins, which is what a burst of quick shots should look like.
- **`BallLanding` replaced the event's parameter list.** `BallLanded` had grown from "what fell" to a world position, then a ball type, then the cell; four positional arguments of which two are vectors is unreadable at the call site, and the ripple needed the **lattice** cell, which cannot be asked of a world position (`MapToWorld`/`WorldToMap` were deliberately deleted — see "Physics in the game" in `docs/game-session.md`).

## The ceiling announces itself (Game)

**A step waits for the drop cinematic, and the reason is that it was saying the wrong thing.** The step comes due on the frame a shot is *fired*, but the shot leaves at `SHOOT_SPEED` and lands about a tenth of a second later — so when that shot cut a large group loose, the glass flashing red and driving its alarm wave down the cluster landed **on top of the drop cinematic**. A player who had just made the best shot of the level was shown the game's one punishment animation while watching their reward, and it read as having done something wrong. Nothing was wrong; only the order was.

- `ReleaseCeilingStep` holds a due step for `CEILING_STEP_HOLD` (0.45 s) — long enough for the shot to have landed and a cinematic to have engaged if it is going to — and then for that cinematic to be over. On an ordinary shot the descent still arrives promptly enough to read as the answer to firing.
- **A count, not a flag.** A level with `ceilingStep` of 1 steps on every shot, and two shots inside the hold must not lose one of them. The hold is re-armed per release rather than shared, so queued steps come down one at a time instead of as a single double-height lurch.
- It cannot deadlock: the release is gated on a timer and on `_cinematic.Engaged`, never on a shot resolving, so a ball that never comes to rest cannot leave a level unloseable.
- The `[ceiling]` line carries `waited`, which is the one figure that says whether the deferral did anything. Measured over three played levels: every ordinary step reads `waited 0.45 s` (the minimum), and the two steps that landed on a cinematic read **`waited 1.51 s`**.

The glass stepping down is the pressure the whole `ceilingStep` rule exists to apply, and it was **arriving unnoticed**: a translucent plate sliding a little way down against a sky, while the player's eye is on the cluster, is very nearly invisible. It now says so twice, on the frame it steps.

- **The plate lights up red.** `EmissiveTint` is a `float3` on `InstancedModelRenderer`, added at the end of `ShadePixel` so every technique that shades through it can use it, zero everywhere else. `_ceilingFlash` spikes to 1 on a step and decays **linearly** (the `CameraShake` rule — it genuinely ends), and the tint is scaled by its *square*, so the glass is unmistakable on the frame it steps and has thinned well before the slide finishes. It marks the event; it does not colour the plate for the duration.
- **`EmissiveTint` is deliberately not multiplied by the material's alpha**, unlike the specular ambient beside it: alpha is how much of what is *behind* a surface comes through, and a pane that is glowing is emitting rather than transmitting. Attenuating a warning by the glass's own transparency is what would make it invisible.
- **The number has to be far over 1 for the same reason.** The plate is well under half opaque (`CeilingPlate.GLASS_ALPHA`, and deliberately not restated as a number here — the percentage this sentence used to carry was left behind by a retune and lied for it), so most of what is seen where the glass is is the *sky behind it*; at 1.5 the red merely tinted that blue-white and the plate came out pink. At 6 it reads as a hot red slab. (It is still not a pure red — a mostly transparent plate over a blue-violet sky cannot be — but it is unmistakable, and it blooms.)
- **And a red wave runs down through the cluster**, seeded from *every ball on the topmost occupied level at once* (`StartCeilingRipple`) rather than from a point, because that is what a descent is: a shock delivered by the ceiling to everything hanging on it. The walk then only ever moves outwards, so it travels down the way the push does. See "The ripple" for how the two waves share one channel.

## The floor alarm: the laser grid (Game)

The descending ceiling's warning at the other end of the field. When the cluster's lowest ball is within **two more ceiling steps of the death line**, a grid of pulsing red laser beams appears across the field's footprint, hovering on the exact plane a ball's *surface* would touch at the moment the level is lost (`CEILING_DEATH_Y − BALL_RADIUS`; the loss compares ball centres against the line). It stands down when the danger passes — the player frees the bottom levels — and when the level ends either way it keeps pulsing for a moment after the result screen goes up, then goes out. `Game/Effects/LaserGrid.cs` owns the net, `Testbed/Content/Shaders/LaserGrid.fx` draws it (Game-only: registered in the Game's `.mgcb` alone, the `Fireworks.fx` precedent), and `GameplayScreen.UpdateLaserWarning` owns the trigger.

- **The trigger is geometry, read off the very walk the loss is decided on.** `CheckLevelLost`'s scan over the live body poses tracks the minimum Y rather than stopping at the first offender, and the warning is `lowest ≤ CEILING_DEATH_Y + 3 × CEILING_DESCENT_PER_STEP` (−3.7) — the loss's own comparison, three descents early (two until #110; playtesting asked for it a bit sooner, and at the set's cadences of 4–9 shots per descent the extra step buys roughly that many shots of noticing it before it matters). Geometry rather than shots, deliberately: a level whose ceiling never steps still warns when attaching balls grow the cluster down to the line, and a ceiling-driven countdown would sit silent through exactly that. `LASER_WARN_HYSTERESIS` (0.3) is on the way *out* only — a shot shoves the structure, and a lowest ball bobbing across the exact threshold would otherwise arm and stand down the net with every swing. An empty field's `MaxValue` stands it down for free.
- **A drop cinematic holds the level's endings, never the warning** (`CheckLevelLost`'s `mayLose`). The release that engages a cinematic is exactly the release that rescues a low cluster — six or more balls cut off its bottom — so a warning frozen behind the cinematic gate kept blazing across the player's reward for the whole slow-motion dive down the drain, seconds after the danger it warned about was gone. The walk and the arm/stand-down now run on every frame of a built session; only `LoseLevel` and the budget test wait for the cinematic to let go.
- **Everything time-driven is stamped in wall-clock seconds and evaluated in `Draw`, and that is the load-bearing decision.** A lost level pushes the result screen over the session, which stops its `Update` while `Draw` keeps running (`DrawsUnderlying`) — so a fade advanced in an update would freeze at full brightness at the exact moment the player is being told what it warned about. Instead the envelope is a pure function of the clock (the fireworks' contract with their sparks' age): `SetVisible` and `NoticeLevelEnded` stamp transitions, `Draw` evaluates them, and the linger (`LINGER_SECONDS`, 2.5) expires *in Draw*, the one place still running under the result page. The linger is stamped in `ShowResultScreen` — the one funnel both endings come through — and not where the level logically ends: a clear's page arrives `LEVEL_CLEARED_BEAT` after the field empties, plus a whole cinematic when one is running, and a linger stamped at the clear was spent before anyone saw it. Under a pause the net keeps pulsing, like the ball heartbeat and the city's windows — everything alive runs off the wall clock.
- **The beams billboard about their own axes (ShotTrail's trick), and here it is load-bearing rather than nice.** The play camera stands at Y −7.9, barely below the grid's plane at −6.0 — a flat quad lying in that plane is seen edge-on and vanishes. Billboarded, every beam holds its thickness from any lens, including precise aim's.
- **The red is the ceiling flash's own** (`CEILING_FLASH_COLOR`, passed into the constructor so the two cannot drift apart): the plate says "the glass stepped", the net says "and this is where it ends". The channel has to be far over 1 for the glare's sake — the bright pass keys on *luminance* and red's Rec. 709 weight is 0.2126, so a pure red needs a channel near 2.6 before it clears `GLARE_THRESHOLD` (0.55) at all.
- **The white core bleached the whole net at every peak, and the screenshots caught it.** The hot filament inside each beam lifts the channels red does not have (the cluster ripple's lesson) — but the play camera sees the grid near edge-on, so the beams stack additively across the frame, and at `CORE_WHITE` 2.5 the net turned white at exactly its loudest moment. A red warning that stops being red at its peak is the failure mode; at 0.9 the peak blooms red-pink and the trough stays pure laser red.
- **The pulse is a raised cosine squared at 1.6 Hz** between a floor of 0.35 and 1 — sharp peaks, wide troughs, an alarm's shape rather than a breath. At the floor the beams' luminance sits just under the glare threshold and at the peak far over it, so the pulse visibly breathes the bloom itself, not just the brightness.
- **The net spans the field footprint (+0.75 bleed), which keeps it entirely inside the drain's mouth.** Every authored footprint's half-diagonal is well under `ArenaIsland.FUNNEL_TOP_RADIUS` (14), so the beams can never cross the gold bead at the rim, and the gun — whose orbit's lower bound already clears the footprint — never stands in them. One static buffer per level, rebuilt in `Fit` from `FitFieldToMap` (the ceiling renderer's cadence), one draw call, three per-frame uniforms.
- **Drawn in the trails' states — additive, `DepthRead`, `CullNone` — but between the two glasses, and the position is load-bearing.** The net lies between the drain and the ceiling in Y, and neither translucent draw can depth-test against it (the net writes no depth), so the order has to encode the geometry: *after* `DrawSettingGlass`, because the funnel is entirely below the net and a drop cinematic looking down the throat has the net in front of the glass — drawn earlier, the cone wrongly dimmed it — and *before* the ceiling plate, which is above the net, so an overhead shot seeing the net through the glass still gets the plate composited over it. The island, the gun and any ball in front occlude the beams either way. (There is a third translucent surface in the frame since the barrel's window was glazed, and it needs no such reasoning: the gun's pane is drawn after everything, being nearer than all of it — see "The gun's hardware, in one copy" in `docs/testbed.md`.)
- **`lasers` on the command line pins it on** while a level is played: reaching it honestly means playing to within two steps of losing, which can no more be scripted than clearing a level can — the `celebrate` reasoning, for a session-owned effect.

## The in-play HUD (Game)

What the scoring rules (see "Scoring" in `docs/game-session.md`) look like while a level is being played (#60), and how they *move*. It lives in **`Game/Screens/PlayHud.cs`** — its own class, for the reason `ScoreKeeper` is one: this is the feel of the thing, the part most likely to be argued about and retuned, and it has no business being another few hundred lines inside `GameplayScreen` where the dials cannot be seen together. It owns no score; it is handed the keeper each frame and animates what it reads there. Drawn in one `SpriteBatch.Begin/End` **after the tonemap resolve**, in display space, like the crosshair and for the same reason — a HUD downsampled with the scene would be soft.

**Not Myra**, and that is not only about input arbitration (Myra's `Desktop.Render` also consumes the mouse and the keyboard, and the game captures the cursor while playing): Myra has no animation system to reach for. It lays widgets out, and every motion below would have to be driven from outside it frame by frame anyway. And **a shader over the text is the wrong tool** — `SpriteBatch.Begin(effect:)` will happily put a pixel shader over the glyph quads, but each glyph is its own quad, so anything that reaches *outside* the glyph (a blur, a smear) is clipped at the quad's edge and stops dead at the letterforms. What is used instead: FontStashSharp's own `FontSystemEffect.Blurry`, which rasterizes a blurred variant into the **atlas** (so a glow costs one extra atlas variant per window size, not a pass per frame), and repeated draws along the path for the motion smear.

- **The corners, because they are the only space free in *both* poses.** The centre of the frame belongs to the cluster, the bottom to the gun, and precise aim leans the lens in over the barrel and fills more of it still. Score top **right** (the FPS line owns the top left, and `InfoRenderer` draws after this in `base.Draw`), balls left bottom **left**. Verified in the overview, in ADS and at 21:9 fullscreen (3840×1600) — everything is placed off `Viewport.Width/Height` and scaled by `Viewport.Height / 2160` through the menu's own `Scaled()`, so a wider display only moves the two columns apart.
- **It is authored LOUD, and that is the brief.** A HUD in a game is not a data readout: the score and the ball count are two of the three things the player is tracking, they have to land while the eye is in the middle of the frame on the cluster, and the corners are empty in both poses so size costs nothing. The first pass was tuned like an overlay — small type, a 0.34 pop, a single-pass halo — and read as barely-there at a glance, which is the failure mode to check for. Type runs 140/112/76 design units (score/award/label), the score's pop is +0.7 of its own size, and the halo is drawn `HUD_GLOW_PASSES` times because **one pass of a blur is always fainter than the text it came from** — a blur spreads a glyph's energy over its own area, which is the wrong way round for a flash meant to be the loudest thing on the frame. Compounding alpha gets a dense core and a soft edge, which is what a bloom looks like and what a single pass at any alpha cannot be.
- **One primitive carries "this number just changed": `Pulse`.** A scalar resting at 1, **displaced** by a kick and sprung back. A spring rather than a keyframed curve for the reason `PreciseAim.Blend` is a blend and not a state machine — a second kick landing mid-settle adds to the one in flight, so a burst of scoring shots reads as one rising swell instead of an animation restarting under the eye. The kick moves the *position*, not the velocity: a pop that has to accelerate first reads as a wobble, not as a hit. ω ≈ 17.9 rad/s at ζ ≈ 0.45 is one visible undershoot of about a fifth of the kick and then done, and it is **snapped to rest under an epsilon** exactly as `CameraShake`'s decay ends — an exponential settle never quite arrives, and a permanent sub-pixel tremble on a number read every second is worse than no motion. It is **sub-stepped at 1/120 s**, because an explicit spring this stiff goes unstable as the step approaches 2/ω ≈ 0.11 s and this game really does run at ten frames a second on weak hardware until the adaptive tier steps in. Kicks **saturate** (`MAX_OFFSET`) the way `CameraShake`'s trauma does — at this amplitude two awards inside one settle would otherwise throw the score off the top of the frame, and `HUD_FONT_SCORE` is chosen against that clamp: the score is pivoted on its vertical centre at `HUD_MARGIN + height/2`, so the fully-kicked number must still clear the frame.
- **The halo must be centred by its own measurement, not the text's.** A blurred glyph is a larger bitmap with its own render offset, so drawing it from the plain text's origin puts the halo down and to the right of the number — on screen it looked like a stray glow *beside* the score rather than the score glowing. `MeasureString` takes the effect and amount; measure with them and centre the two boxes. `HUD_MARGIN` also has to clear the halo, not just the text: a glow drawn hard against the frame's edge is a glow cut in half by it, which reads as a rendering fault.
- **The direction of the kick is the information.** Score and streak grow (they went up); the ball count shrinks (it went down). They are deliberately not one shared figure. Each is pivoted on the corner it is aligned to — the score on its right edge, the ball count on its bottom-left — so the margins hold rock-steady and only the figure moves.
- **The awards fly into the score, and landing there is what pays it.** A popup is born on the cell it was earned in, held `POPUP_HOLD` (0.5 s) to be read, then flown to the corner over `POPUP_FLIGHT` (0.55 s) — and the readout's count-up starts on the frame it *lands*, not a second earlier when it would mean nothing. `_scoreShown` therefore chases `score.Score` **less the points still in the air**, recomputed from the list rather than carried as a running total, so the two agree by construction once the last popup lands, whatever happened in between (a level torn down mid-flight, a completion bonus with no popup at all — that one calls `FlashScore` so it is not the one award the score takes without being hit).
- **The flight is a lerp from a source reprojected every frame**, so at the handover the blend weight is zero and nothing snaps — the one-reversible-scalar idiom again. The ease is a *back-in*: it pulls backwards first, then accelerates, arriving at its greatest speed, which is where the smear should be longest and where the corner is about to be hit. The last valid projection is remembered, because a drop cinematic routinely swings the cell out of frame mid-flight.
- **The motion blur is sampled off the curve, not off the last frame.** The path is closed-form, so looking back a fixed distance *in flight parameter* gives a trail whose length is the curve's own local speed — nothing to store, nothing to go stale, identical at any frame rate, and zero cost while the number is not moving. Ghost alpha falls as `t²` behind the head, which is what a shutter integrates and what stops the trail reading as a row of copies.
- **Colour: one accent, and it means *gain*.** The HUD sits over thirteen scene palettes whose colours are nothing alike, so a hue that reads as the game's own over one of them fights the next — the same constraint the menu solves by going greyscale. **Amber** (`HUD_ACCENT`) survives them all because it is the sun's own colour and every scene already has it, and it is spent on the score's arrival flash (tint + `Blurry` halo, the halo squared so it marks the hit and is gone before the spring settles), on the streak, and on the last two balls. The **only** other colour is a popup's at birth: the colour of the balls that were actually cut, as the lit ball actually shows it — `TypeColor` bakes each type's tint once through the pipeline's own transform (the pattern shader's linearization, a nominal lit level, the tonemap's ACES curve and its sRGB encode, calibrated against a screenshot of the real cluster; #153). It used to be the raw tint peak-lifted the way the launch smear does it, which kept the hue but printed every colour lighter than the ball it stood for — the 8-ball's 0.045 grey came out almost white. It turns to amber as it flies, so the gesture literally says *those balls became those points* — colour that is never decoration because the cluster is always there to justify it.
- **Balls left escalates in three steps and only the last is a colour.** At `HUD_LOW_BALLS` (5) the number grows a step (`HUD_LOW_EMPHASIS`) and starts to **breathe** — motion is a stronger alarm than hue and costs nothing over thirteen palettes; at `HUD_CRITICAL_BALLS` (2) it takes the accent as well. That first step was a heavier *weight* until the HUD moved to Anton, which has only one: a bold slot on a single-weight face resolves to the very same glyphs, so the step would have vanished in silence and left a documented three-step alarm with two. It is pivoted on its bottom-left corner, so growing it cannot push it off frame the way the centre-pivoted score would be. Breathing, not blinking: a slow swell reads as urgency, a hard flash reads as a fault. Both run off the **play** clock, so they stop when the game does.
- **The streak warms as it climbs and is held when it breaks.** ×2 → ×5 ramps `MENU_TEXT` → `HUD_ACCENT` over the whole range `ScoreKeeper.MaxMultiplier` allows, so the ramp keeps meaning the same thing if the cap is retuned, and the badge on a popup is the same colour as the number in the corner. It is still held back at ×1 — it reads as something the player is carrying rather than furniture — but its *loss* is the event, so the broken value is held `STREAK_BREAK_TIME` (0.45 s), sliding down and fading. A thing that vanishes between frames is a thing nobody saw.
- **The ceiling warning is the cluster profile, not a counter — and the reason a counter was the wrong shape.** The 3D glass coming down at the cluster *was* meant to be its own warning, and a number counting to the next descent says a second time what the player can already see — so for a long time there was none, and `ScoreKeeper.ShotsToNextCeiling` sat unused. What that left unspoken is *where the glass stands*: the 3D plate sliding against a sky is nearly invisible (see "The ceiling announces itself"), the floor laser net says only that the end is near, and neither shows the glass's height against the cluster and the death line. A counter would name *when* but never *where*. The answer is a **side-cut panel** down the left edge (`DrawClusterProfile` in `PlayHud`, the only strip free in both poses between the FPS line and the balls-left readout): the glass as a bar at its true height, the cluster's balls as round markers in their own type colours at their true spacing, and the death line at the bottom — so a descent reads as the bar closing on the balls.
  - **A side cut, ISOTROPIC, drawn directly over the scene with no backing plate.** The cut is a true section: the vertical axis is world Y, the horizontal axis is each ball's projection onto the **camera's right axis** (`forward × up`, the same basis the audio listener is posed with). Both axes share ONE scale — `pixelsPerUnit = min(panelHeight/span, width/(2·halfDepth))` — so the cluster keeps its real proportions and the balls sit at their actual spacing (a ball is 1 unit across, levels 1/√2 apart). `halfDepth` is the field's half-diagonal (`FieldHalfDiagonal`, the same arithmetic `FitCannonAndGameCameraToLevel` clears the corners with). There is no border, no label, no dark fill behind it — the balls and bars draw straight over the scenery, which keeps the panel from reading as a box plastered over the world. The markers carry their own contrast (their colours as the lit ball shows them — `TypeColor`'s baked display-space palette, shared with the award popups since #153; the small baked-in sheen is what keeps the 8-ball's dark marker visible instead of the almost-white dot the old peak-lift printed).
  - **A ball marker is a PROCEDURAL circle, not a texture.** `DrawDisc` builds each dot as a stack of horizontal bars — one per pixel row — each as wide as the circle's half-chord at that height (`√(r²−dy²)`), drawn from the HUD's own 1×1 white texel. A circle texture needed premultiplied alpha to lose the white square every dot sat in, and a separate asset to keep in step; a circle built from the pixel needs neither, and is exact at any radius. The marker is sized at the ball's true world diameter (`pixelsPerUnit`, since a ball is 1 unit across), so a dense cluster's dots sit edge-to-edge the way the real cluster packs.
  - **The panel is FIXED size and frames the whole field.** It spans from the glass's resting place at the top (`FIELD_TOP_Y + CEILING_CLEARANCE`) to the death line at the bottom, and does not resize as balls are shot and matched — a panel that grew and shrank read as something wrong with the HUD, and it shifted under the eye every shot. The glass's descent is what moves; the frame that holds it does not.
  - **`PROFILE_WIDTH` is the only lever on how big the cut reads, and #89 ("the panel reads as tiny") was the width alone.** The scale is the `min` of two terms and they are nowhere near each other: the field spans **13.16** world units vertically against a half-diagonal of **9.2–12.7**, so at the old width of 200 the width term gave **10.9 px per world unit** where the height term offered **98.8**, and the cut was drawn nine times smaller than the panel holding it. A ball came out **11 px** across at 2160p. At 480 it is **26**, and the whole cut 344 px tall instead of 143. Raising `PROFILE_HEIGHT` does nothing whatever: it feeds only the losing scale term and a centre that works out to the viewport's own middle regardless — it survives as the cap it always was. Making the height term bind would need a width of ~2 500 design units, two thirds of a 16:9 frame, so the two are not meant to meet. Design units scale off viewport **height**, so the panel holds its proportion of the frame at every aspect (12.5 % of the width at 16:9, 14.6 % at 4:3) and the `panelHeight <= width` guard cannot trip: `maxHeight` is 0.915·H against a width of 0.194·H.
  - **A ball still in the AIR is drawn too, as a RING.** `BuildClusterProfile` used to walk only the hanging structure, so a shot was invisible on the panel for its whole flight and appeared only once it had attached — which is exactly the stretch worth watching (#89). It now also walks `_shotBalls` and `_fallingBalls`, the two lists the cluster array by definition does not hold, and marks them `InFlight`. The ring is the same `DrawDisc` scan with an inner radius: the rows that cross the hole draw their two remaining segments instead of one, so there is one primitive rather than two that could drift apart. A ring rather than a disc because the panel's subject is where the glass stands against the *cluster*, and a ball that has not attached yet must be visible without being counted by the eye as cluster. **Held to the panel's window**: the gun stands on the island well below the death line, so a shot spends the first half of its climb under the panel's floor — drawn unclamped it would sit under the death line, which on this panel means exactly one thing, *lost*. A cluster ball can never fail that test, having ended the level if it did.
  - **A ball that crosses the death line dissolves rather than popping (#134), and the two things under that line are told apart by which way they are moving.** Cutting a falling ball dead on the frame it crosses was the pop: it is still falling for another 36 units before `KILL_PLANE_Y` takes it. It now goes on being drawn past the line, its colour multiplied down to nothing over `PROFILE_SINK_FADE` — 2 world units, like everything else the panel measures, so it is the same fall at every resolution (~22 px at a 1600×900 client, 52 at 2160p, both inside the space under the panel). The rising shot above must *not* get this, or every shot would show a ball under the line for the first half of its climb, and the discriminator cannot be which list the ball came from — a shot that missed is still in `_shotBalls` on the way back down. It is the **body's own velocity** (`BallMarker.Falling`), read fresh each frame, so the panel stays what it is: rebuilt from scratch, remembering nothing. A distance and not a duration for the same reason — a timed fade would have to keep per-ball state — which also makes the dissolve last as long as the ball's speed says it should. **Measured** from the draw itself: a missed shot fell back out at 7.4 units a second and the alphas ran 0.73 down to 0.04 in even steps, **16 frames** of dissolve at 60, with the climbing shots below the line still culled throughout. (Verified numerically rather than from a capture: the film grain animates every pixel under the panel, so frame-to-frame differencing cannot pick a marker out of it — the same reason the grain goes off before judging any shader change.)
  - **The glass bar is drawn at the plate's OWN thickness, and that was the one mark that used to lie (#133).** Everything else on the panel is a literal isotropic read of the world; the bar was a fixed design-unit figure (16) with no relation to `CeilingPlate.THICKNESS`, so at the 1600×900 client it came out **7 px where the plate is 11**, its underside floated 2 px above the glass's real underside, and the topmost balls read as hanging with a gap. There is no gap: at rest the cluster hangs **flush**, the ball's top surface and the plate's underside agreeing to **0.001 of a unit** (measured on One — 7.156 against 7.157). `CeilingPlate.THICKNESS × pixelsPerUnit`, floored at 3 px so the mark the whole panel is read against cannot vanish on a short window, and the gap measures 0 px after it. The death line keeps a design-unit thickness on purpose: it is a **threshold**, not a thing, and has no world thickness to be drawn at. **A trap worth recording**, because it made the first probe of this report a liar: `CLEARANCE` places the plate 2 units over the field's topmost level, but that is the layout's *grid* position, and the constraints then pull the cluster **0.272 up** to hang off the glass. Sampled on the first frame the gap reads 0.273 and no player ever sees it — sample after the cluster has settled.
  - **The glass bar is NEUTRAL at rest and red only on the flash; the death line is ALWAYS red.** The glass is the ceiling the balls hang from — a bar that is red at rest read as a warning when none was due. It takes the alarm's red only for the flash, squared exactly as the 3D `EmissiveTint` is scaled by `_ceilingFlash²`: unmistakable on the frame the glass steps, back to neutral before the slide finishes. The death line at the bottom is the one place the red is permanent — it IS the warning, the line a ball must not cross.
  - **The cut reads the GAMEPLAY camera, not the cinematic lens.** `_gameplayCameraRight` holds the right-axis of the pose *before* the drop cinematic's blend (`UpdateCamera` resolves it before lerping towards `_cinematic`), and the profile reads that vector instead of the live camera. A cinematic swings the lens to follow the falling cluster, and a profile that turned with it would read as the HUD shaking rather than as the cluster turning — so the cut holds still while the camera flies.
  - **The balls are the live bodies, this frame.** `BuildClusterProfile` walks `_physicsBalls` exactly the way the loss check and the instance collection do — same null-check, same XZLevel sizing, the drawn position render-offset included — so a ball gliding into its cell glides in the cut too, and a swaying cluster sways in it. They are written into one reused backing array (`_profileBalls`, sized to the field's cell count) and handed to the HUD as a span — no per-frame allocation on the gameplay path, the same discipline the instancing buckets keep.
  - **Drawn first, inside the corner readouts' own `Begin/End`, and hidden with them.** It is the first call in `PlayHud.Draw` so the corner numbers and any incoming award pass over it; it lives in the same default-state `SpriteBatch` block as the readouts, in display space after the tonemap, so it adds no render pass. And it stops when the level does — the `if (_pendingOutcome == LevelOutcome.None)` that gates the whole HUD gates it too.
- **It stops when the level does.** The result screen states the score itself, so the readout behind it is the same figure said twice — and the corners would be the only thing left pinned to the screen while the camera is released and swings out around the arena. An award caught mid-flight is the visible half of that: it ages on the play clock, which has stopped, so it would hang frozen and be reprojected against a moving camera, sliding across the frame towards a score nobody is playing for.
- **The score chases rather than jumps** (an exponential chase with a floor rate so a big award still lands promptly). A number that snaps 0 → 2,960 is not read as a number at all.
- **Every figure is `CultureInfo.InvariantCulture`**, and the strings are rebuilt only when their *number* changes. The machine's locale decides what `"N0"` groups with, and a popup reading "+2 960" beside a corner score reading "2,960" is one number written two ways in one frame — it happened. Formatting three of them per frame would also be three managed allocations per frame on the gameplay path, which the render-hygiene rules rule out, and the count-up means the value genuinely does change on most frames of an award.
- **Testing it needs the game actually running, and the harness traps cost a round of screenshots each.** A popup lives ~1.05 s and the flight leg is the second half of that, so a capture taken a second after the shot shows nothing and reads as "it does not work" — capture in a burst, and note a full-window `CopyFromScreen` at 1600×900 costs ~90 ms, which is about as fast as a scripted burst can sample a 0.4 s spring (crop to the corner to go faster). **Drive the menus with Enter alone and never with Down**: Enter is edge-driven and has no repeat, and pressing it with the focus cursor down only *raises* it, so three Enters reach level 1 whether the cursor started down (raise, Play, level) or up (Play, level, a no-op in play) — whereas a held Down flirts with `NAV_REPEAT_DELAY` (0.42 s), intermittently steps twice, opens the **Scene page** instead of Play and silently voids the run, changing the scene under the very measurement being taken. (A key must still be held across a frame to be seen at all; the keyboard is polled once per frame.) Confirm the window actually took focus before sending anything — unfocused, `UpdateMenuChrome`'s `IsActive` block never runs and every press is dropped. And a scripted `rmbdown` for the ADS pose that is never released leaves the **button held at the OS level** after the process is killed: Windows then treats every later click as part of a drag and the next run's menu clicks vanish silently. Release it in the harness's exit path.

## The sound (Game)

The first audio the game has (#46): the shot leaving the barrel and a ball landing in the lattice, both **synthesized from raw 16-bit PCM at startup** in `Game/Audio/ProceduralAudio.cs` and played back as plain `SoundEffect`s. `SoundEffect`'s constructor takes a PCM buffer directly, so there is no asset file, no `.mgcb` entry and no pipeline step — the same "procedural, nothing to install" line the meshes and the surface textures already take.

- **The synthesis is a small signal chain, not a tone**, and that is what separates it from a beep: layers of additive harmonics, low-passed noise transients and a sub-bass weight are mixed on a float buffer, run through a **Schroeder reverb** (four parallel comb filters into two series all-passes — the Freeverb topology) for a sense of space, normalised, and only then quantized to PCM. Baking once at load means the runtime cost of a shot is a buffer play.
- **The shot is layered like a discharge**, because a cannon is not one sound and each part of it lives in a different band: a **boom** (a 150→60 Hz pitch drop that crashes in 30 ms and then rings, plus a rumble low-passed to 190 Hz), a **zap** (a short additive sawtooth sweep, the charge's own whine), a **crackle** (noise band-passed to 1.5–5 kHz under a ~45 Hz tremolo) and a **muzzle thud**. The crackle is there for a specific reason: that band is where the ear is most sensitive, so it is what keeps the shot present and cutting **at the volume a game is actually played at**, which is far quieter than a real cannon.
- **A landing is one note per ball type** — eight steps over a low register wide enough that adjacent colours sit about a tone apart rather than a fraction nobody can hear — fronted by a filtered contact click and underpinned by a sub thump. Shorter and duller than the shot on purpose: a ball meeting its own kind should read as solid, not explosive.
- **A release answers separately (#46): the lattice's snap, then the freed balls popping away in a quick rising run.** The landing tone stays blind to what came loose, deliberately; the release sound carries that half, scaled by the count the way the firework burst scales by size — bigger is louder and a shade deeper, capped at 15 balls where louder stops meaning anything. The six pops are **band-passed noise, not tones**: the theme transposes itself per pass, so a melodic run in any fixed key would land wrong against half of them, where a noise pop's "pitch" is a gesture the ear reads against nothing. The run rises and accelerates slightly — release reads as reward even though the balls fall — and a plain attach plays only the thunk.
- **Nothing plays twice the same.** Every play takes a small random pitch, and a landing is **placed where it happened** and attenuated with distance to a floor rather than to silence, so a hit on the left of the field is heard on the left and a far one is quieter but never lost.
- **The five sounds that have a place in the world are put there by the hardware (#75)**, through `AudioEmitter`/`AudioListener`/`Apply3D` rather than by a pan computed here: the shot leaves the **muzzle** (which it did not before — it was centred, and the muzzle was treated as the lens's own work), a landing sounds at the cell it stuck to, a release at the cell that broke, and a shell whistles and bursts where it actually is. The four that have no emitting object — the party popper and the three UI sounds — never reach an emitter at all, which is a stronger form of the rule that already governed them.
- **Direction from the hardware, distance from the game, and that split is the whole design.** X3DAudio's own attenuation is a `1/d` curve that would put a landing 28 units out **29 dB down** — precisely what the floor above and the fireworks' near-flat term were written to avoid, and it cannot be replaced, because XNA's `AudioEmitter` exposes no custom curve. So it is switched **off** instead: `SoundEffect.DistanceScale` is set once to 10 000, at which the output matrix sums to exactly 1.0000 from 0 to 500 units in every direction (measured, stereo and 5.1 alike). The platform then contributes placement and nothing else, and every distance law in the file survives verbatim. It is a plateau radius, not an off switch — the curve does bite, at 20 000 units — and varying it per source would quietly put an inverse-square curve back on top of the game's own.
- **`Apply3D` needs a handle, and `SoundEffect.Play` is fire-and-forget** — so each spatial buffer gets a `VoiceRing`: a fixed ring of `SoundEffectInstance`s built once at load and reused forever (4 shot, 3 per ball type, 3 release, 12 launch, 32 burst — 75 voices). Bounded by construction because **the framework will not bound it**: MonoGame only recycles the instances it pooled itself, and `MAX_PLAYING_INSTANCES` is `int.MaxValue` on WindowsDX, so a create-per-play design leaks a source voice per sound with no exception and no symptom. Every `Take()` **stops** the slot it hands back, and that is correctness rather than tidiness: `SoundEffectInstance.Play()` returns *early* when the instance is already playing, so a still-sounding slot would silently swallow the new sound instead of replacing it. The two firework rings are sized by the **opening barrage**, not the steady state — at the start of a display every shell slot is free, so shells go up at ~14 a second until they run out (a simulated peak of ten concurrent whistles), and all 32 shells report inside one 2.6 s window, which is why the burst ring is the shell count itself. That last one has **no margin**: a shell slot can free and refire in 2.52 s, already inside the report, so a longer burst bake or a shorter shell life starts clipping tails silently.
- **The call order around `Apply3D` is load-bearing, and each way of getting it wrong is silent.** It writes the voice's output matrix **and** its frequency ratio, so the pitch must be set *after* it or every random nudge and every size-encoded offset is wiped. `Volume` is a separate setting and is safe anywhere. `Pan` writes the **same matrix** `Apply3D` does, so it must never be touched on a placed voice — which is exactly why the no-listener-yet fallback uses it deliberately: before there is a listener there is nothing to place against, and Pan is the only way left to address that matrix. And nothing resets a reused voice's matrix between plays, so `Apply3D` is re-applied on every take rather than only when the position moved: a slot replayed without it would sound at the **previous** event's position.
- **A NaN reaching X3DAudio silences the whole audio graph** — every voice, permanently, with no exception raised and no way back. Both ends are gated on being finite: a bad camera pose leaves the last good listener standing, and a bad world point falls back to the listener's own position (a coincident emitter is measured safe, and comes back as an even spread). The listener is also posed with a **normalised** up vector crossed from world up, because X3DAudio neither normalises nor validates the orientation it is handed: an up vector of the wrong length silently skews the image and a degenerate one collapses it to dead centre.
- **The bearing is an angle now, which is why one width constant replaced two.** The old law divided a *sideways distance* by a full-width — 18 units for the cluster, 90 for the sky, because a sideways distance is scale-dependent and the two live at completely different scales. An angle is not, so the sky needs no constant at all: the honest bearing up there is already **more than twice as wide** as `SKY_PAN_WIDTH` made it. The near field is the opposite case and keeps one exaggeration (`NEAR_WIDEN`, 1.9): a cluster ten units across seen from thirty subtends about twenty degrees, so the *truthful* image of a landing is narrower than the mix has ever had, and at 1.9 the edge of the field lands within a fraction of a decibel of where it has always sat. Set it to 1 for the plain truth — it was tried, and it reads as the stereo collapsing.
- **Overhead is folded towards the middle, and without that a bang comes out of one speaker while the player looks straight up at it.** A stereo image carries a horizontal bearing and nothing else — measured: a source at a fixed ground bearing returns an *identical* matrix at heights 0, 10, 25, 52, 80 and 130 — so a shell nearly above the lens has its side decided by a few units of horizontal offset, and about four shells a display would crack hard over. The sideways leg is scaled by the cosine of the elevation, which is what every height channel's downmix does and which leaves anything near the horizon exactly where it is. The old `lateral/90` law damped this case by accident; an angle law does not.
- **What "positional" does *not* buy, so nobody oversells it.** There is **no height cue** on stereo *or* on 5.1 — X3DAudio's law is the azimuth in the horizontal plane, measured identical at every elevation on both. **Doppler is refused** rather than merely unused (`DopplerScale` 0): no velocity is written, but the lens is not a head — the ADS lean moves it at ~140 units a second, 40 % of the speed of sound, and a listener velocity would detune everything sounding the instant the player leans in. `SoundEffect.SpeedOfSound` is **dead code** on WindowsDX and setting it would be a wrong "why" left standing. And surround placement is real — a source dead ahead goes to the centre speaker, one behind to the rear pair — but it is **unverified on this machine**, which is stereo.
- **The move cost 2.92 dB at the centre of the image and it could not be avoided.** X3DAudio's matrix sums to 1 in every direction where the pan law it replaces wrote *both* channels at full for a centred sound: a centred sound is exactly 6.02 dB quieter, of which only 3.10 dB is recoverable, because `Volume` throws above 1. `BASE_VOLUME` went 0.7 → 1.0 to take that back. A sound at the edge of the field loses nothing (both laws write [0, 1] hard over), so the shift is **position-dependent** and no single constant can undo it — the wide sounds gain relative to the centred ones, which mostly means the celebration reads a little louder and much more even across the sky. The popper and the three UI sounds still play through the centre-hot pan law, so they are trimmed by exactly the old `BASE_VOLUME` (`NON_SPATIAL_TRIM`, 0.7) to leave the menu's mix precisely where it was. If the effects end up sitting too low under the bed and the theme, the honest fix is the other end — `AMBIENCE_VOLUME`, `MUSIC_VOLUME`, `MENU_VOLUME` and `FANFARE_VOLUME` each times ~0.71 — and that is an ear's call, not arithmetic, so it has deliberately not been made here.
- **The listener is the host's, posed once a frame from the one camera there is** — never the session's. Sound is made with no session standing: a whole celebration of it over a frozen gameplay screen, and the menu's clicks over an orbiting backdrop. It is posed from the pose the *previous* frame was drawn from, which is exactly the staleness the panning it replaces already had (half a unit at sixty frames a second). Its up axis is crossed from **world** up rather than taken from the camera, which costs hundredths of a decibel even under the drop cinematic's dutch tilt and buys two things: the soundfield can never be left tipped by a roll the camera is still carrying from a screen the player has left, and the basis stays the one the cluster profile is drawn on. On the very first frame of a run the camera has not been posed yet — it is the *stack* that poses it, further down the same Update — so the fallback plays centred. With the `celebrate` argument that is the display's first launch whistle; the party popper beside it is centred by construction rather than by the fallback, never having reached an emitter at all. The latch is set once and never cleared, so a bad pose later leaves the last good listener standing rather than dropping back to centre.
- **The menu clicks (#46), and one wrapper covers every input device.** `MenuButton` wraps each entry's action, so the press sounds whether it arrived through Myra's `Click` (the mouse) or through the `Tag` that `ActivateNavEntry` invokes (the pad and the arrow keys) — an entry added later cannot forget its click. One 90 ms buffer serves all three UI sounds: the press plays it straight, the focus cursor's step plays it pitched up (`PlayUiTick` — faster playback is also shorter, which is what a step wants, and far quieter, because a held direction walks the list and the tick repeats with it), and backing out plays it pitched down, so leaving sounds lower than entering. The click itself is a dry "thock" — a low-passed knock over a fast 900→420 Hz drop with a touch of 150 Hz weight — with **no reverb and no pan**, the party popper's reasoning: the UI lives at the player's hand, not out in the scene. A screen change restores the focus cursor by assignment rather than through `StepNavFocus`, deliberately, so arriving on a page does not tick.
- **The effects stay MONO, and that is a constraint rather than an omission.** #119 gave the music a stereo image and deliberately stopped there. These buffers are placed by the hardware through `Apply3D`, which takes a mono source — a positional sound has no left and right of its own, because where it is *is* what the matrix is about — so baking them in stereo would not widen them, it would take the placement away. The ambience beds were the open question the music's change did not settle — they are not spatialized, so they *could* carry width — and #146 answered it (see "The ambience"). It needed a **different technique** rather than the same one applied again: a bed is noise, and noise is widened by decorrelating the two channels, not by panning a layer that would only move the point it comes from.
- **The star cue is the one UI sound that is a TONE, and it is not on the click's buffer (#139).** The result screen's rating lands one star at a time, and each landing plays `PlayStarEarned` — a small struck chime over a 2 ms noise tick for the hammer. It could not be the click repitched like the tick and the back are: the click is a dry knock, and a knock four times over reads as a stutter rather than as a reward. It could not be a **noise** gesture the way the release's pops deliberately are, because a noise "pitch" has nothing to read against. And the run **rises**, which is what makes a rating something the player hears being counted; the last star is lifted slightly (`STAR_FINAL_LIFT`), the only thing separating a four-star clear's fourth star from a three-star clear's third.
- **It does NOT play into silence, and that wrong sentence is what #158 cost.** This entry used to say the chime was played over "a stopped world with no music under it" — which was **never true**: `CheckLevelCleared` starts the victory fanfare the instant the field clears, *before the result screen exists*, so every star has always landed inside its ~9 seconds. Three consequences followed from the one bad premise, and all three were reported as the chime sounding cheap. It was baked on a **free bar's inharmonic ratios** (1, 2.76, 5.40, 8.93) — right for a bell struck into silence, dissonant against tonal harmony in any key. It sounded at a **fixed 880 Hz**, so it agreed with the piece only when that piece happened to roll A. And it stepped **~2.3 semitones** a star, which is an interval in no key at all.
  - **The fix is that the chime asks the fanfare what it is.** `ProceduralMusic.TryGetFanfare` reports the piece's root and tempo, the chime is retuned to that root's own **major triad** (0-4-7-12, consonant against every chord of its I–V–vi–IV by construction), the reveal takes **one beat a star** from its BPM, and the partials moved to a **tubular bell's** near-harmonic 1:2:3 with one inharmonic 4.2 on top for shimmer.
  - **The key is answered when the fanfare is REQUESTED, not when it is heard**, and that is the part that took two attempts. The piece is baked on a background thread — **over three seconds on the weakest machine**, measured — while the result page opens on the frame the level cleared, so a first version that read the key at `Enter` got "there is no fanfare" every single time, and a second that *waited* for the sound held the rating longer than any player would sit through. The roll moved out of the baker into `RollFanfare` on the calling thread, so the key is known immediately and only the beat grid needs the audio; when the audio is not there yet the row keeps the authored cadence and is still in tune.
  - **`SoundEffect.Play`'s pitch is in octaves over −1…1**, so the whole four-star run has to fit inside one octave of shift. The root is therefore placed in the octave *below* the baked A5 (an offset of −12…−1), leaving the triad room to reach +11 at the top star. Placed above instead, the fourth star of a four-star clear would silently clamp to the wrong note. Verified across the rolled roots: A gives −12/−8/−5/0 and C gives −9/−5/−2/+3, both an in-key major triad and both inside the range.
- **The buffers are the host's, not the session's**, built once in `LoadContent` and disposed in `UnloadContent` — the same split as the camera. A level is torn down and rebuilt per play; the sounds outlive that, and re-baking nine buffers per level would be work for nothing.
- **`SoundEffect.Play`'s pitch is an offset in OCTAVES, not a frequency multiplier**, and getting that wrong made every sound in the game wrong at once. It takes −1…1 where **0 is the sound as baked** and 1 is a full octave up; `NextPitch` returned `1 + jitter`, written as if it were a multiplier, so the shot, every landing, the whistle and the report all played **an octave above** what they were synthesized as. Everything came out thin, toy-like and squeaky — and no amount of retuning the synthesis could have fixed it, because the synthesis was never what was wrong. It is centred on zero now. **If a procedural sound here ever sounds wrong in a way the maths does not explain, check this first.**
- **Peak normalisation is exactly wrong for an explosion, and that is the second reason the bang was not a bang.** The loudest sample of a report is its *crack* — a few milliseconds, tall and thin — so scaling the peak to 1 scales the **body** down by whatever that transient happened to reach, leaving a tick with a quiet thud behind it. `Loudness(signal, targetRms, ceiling)` drives the RMS instead and rounds the transient off through a `tanh`, which is what every explosion effect ever made does; the burst, the popper and the cannon's own shot all take it, and `Normalize` stays for the sounds that are a tone rather than a transient.
- **The roll is what puts a firework over a landscape**, and a reverb cannot do it: `RollingEcho` lays down slap-backs, the report arriving again off every building and hillside around. A reverb alone gives a smooth wash and no roll. **But its spacing and its level both have to be right, and both were wrong first:**
  - **Over about 40 ms apart the ear resolves each repeat as its own event.** Four taps at 60–85 ms made the burst come out as "bum-bum-bum-bum" — a flutter echo, the sound of a corridor. Nine taps starting at 23 ms and stretching by an **irrational** ratio fuse into one event with a tail; the irrational spacing is what stops the repeats reinforcing into a pitch.
  - **The roll must sit UNDER the direct report, not double it.** At a high feedback the first taps land 20–60 ms behind the crack and add a second wavefront on top of it: measured, the envelope stopped peaking on the first sample and climbed to its maximum a tenth of a second in, which the ear hears as a soft "whoomph" instead of a hard "BUM". `RollingEcho` takes a `mix` for exactly this. With it at 0.42 the very first 10 ms window is back at 89 % of the sound's loudest — an immediate attack, which is as fast as a 33 Hz body can physically get there.
- **A vibrato is a shimmer, not a wobble.** The whistle's spin ran at 7 Hz and 2.8 % depth, and at that rate the ear tracks each swing as its own note: it came out as "vu-vu-vu-vu" rather than as a whistle. Shallow and fast (0.6 % at 16 Hz) is what a spinning shell actually does to a tone.
- **The launch is a SPARKLER, not a siren, and its pitch was got wrong once in each direction.** It began at 620→1950 Hz, was reported as squeaky and taken down to 430→1320 — but the squeak was never the synthesis, it was the octave bug above, and with that fixed the lowered tone sat in the register of a **horn**. It runs 900→2600 Hz now, over **0.55 s** rather than 1.15 (at over a second the ear has time to hear it *as a tone*, and a slow rising tone is a siren by definition), and the balance is pushed hard onto the **fizz** — a firework leaving the ground is a burning fuse and a jet of gas before it is a tone at all. It plays at **0.08** against the report's 0.85: with a shell going up every fraction of a second, anything audible enough to identify turns the display into a chorus of kettles, so it is a texture rather than a sound the ear stops on. Measured against its predecessor: half the length, **rough spectral centroid 2 134 → 3 036 Hz**, and the burst untouched at 270 Hz — which was the point, since the report and its crackle were what worked.
- The shells' visual rise came down to 0.62–0.95 s to match: a shell still climbing a second after its own hiss has finished reads as two unrelated things.
- **Measured rather than guessed at** (the bakes were dumped to WAV and analysed, then the dump removed): the burst runs 2.6 s at a **crest factor of 3.64** with **94 % of its energy below 150 Hz** and still a third of its opening level 800 ms in — a compressed, deep bang with a tail. The cannon's shot is 76 % low at a crest of 4.04. Those are the numbers that say "bang" rather than "click", and they are cheap to re-check if it ever regresses.
- **The volume is the player's now (#46), and the split is authored-mix × player-gain.** The mix keeps its tuning in constants (`BASE_VOLUME` here; `MUSIC_VOLUME` and `FANFARE_VOLUME` in the music; `AMBIENCE_VOLUME` in the beds) and four settings rows — Volume, Effects, Music, Ambience — scale it in quarters, "Off" at zero. Each subsystem takes one product through a `Gain` property (`master × its own row`), written only by the host's `ApplyVolumes`, so the two cannot disagree about what the master row means. The effects apply it on the **next** play (the `FireworkDuck` reasoning — nothing there sounds long enough to hear the difference), while the music pushes it onto the instances **already sounding**, because a two-minute pass and a nine-second fanfare are. The rows cycle *downwards* (the reason to click one is almost always "quieter"), `mute` on the command line starts the master at zero for the screenshot/benchmark harnesses, and like every setting none of it is persisted (see "Adaptive quality" in `docs/game-shell.md`).
- **The celebration adds three more, and they are the reason the noise source grew a seed.** A **launch** (below); a **burst** (a crack, a 110→38 Hz boom under it, and a *crackle* of burning stars gated by a fast random stutter — the crackle outlasts the boom by half a second, and that is what separates a firework from a gunshot); and a **party popper**, deliberately close and dry where the shells are big and wet, which is what makes the shells read as distant. `Noise(i, seed)` and `MakeNoiseArray(samples, seed)` exist because these mix **two** noise layers each: the source is a function of the sample index, so two unseeded layers are the *same* sequence differing only in how they were filtered, and summing two filtered copies of one sequence is correlated — it thins out and stops sounding like two separate things.
- **A firework's pan is not a ball's**, and that finding outlived the code that carried it. `PanFor` took one fixed full-width sized to the cluster (18 units); a shell bursts a hundred up and as far out again, and at the cluster's width every one but the shell straight overhead would pan hard to one side — so the width became a per-source argument (`SKY_PAN_WIDTH`, 90). Both constants are gone with `PanFor` itself (#75, above): a *sideways distance* is scale-dependent and needs one constant per scale, an *angle* is not and needs none. The half of the bullet that was never about the pan is untouched — the burst's distance term is still near-flat, because a firework *is* far away and rolling it off the way a landing does makes every burst a whisper.

## The ambience (Game)

Every backdrop sounds like itself now (#46): one looping bed per scene — surf for the sea, a wind for each terrain, a distant traffic rumble for the cities, a near-subliminal drone for space, an ethereal two-band shimmer breathing in opposite phase for the dream, hollow air with sparse water drips for the cavern (the campfire's crackle gate slowed to a few soft taps a second) — synthesized from raw PCM in `Game/Audio/ProceduralAmbience.cs` and crossfaded when the scene changes. The host owns it beside the music, `SetScene` (the one scene writer) retargets it, and it runs off the frame like the clouds: the scene is on screen whether or not a session stands, so its sound is too, **pause included**.

- **Filtered noise, never a tone**, and that is the design rule of the whole file: a bed sits under the music and the effects for the entire run, and anything with a pitch would either fight the theme (which transposes itself per pass) or be noticed — and a bed that is noticed is a bed that is too loud. The one near-tonal thing in the set, the neon city's electric hum, is a narrow **band of noise** around 100–150 Hz, not an oscillator.
- **The loops are sealed by construction, and the seam was measured.** Every envelope runs a whole number of cycles per 16 s loop, and each bed renders a second past the loop point that is folded back onto the head under an equal-power ramp — continuous play of a loop is exactly the head plus the previous pass's ring-out. Measured at the wrap: the sample step across the seam is **0.1–1.5× the mean step** of the bed itself, i.e. indistinguishable from any other pair of samples; no click.
- **Levels are RMS targets per scene** (sea 0.16 down to space 0.05), set at bake so the mix is authored, and the band splits confirm each bed is what it claims: the cities put 52–56 % of their energy under 200 Hz (rumble), the forest 69 % above 2 kHz (rustle), space 82 % low (drone), the sea spread across all three (wash and foam a quarter-cycle apart, the hiss of a wave arriving after its weight).
- **The savanna carries its campfire** — the scene visibly has one, so its bed audibly does: band noise gated by a fast random stutter (~47 re-rolls a second), the firework crackle's trick at a hearth's scale, each snap opening the gate at once and the gate falling with a tail.
- **The crossfade is 1.5 s, equal-power, and only ever two instances live** — the bed fading out and the one fading in; an instance is made per arrival and disposed once it has fully faded. A retarget mid-fade lets the outgoing bed go where it stands, audible only when scenes are clicked through faster than the fade — the scene picker, not play. Volumes are written only on the frames the fade or the settings actually moved them.
- **They are in STEREO since #146, and for NOISE that means decorrelation rather than panning.** The obvious approach — give each layer a pan, the way an instrument is seated in a mix — does not work here and is the trap this was written around: a bed is filtered noise, and panning noise does not widen it, it moves the point it comes from, because two channels carrying the same sequence at two levels are still one source. So each layer draws a **shared** sequence and one of its **own per side**, mixed constant-power (`left = shared·cos θ + uniqueL·sin θ`, `θ = width·π/2`), which puts the inter-channel correlation at exactly `cos²θ` and — the three sequences being independent and unit-variance — leaves each channel's power at 1 for *every* width. Width is therefore a dial on the image alone and not on the level, which matters more here than anywhere else in the game because these levels were settled by ear once already. It costs almost nothing: `Noise(i, seed)` is a pure function of the index and two seeds are uncorrelated by construction, which is the property `BakeScene` already spent a seed per layer on. The per-side offsets are **+5101/+9173 and not +1**, or a layer's side would be drawn from the very next layer's own sequence.
  - **Width is per layer and per scene, and "wider" is not the goal for all thirteen.** The rule inside a bed is that **low bands stay nearer the middle** — a very low sound gives the ear almost no direction anyway, so decorrelating it buys little image and costs the most on a mono downmix. Across beds it is a judgement about the *place*: the mountain's gusts and the forest's canopy are fully decorrelated because an exposed basin and a canopy genuinely surround you; the sea keeps a middle for the swell's weight and opens out on the foam; the cities hold their rumble near the centre and widen the murmur above it; the dream puts the width on the contrast its two antiphase bands already had, so the image opens and closes as the pair breathes. **Space and the Moon stay nearly a point on purpose** — an enveloping void is a contradiction, and this file's own rule is that a bed the player notices is too loud whatever its level says. So is the `default` arm, which is meant to be conspicuously featureless.
  - **The crackles really are panned, because a snap is a point event.** A burning knot and a falling drop each *have* a place, so each snap rolls its own side from its slot index and holds it for its whole tail (a drip that slid across the room while it rang would be worse than a centred one). The savanna's fire is kept to a **narrow** spread — a hearth a few feet across, where a wide scatter would read as a ring of fires around the player — and the cavern's drips take the **widest** in the set, which is the thing that turns a hollow noise into a room with a roof on it.
  - **Measured, all thirteen, through the private baker with no playback path.** Every bed's RMS is unchanged to four decimals (sea 0.1600, space 0.0500, Moon 0.0350 …), so the authored mix is exactly where it was. Correlation lands on the maths: **0.907 and 0.904** for space and the Moon against a predicted `cos²(0.1π)` = 0.905, **−0.005 and 0.014** for the mountains and forest where both layers are fully spread, and the cities at **0.49/0.59** with their low bands held in. Side/mid runs **0.22** (space, Moon) to **1.01** (mountains). Summing to mono costs **−0.21 dB** on the narrow beds and at most **−3.03 dB** on the widest — which is the theoretical floor for fully decorrelated material, i.e. **nothing cancels anywhere**. The seam still closes in **both** ears (wrap step 0.19–2.63× the mean step, L and R alike) — worth stating, because folding one channel and not the other opens a seam *in one ear only*, which is markedly harder to notice than one in both. The one blemish: the cavern sits **0.40 dB** left, because ~48 randomly-panned drips in a 16 s loop are too few a sample to average to centre; it is well under the ear's threshold at this level and chasing it would make the drips mechanical.
- **The beds have a volume row of their own** (`master × ambience`, through the same `Gain` idiom as everything else): how much atmosphere sits under the music is a taste, and chaining it to the effects row would turn the shot down with it. The authored level itself was retuned by ear after the first build — from 0.16 to a quarter of it — because at 0.16 the beds sat *over* the theme and buried it, and an ambience the ear picks out over the music is too loud by definition.
- Verified in the running game with the keyboard-driven walk: boot fade-in on the pinned scene, four picks on the scene page — one retargeted mid-fade at 400 ms — then Escape back, with the bed continuing under a played level and its pause throughout; no errors, clean stderr. Re-run after #146 for the case the stereo bake actually put at risk — a **crossfade between two stereo beds**, cavern into sea — which needed nothing: the fade writes instance `Volume`, which knows nothing about channels.

## The music (Game)

`Game/Audio/ProceduralMusic.cs` — one file, and **not a loop but a generator**: every pass is a fresh track, synthesized from raw PCM on a background thread while the previous one plays, so the player never hears the same two minutes twice and no two runs of the game sound alike. No tracker file, no asset, no pipeline step: the score is a handful of arrays and the instruments are oscillators, the same line the sound effects, the meshes and the surface textures all take. It plays while a level is being played and stops with it.

There are **four compositions** — `MusicTheme.Pulse`, `Bohemia` (#120), `Nocturne` (#162) and `Dechovka` (#164) — separate pieces, not one piece with a mood switch, sharing only this file's instruments and its one rule. The bullets below are Pulse, the ~2:30 eurodance track the game was built on and the one it opens on; the named parts further down cover what each later piece had to be different in to be worth having. Which one a level plays is "The rotation" at the end of them.

- **The front end has its own piece now (#46), and it is a loop where the theme is a generator.** The menu is visited for moments rather than minutes, so endless variation buys nothing there; instead it is a small arrangement rolled per run (tempo, key, progression, line direction), so no two runs share a lobby: two rounds of held pads — chords over the theme's diatonic progressions, their root an octave under for the warmth a bass would bring — under a quarter-note line and an every-other-bar sparkle on the lobby's own **`Keys`**, an electric-piano voice; then the **groove** (a four-on-the-floor kick, off-beat hats and the theme's own off-beat bass figure — no clap, no ride, a step under the theme's levels, so the lobby stays a lobby), then the **refrain** — `MENU_HOOK`, one syncopated chord-tone motif stated identically across three chords and resolved onto a held octave on the fourth, played twice over the running groove while the line and the sparkle stand aside (two keys lines at once is mud, not counterpoint) — and the groove again to walk it off. The ~77 s loop means the hook returns now and then rather than nagging, and the wrap from full groove back to bare pads reads as a breakdown rather than a restart. Measured across three seeds: intro RMS ~0.055 at 26 % below 200 Hz, groove and refrain ~0.117–0.118 at 58 % — the bass's arrival is real, and the refrain's identity is deliberately melodic rather than a level step. The line sat on the theme's square-wave `Arp` first and **read as a touch-tone phone**: a bare square with a fast decay is a texture inside a full mix and a beep on its own, which is what a slow exposed line makes it. The keys are a fundamental-heavy body with a bright *inharmonic* tine partial that fades several times faster (the attack says "struck", its quick fade is what stops it beeping), a ring long enough that each note runs a step past the next one's start — a phrase, not four plinks — and a shallow tremolo so the ring stays alive. The loop's **seam is closed by construction**: the piece renders a bar of room past the loop point and folds whatever rings into it back onto the head before the cut, so the join carries the same overlap every other bar boundary does. **Which music the moment wants is the stack question**: the host plays the loop exactly while no `GameplayScreen` is on the stack, and the theme's lifecycle stays the session's — with the one ownerless gap closed: leaving to the main menu keeps the session but must not keep its music, so the host stops the theme there and re-wants it on Continue (which comes back *without* a `BuildLevel`; a fresh build's own `Play` is the "already sounding" no-op, so the two writers cannot fight). A pause deliberately keeps the theme running — the game is stopped, not left.
- **Random parameters, never random notes** — that distinction is the whole difference between variation and noise. A pass rolls its own tempo (122–132 BPM), its key (whole tones and minor thirds only: a random *semitone* would put successive passes a half-step apart, which is the one interval that sounds like a mistake rather than a key change), which of five diatonic progressions it uses, whether the arpeggio runs up or down, and how often it embellishes. Everything it can choose is inside a set that cannot sound wrong.
- **The melodies are written as CHORD TONES, not as pitches**, and that is what makes the random progression possible at all. A melody written as absolute pitches only fits the chords it was written over; written as degrees of whatever chord is underneath, the same motif transposes itself and is consonant by construction — which is also the literal form of "one motif moved across the chords", the thing that made it a hook in the first place.
- **The handover is why there is an outro.** A pass is played **once** rather than looped; `Update` puts the next variation on when the current one ends. That costs a frame or two, which would be an audible gap in the middle of a beat — so the arrangement *ends*, fading over its whole last section, and the join lands in silence. Measured: the last two seconds of a pass are at an RMS of **0.0016–0.0019**, about −55 dB. The prelude is the same idea from the other end (no kit in it at all, first two seconds at **0.073** against a chorus's 0.23) so a new pass arrives rather than crashing in.
- **There is always one baking in hand** — one per composition since #120, see "The second theme". If the next pass somehow is not ready, the current one is replayed rather than leaving silence, and the old buffers are disposed only once the replacement exists — so a failure part-way through leaves the previous pass intact and playable.
- **Measured across three separate runs** (re-measured after #186): 155.4 s / 154.0 s / 148.7 s at 123.5 / 124.6 / 129.1 BPM, three completely different SHA1s — and an RMS of 0.1791–0.1792 in all three, because the limiter sets the loudness whatever the arrangement rolled.
- **It is in STEREO since #119, and the width is a seating plan rather than a widener.** Every piece here was one mono buffer every voice summed into, so a dozen instruments played back from a single point; the buffer is interleaved stereo now and each instrument has a seat. Two rules decide it, and the second is where the width actually comes from. **Low stays centre** — kick, bass, timpani and the snare are panned nowhere at all: below ~200 Hz the ear takes almost no direction from level difference, so panning them buys no width, and it costs, because a hard-panned low end loses half its power the moment anything downmixes to mono, which is every laptop and phone this will be played on. Since 61–70 % of these pieces' energy is below 200 Hz, that rule is most of the mix by energy and none of it by width. **Width comes from what is already detuned**: the lead is two saws twelve cents apart and the string section is seven, written to beat against one another, and pulling those apart makes the beating sweep *across* the field instead of wobbling at one point — genuinely different signals on the two sides rather than one signal at two levels, which is why it is far wider than moving a finished mono note off centre. Their **notes** stay where they were; only the detuned voices move. The rest is a kit seated as a kit (hats one side, ride answering from the other), the arp ping-ponged on alternate sixteenths — it never stops, so it is what keeps the image alive through the melody's rests — and the pad and brass chords opened out across their voices.
  - **Seat by the voice's INDEX, never by its pitch**, and this one was measured wrong first. Pitch-derived seating looks equivalent and is not: every pass rolls its own key, so `note % 12` rotates with the transpose and the same chord shape lands somewhere different in every pass — an image that *moves between passes*, which reads as the mix being unstable rather than as variation, and which left the piece leaning to whichever side the key happened to put the chord tones on. The index is a property of the arrangement and is the same in every pass.
  - **A part that can ever play ALONE must not take half of a symmetric pair's seat.** The defeat fanfare's harmony is conditional on the run's intensity, so seating its melody as one of a pair left the tune singing from one speaker on every quieter loss — **measured at 1.41 dB of lean** before the melody went back to centre and the *harmony* became the part that moves. The general form: centre is for whatever may be the only thing sounding.
  - **The limiter reads the interleaved buffer as one signal and drives it with a single number, and that is load-bearing.** Measuring and driving the two channels independently would turn every loud moment on one side into a level change on that side alone — the image pumping, the classic way to wreck a mix while leaving each channel individually correct. If it ever grows a real envelope follower the gain must stay linked.
  - **Measured through the private bakers, three seeds each, no playback path involved.** Everything the rows above record is unchanged — Pulse 134.5–138.5 s at RMS **0.1778–0.1779** *(as #119 left it; #186 rewrote Pulse's arrangement and its figures are in that part below)*, Bohemia 194.4–200.7 s at **0.1765–0.1767**, **zero clipped samples** anywhere and DC 0.0000–0.0016 — because constant-power panning moves energy without changing how much of it there is, and the limiter still sets the loudness. What is new: **channel balance** within 0.15 dB on both long pieces (Pulse −0.14, Bohemia −0.06, victory +0.11, defeat −0.04, the menu loop +0.3 — the lobby's keys carry that piece almost alone, so their seat is deliberately shallow); **side/mid above 200 Hz**, where the image actually lives, of **0.30 (Pulse), 0.26 (Bohemia), 0.32 (victory)**, against 0.17–0.18 for the menu loop and the defeat, which are pads and a single line and have less to spread; and **mono compatibility of −0.07 to −0.19 dB**, i.e. summing to mono costs a fifth of a decibel and cancels nothing. Pulse's peak reads **0.836–0.856** where this file recorded 0.83 for the mono bake — the one figure the change moved, and still nowhere near the ceiling.

- **It is ARRANGED, in ten eight-bar sections** since #186: prelude, intro, verse, chorus, **breeze**, verse, chorus, build, chorus, outro. A sixteen-bar loop with no sections is a ringtone; what makes a track worth hearing for two minutes is that it keeps arriving somewhere. Measured across three seeds (full-band RMS, per section, seed to seed within 0.002): prelude 0.073, intro 0.111, verse 0.215, chorus 0.228, **breeze 0.112**, verse 0.215, chorus 0.228, build 0.196, final chorus 0.231, **outro 0.050** — a range of **13.1 dB** where the nine-section version spanned 10.4 (its row read intro 0.094, verse 0.205, chorus 0.220, breakdown 0.067, build 0.190, outro 0.071, and the figures before *that* — intro 0.130, breakdown 0.041 — predate the breakdown fix the bullet below describes). The pass's *overall* RMS has barely moved through any of it: what changes is the distribution between sections, which is what every one of those fixes moved.
- **The hook is ONE rhythmic motif transposed across the chords**, not four different phrases. Repetition with variation is what a hook *is*; a melody that never repeats is one nobody can hum. The chords are one a bar, four bars round; the verse is short notes with gaps in them and the chorus is **simpler, higher and longer-noted**, because a chorus is usually *less* busy than its verse and that contrast — not volume, and not more notes — is what makes it a chorus. The last bar of every section is a descending fill that hands the phrase on.
- **A breakdown is EMPTIER, not quieter, and the measurement said so.** At its first setting the section fell to a fifth of the verse's level and a *ninth* of its low band, which is a gap rather than a breakdown; almost all of that was simply losing the kick and the bass, which carry most of a mix's energy. The parts that remain are pushed up to compensate. #186 finished the thought: the section is the **breeze** now, and it keeps the sustained floor while losing the kit, so what is emptied is the drums and not the bottom of the piece — measured, its low band went from 22 % of the section's energy to 78 %, i.e. from *no* low end to the same share the verses carry.
- **The parts are arranged around the kick, not laid over it.** Four on the floor; clap on two and four; eighth hats with the off-beats opened; **bass on the off-beat eighths**, because a bass on the beat doubles the kick and both go to mud, while a bass between them is what reads as a pump. The sixteenth arpeggio never stops inside a section, so the track keeps moving through the melody's rests — it is the *sections* it rests in (the prelude and the breeze), not the bars. Under all of it since #186 there is a **sustained floor** that ducks to the beat rather than doubling it, which is the same argument one octave down: see that part below.
- **PolyBLEP, and it matters more here than anywhere else in the project.** A naive saw or square has energy at every harmonic, and at 44.1 kHz everything past Nyquist folds back as *inharmonic* tones — on a lead playing an A5 that is a chorus of wrong notes moving the opposite way to the melody, which reads as cheapness rather than as a bug. Two lines of polynomial correction at each discontinuity remove nearly all of it and, unlike summing band-limited harmonics, cost the same at any pitch.
- **The lead is two saws detuned about twelve cents apart.** That beating is the whole "supersaw" the genre is built on, and two oscillators are enough — the width comes from the detune, not from the count. Much less and they sound like one flat oscillator; much more and it is an out-of-tune chord.
- **A whole number of samples per step, and the track's length taken from that** rather than from the nominal tempo. Rounding each step and then trusting the nominal length leaves a fraction of a step of silence at the end of the pass.
- **Measured** (baked, dumped to WAV, analysed, dump removed): peak **0.826–0.830** with **zero clipped samples** and a DC offset of 0.001 — it read 0.83 for the mono bake, 0.836–0.856 after the stereo one (#119), and #186's floor took it back down. Earlier, on the fixed-tempo version, the low-band envelope per sixteenth read `9542 7442 7442 7442` — a nine on every beat and a four between them, i.e. exactly the four-on-the-floor-against-off-beat-bass the arrangement calls for. Synthesis takes a few seconds per pass, which is why it happens on a background thread rather than during load.
- The track stops **dead** when a level is cleared, so the fireworks' reports land in silence — a bang competing with a four-to-the-floor kick is a bang nobody hears, and the sudden quiet is itself part of winning. It stops on a loss too: a dance track carrying on over a result screen that says the player ran out of balls is the wrong feeling entirely. It is authored as Level One's theme and is still exactly that — level one plays it, and so does every fourth entry after it (see "The rotation").

**Pulse was reworked in #186**, which reported it as primitive, aggressive and aimed at small children, and asked for dignity, a "summer breeze" quality, real bass and a rare climactic shock. Three of those are arrangement and one is a voice, and the measurement disagreed with the report on exactly one point — which is the useful part.

- **The dynamics were already real; the rhythmic density was what was uniform.** The report read the `Level` column (0.62 to 1.00, about 4 dB) as the piece's dynamic range, and it is not — measured, the nine sections spanned 0.067 to 0.222, i.e. **10.4 dB**, because what a section *has in it* sets its level and the dial only trims (the lesson Bohemia's own figures record twice). What was genuinely uniform is what the report heard: the kick played in **eight sections of nine** — every one but the breakdown — and the sixteenth arpeggio in all nine, so the track ran at full rhythmic density for almost its whole length whatever its levels did. The fix is therefore air and not a dial — and the levels moved anyway, to **13.1 dB**, because taking parts away is what moves them.
- **The floor is a new voice, and deliberately not a change to the old one.** `Bass` is a **pluck** — `Exp(-t * 6.5f)`, gone in about 150 ms — so the piece had a pump and no bottom. Re-enveloping it, which is the obvious fix, would have re-voiced **three other pieces**: Bohemia, the lobby loop and the victory fanfare all play that same voice. `SubBass` is a separate sustained note, one a chord, an octave under the root, held **past the bar line** so one chord's floor is still releasing while the next one's speaks (ended *on* the line it leaves a hole every bar, measured at a quarter of the level in the sections with no drums to cover it — the bass audibly pulsing once a bar).
- **The pump stays an octave up, and that is the whole design.** The report's sketch proposed writing the recurring off-beat hit at the true root instead of `+12`; that would have doubled the new floor and turned both to mud. A floor *under* a pump is how the genre is actually arranged — the pump is a mid-bass rhythm part and the sub is the bottom, and they are two jobs rather than one part in the wrong octave.
- **It ducks to the beat, and the duck is the fix rather than an effect.** A sustained sine in the kick's own octave meets it at whatever phase it happens to and either doubles or cancels — the classic way a mix loses its low end with every part individually correct. Ducking 55 % on each beat and recovering over ~110 ms is both the cure and the pump the ear expects. Where no kick plays it holds dead flat, which is what gives the prelude, the breeze and the outro a bottom.
- **A sine is the correct sub and is silent on the hardware this is played on.** The fundamental here runs 37–82 Hz, which a laptop or a phone reproduces not at all; a second harmonic at 0.24 and a third at 0.08 are audible on those speakers and the ear reconstructs the missing fundamental from them. The ladder is audibility, not colour.
- **Two sections carry no kit at all, and they carry a second tune instead.** A dance track with one tune has only volume to make a quiet section out of. `BREEZE` is a four-bar sentence on the **keys** — state, the same cell a step higher, an answer that falls, a held note to rest on, the shape Bohemia's theme proved — sitting an octave under the chorus and moving by step where the chorus leaps. It opens the piece (a **prelude** with no drums in it), it is what the breakdown plays instead of hats, and it walks the **outro** off, so the ear meets it three times and the outro ends rather than fading with a kick still going. The kick now plays in six sections of ten and half of a seventh.
- **The shock is one event in one place: a stop, and one chord from outside the piece.** The build's last bar cuts dead three beats early — measured, **six sixteenths of literal digital silence** — and what lands in the hole, off the beat, is **E major**: the harmonic-minor dominant, whose G♯ is the one note two minutes of strictly diatonic A minor has not played. It resolves onto the Am the final chorus opens on, guaranteed because *every* progression in the pool starts there, so the surprise is a cadence and not a wrong note. It is scored for **brass**, the one voice this piece never otherwise uses: a part heard once is an event by construction, where the same chord on the lead would just be the piece getting louder. This is what the report meant by rare — the per-bar `Embellish` and `Ghost` rolls fire in nearly every bar and are therefore texture.
- **Measured, before and after, one harness, three seeds.** The low band per sixteenth through a verse bar went from `2727 1337 0894 0445 | 2754 1263 0881 0354` — the bottom *gone* for the last sixteenth of every beat, a 7.7:1 fall — to `2739 1547 1283 1100 | 2872 1405 1326 1092`, a floor that never drops below a third of the beat while the beat itself still stands 8 dB proud of it. Through the breakdown the same row went from `0082 0122 0230 0261 …` to `0984 0955 0961 0961 …`, i.e. from no low end at all to a flat one. The build's last bar reads `3062 1716 1814 0916 0000 0000 0000 0000 0000 0000 2388 2530 2589 2578 2569 1620` — the stop and the stab, in one row.
- **And the figures that must not move did not.** 148.7–155.4 s at 123.5–129.1 BPM, RMS **0.1791–0.1792** against the set's 0.171–0.182, so no level step between one level and the next; peak **0.826–0.830** with **zero clipped samples**, DC 0.0010–0.0011, channel balance within **0.11 dB**, mono compatibility −0.10 to −0.11 dB. Two did move and both are the change: energy below 200 Hz went **63 % → 66–70 %** (Bohemia measures 71 % on the same instrument), and side/mid above 200 Hz **0.295 → 0.27**, because the two new sections have no ping-ponging arp in them and the added energy is centred by the low-end rule. A pass costs about **4 %** more to render for 11 % more steps — the sections that were added are the cheapest in the piece.

**The second theme: Bohemia (#120).** A level set with one piece in it is one piece heard all evening, transposed — the variation is real but it is variation over a single composition, one `ARRANGEMENT`, one hook, one chord pool. `BakeBohemia` is a second work by the same hand: **D Dorian, twelve eight-bar sections, ~3:20 a pass**, with a string section, brass pillars and a tuned drum over the dance floor Pulse built. It shares this file's instruments, the chord-tone melodies and the limiter, and nothing else — its own mode, its own tunes, its own form.

- **A small curated pool, not a generative composer.** Both pieces are hand-arranged by ear against measurements, and that craft is exactly what composing from scratch at runtime would throw away. The rule the file already lived by — random parameters, never random notes — is what makes two hand-written pieces enough: each still rolls its own tempo, key, progression and embellishment per pass.
- **The mode is the whole reason a second pool exists.** Pulse is A natural minor, whose colour is the flat sixth. Dorian *raises* that sixth — here the B natural inside the G major chord — and one chord is the difference between "minor" and "modal". It is the major chord a step below the relative major, i.e. the one nobody expects, and it is what makes the piece read as folk rather than as the same track in another key. The roots are voiced so the cadence **B♭ → C → Dm walks up by whole steps in the bass** (46, 48, 50) rather than sitting at whatever octave was nearest. Three of the five progressions end on C, the flat seventh, which resolves up a whole step onto the Dm at the top of the round — so a four-bar loop *cadences* instead of merely restarting.
- **The tune is a four-bar sentence, which is what Pulse's chorus is not.** Statement, then the same rhythmic cell a step higher (a sequence — the oldest trick there is for making a tune sound inevitable), then an answer that falls, then the climax an octave up. Long notes and few of them: this is the one meant to be left humming. Pulse's hook is one motif *transposed across the chords*, which is a different kind of good and a worse kind of memorable.
- **There is a second subject, and a piece with an arch needs one.** Everything about it is the theme's opposite — stepwise where the theme leaps, two notes to a bar against three, and carried by the keys under strings rather than by the lead. It arrives where a dance track would put a breakdown, which is the point: **a different tune is a far better reason to take the drums away than a gap is.** It comes back in the **coda over the full floor**, the quiet middle tune made the big one, and that return is what the twelve sections exist to reach.
- **The turnaround rises where Pulse's falls.** A descending fill hands a phrase on; an ascending one throws it, which is what a piece with somewhere to get to wants.
- **The floor stays underneath it rather than being replaced.** A purely orchestral piece would be a better piece of music and a worse piece of *this game*: the player changing level should hear a second work by the same hand, not a second soundtrack.
- **Measured across three seeds** (baked through the private baker and analysed, no playback path involved): **201.2 / 192.4 / 200.5 s** (3:12 – 3:21) at **114.5 / 119.7 / 114.9 BPM**, three completely different SHA1s, and an RMS of **0.1763 / 0.1767 / 0.1767** — the limiter sets the loudness here exactly as it does for Pulse, and at the same level, so switching pieces mid-set is not a jump in volume. Peak **0.891 – 0.911** with **zero clipped samples**, DC offset 0.0013 – 0.0015. The handover is Pulse's: the last two seconds of a pass sit at an RMS of **0.0012 – 0.0014**, about −58 dB, so the join between passes lands in silence. **68 – 70 % of the energy is below 200 Hz**, which is the timpani and the brass, and is the measurement that says the floor really did stay under the orchestra. It was recorded here against Pulse's 61 – 64 %, and that contrast is gone: #186 put a sustained floor under Pulse and took it to 66 – 70 %, so this figure now says what the piece has rather than how it differs from the other one.
- **Per section, full-band RMS** (seed to seed within 0.008, so the arrangement and not the roll decides these): intro 0.068, statement 0.136, verse 0.191, chorus 0.223, **second subject 0.064**, verse 0.191, chorus 0.223, development 0.105, **build 0.248**, climax 0.226, coda 0.217, outro 0.062. Two things in that row are worth keeping. The **second subject sits at a third of the verse** — the ratio Pulse's fixed breakdown sat at too, which is the level an *empty* section wants regardless of why it is empty. Pulse's is no longer empty: since #186 the same slot keeps a sustained floor and a tune and measures at **half** the verse, which is what a section that is quiet rather than absent costs. And the **build is the loudest thing in the piece**, above the climax it points at; that is the snare roll's density rather than a level dial, and it is the one figure here that a measurement cannot settle — it wants an ear.
- **The authored `Level` dial is not what sets the audible level, and the row above proves it again.** Intro, second subject and outro carry 0.60, 0.82 and 0.70 in the table and all three measure at ~0.065: what a section *has in it* decides how loud it is, and the dial only trims. This is the same lesson the breakdown taught Pulse, and it is why every section here was measured rather than assumed.
- **A pass in hand per composition, and getting that wrong is what a second piece cost.** The sounding pass is still dropped when the piece changes — `Advance`'s fallback of replaying what is loaded is right mid-level and wrong at a boundary, where it would play the previous level's music for its whole length. But the *baking* pass must not be: with one slot shared by both pieces, a set that alternates threw away the pass it held at **every** level boundary and paid a full bake in silence. Measured on the development desktop, a Bohemia pass takes **12.6 s** to render against Pulse's **2.7 s** — its string section is seven detuned oscillators with their own vibrato per note where Pulse's heaviest voice is two — so every Bohemia level opened on 12.6 s of nothing, and proportionally worse on a weaker machine. A slot each, both primed at construction, makes every boundary after the first **instant** (measured 0.00 s); the cost is one more pending buffer, about 35 MB. This was reported as "no music at all on level two", which is what a hole that long reads as.
- **The piece is chosen before the music is started, not after.** `BuildLevel` called `Play()` ahead of `InstallLevel`, and `SetTheme` lives inside the install — so a level boundary put a pass of the **outgoing** piece on and stopped it a moment later. Measured, the theme was sounding 0.05 s into a level and it was the wrong one: an alternating set opened every level on a fragment of the previous level's music, and spent the pass it had in hand to do it. `Play()` moved below the install, which is the general rule the ordering states — decide what to play before playing.

**The third theme: Nocturne (#162).** Smooth jazz — a trio playing sevenths on a swung grid, nine sections, ~3:00 a pass. Where Pulse and Bohemia differ in **mode over one shared dance floor**, this one differs in **harmony and in time**, which is what makes it a third language rather than a third accent.

- **The genre's identity is chords and placement, both of which are tables** — which is why a whole third piece cost almost no new synthesis. Every chord is a **four-note seventh** where the other two use triads-plus-octave (the seventh is not a decoration here, it is the chord), the five progressions are all built on **ii–V motion**, and it is in plain C major on purpose: the colour comes from the extensions, and a piece whose whole point is its harmony should not also be fighting a key. Two of the five end on the dominant instead of the tonic, so a four-bar round can circle rather than land.
- **The swing is worth more than any of the chord tables.** The same notes on a straight grid read as a ballad. Only the **off-beat eighths** move — steps 2, 6, 10, 14 — and the sixteenths between them are left alone, because swinging those makes a shuffle, and the walking bass on the beat must not move at all or the time itself wobbles. `SWING` is 0.62 rather than a mechanical 2:1 triplet, which reads as a shuffle.
- **The walking bass is written as an algorithm, not as a figure**, because that motion *is* the backbone: beat one is always the root so the harmony is never in doubt, beats two and three take chord tones in the voicing's own spacing, and beat four is the **chromatic approach a semitone under the next chord's root** — the one note that makes a walk sound inevitable rather than merely busy.
- **One new voice, and the reason is the attack rather than the pitch.** `Bass` is a saw held open through a filter that sweeps *with* the note — a dance bass, and what it does is sustain. `UprightBass` is plucked: a **triangle** (nearly all fundamental with odd harmonics falling off as 1/n², the string's own spectrum) under a filter that **closes** as the note decays, over a few milliseconds of finger noise. A bass with no finger noise reads as a sine rather than as a player. Everything else — the Rhodes, the ride, the brushes — is a voice the game already had; the tune is on `Keys` because that voice *is* the mellow lead this genre wants and it was already here for the lobby's line.
- **Two things measured wrong first, both recorded because they look right.** At its authored `targetRms` of 0.16 the piece came out **2.0 dB quieter** than the other two — and a piece that is quieter by *level* rather than by arrangement puts a volume drop between two levels of one set, which is the thing Bohemia's own figures were matched to avoid. It takes Pulse's 0.20 now, and the intimacy comes from what is playing. And the piece leaned **+1.9 dB left**, because `Keys` carries *both* the comp and the tune here and both sat on the keys' own seat: the soloist is centred now and only the accompanist moves, with the comp's four notes seated across the field the way two hands are. That is #119's own lesson — a part carrying a piece alone belongs in the middle — arriving a second time.
- **Measured, three seeds, through the private baker:** **178.0 / 185.6 / 189.5 s** at RMS **0.1718–0.1723** against Pulse's 0.1778 and Bohemia's 0.1766, so switching pieces mid-set is not a jump in volume; peak **0.918–0.925** with **zero clipped samples**, DC **0.0004** (the lowest of the three), channel balance **+0.01 to +0.11 dB** (the best of the three), correlation 0.93 and side/mid 0.185, i.e. the same stereo width as Pulse.

**The fourth theme: Dechovka (#164).** A Moravian brass band — oom-pah, clarinets in thirds, nine sections, ~2:20 a pass, in B flat.

- **The oom-pah is a division of labour, not a rhythm.** The tuba takes every beat alternating **root and fifth** (a part that repeated the root would sit still; the fifth is what makes the bass walk in place), and the mid brass answers on every off-beat eighth with the chord. Neither ever plays where the other does, and both are clipped short — the *gap* between them is the texture, and a tuba held through the off-beat turns the whole thing to porridge. It is why this piece feels like it is marching without needing a drum part to say so.
- **The trio modulates to the subdominant, and that is what makes it a fourth piece rather than a fourth chord pool.** A polka states its strains and then goes a fourth up and *softer* — the moment the band drops its volume and the clarinet takes over is the most recognisable event in the idiom. It costs one transpose applied to a flag, and it rides on the pass's own roll, so the modulation is a property of the **form** and the key a property of the roll; neither has to know about the other. Plain triads with a seventh only on the dominant: polka harmony is not where its interest lives, and dressing it in Nocturne's extensions would make it a different piece wearing a hat.
- **One new voice, and it is acoustics rather than taste.** A clarinet is a cylindrical pipe stopped at one end, so it resonates **odd harmonics only** — which is a square wave, the same oscillator the DOS-era `Arp` uses, put to the opposite purpose. It sustains (a reed is blown, not struck), carries a fifth of a second of breath across the attack, and its filter opens only slightly with the envelope, because a clarinet gets louder without getting much brighter. The **tuba is `Brass` played low**, which is what a tuba is.
- **Four attempts at its stereo image, and the three that failed are the useful part.** Seating the "pah" chord's notes symmetrically bought almost nothing — *four simultaneous voices placed evenly about the centre sum back to the centre*. Answering it antiphonally across the stand moved it from 0.029 to 0.031, because `Brass` anchors its own sub to the middle whatever its pan says. Centring the clarinet fixed a 1 dB lean and took the whole band to **0.03 side/mid, i.e. mono**. What worked was scoring the tune for **two clarinets in thirds**, which is the idiom's own scoring anyway: two genuinely *different* signals, which is #119's rule — width comes from different signals, never from one signal at two levels — and it took the piece to 0.29, the widest of the four. The desks are **not** seated symmetrically: the seconds play under the firsts, so equal distances leaned it 0.72 dB, and putting the firsts nearer the middle is both what balances it and how a band is actually seated.
- **Measured, three seeds:** **138.6 / 143.2 / 145.4 s** at RMS **0.1821–0.1823** (the set runs 0.172–0.182, so no entry is a volume step from its neighbour), peak **0.730–0.744** with **zero clipped samples**, DC **0.0005**, channel balance **−0.04 to −0.06 dB**, correlation 0.84 and side/mid **0.290**. Mono costs 0.35 dB and cancels nothing.

**The rotation.** `ProceduralMusic.ThemeFor(named, index)` answers which piece a level plays, and `GameplayScreen`'s install calls it before anything else is put in place — including when the level fails to load, since the fallback map is still level `index` and should sound like it. With nothing named, **the level's position in the set picks the piece**, alternating: level one is Pulse (the piece it was written for), level two is Bohemia, level three Nocturne, level four Dechovka, and so on, so a set has variety without a single file having to say anything. A level may pin one with a `"music"` field (see "Levels, scene config and serialization" in `docs/formats-and-tools.md`); it is matched case- and whitespace-insensitively, and an **unknown spelling falls back to the rotation rather than throwing**, for the reason the scene names do — it is a hand-editable file. A negative index is folded back into range rather than indexing off the end.

**The victory fanfare was tightened in #185**, which reported it as forced rather than punchy — and read against the report that produced it in the first place ("old, cheap, nothing to dance to", of a lone trombone), the two together **bound the target between bare and busy** rather than cancelling out. So the eurodance drop stays and everything round it is cut: **three drop bars instead of four**, the extra build bar reserved for a genuinely big win (intensity 0.75 rather than 0.35 — at 0.35 nearly every non-trivial clear got it), the pad's sustain down from 15.5/22 steps to 10/14 and **struck rather than swelled** (a 0.12 s attack against the shared voice's 0.35, which is a parameter now because all four pieces share that voice and only this one wants the hit), the close's lead hold 14 → 9, the sparkle run 12 steps → 8, and the layer thresholds spread from 0.2/0.35/0.45/0.5 out to 0.3/0.5/0.7/0.8 — at the old spacing seven simultaneous voices arrived by a *below-average* score, so the fully stacked version was the ordinary case rather than the exceptional one.

- **The shortened drop plays I–vi–IV, not the first three of I–V–vi–IV.** Taking bars 0-1-2 would drop the IV — the one chord the vi exists to set up, and the lift the whole progression is written for. Home, the minor turn, the lift.
- **The two fanfares need different room past their last bar, and one constant for both is a constant that fits neither.** Cutting the shared tail to 8 for the victory truncated the *defeat's* closing pad, which is held 26 steps from the top of its last bar: measured, its final five milliseconds sat at **37–39 % of the piece's own peak** — cut off mid-note, a click, the exact fault the constant exists to prevent. `VICTORY_TAIL_STEPS` is 8 and `DEFEAT_TAIL_STEPS` 14; both pieces now end at **0.0 %**.
- **Measured across the intensity range** (seed 1234, so the tempo is held): an ordinary win **10.6 → 8.1 s** and a big one **12.3 → 9.9 s**, i.e. about a quarter off each, with zero clipped samples throughout and RMS climbing 0.165 → 0.202 with the score, which is the layering still doing its job. The defeat was left untouched at 17.5 s — long, and worth its own look, which it got as #190 below.

**The defeat fanfare was shortened in #190**, which is #185's finding followed up rather than a new complaint: the defeat ran **four bars whatever had happened**, and at 62–74 BPM that measured 17.5 s over a screen that had just told the player they lost — nearly twice the victory's own length once #185 had shortened that. Only the bars could pay for it. The **tempo is the piece's whole character** ("it has to feel like it is running out", and half the theme's tempo is what does that), and the **tail cannot be touched** — the closing pad is held 26 steps from the top of the last bar, and #185 measured what a shorter one costs: the final five milliseconds at 37–39 % of the peak, i.e. a click. So the length became the intensity's, like the density already was: **three bars for a loss, four only above `DEFEAT_FULLER`** — the very threshold that already put the harmony under the melody, so "the run deserved better" grows the piece in one step rather than by two figures that could drift apart.

- **The third bar is the one that goes, chord and melody note together** — a three-bar **i–VI–i**. The iv is a step on the way home, where the VI before it is the sag the whole piece is written round and the i after it is the landing, so the short version still sags and still lands, and the melody still falls (3-2-0 or 2-1-0 against the four-bar 3-2-1-0). Dropping the VI instead would leave a plain plagal cadence with none of the piece's colour. It is #185's own rule for the victory's I–V–vi–IV applied to a falling progression: keep the chord the progression exists for. The four-bar arrays stay the canonical statement and a bar index is mapped onto them, rather than a second set being authored per length.
- **Measured, through the same private baker** (no playback path; it reproduced #185's figures exactly before anything was changed, which is what says the two measurements are comparable): the ordinary loss **17.52 → 13.92 s** at seed 1234, and the near miss deliberately **unchanged at 17.52**. Across eight spread seeds the three-bar piece runs **13.2–15.0 s** and the four-bar **16.7–18.9 s**, the spread being the rolled tempo. **RMS does not move at all** (0.0933 → 0.0933 at intensity 0.1, 0.1321 → 0.1321 at 1.0) because `Limit` normalizes each bake to a target; zero clipped samples throughout, and the last five milliseconds still sit at **0.0 % of peak**, so the tail arithmetic is independent of the bar count as it should be. The only figure that moved is the **peak**, 0.397 → 0.415 at the low end: the same RMS driven into a shorter buffer puts the crests a touch higher, and at a 0.9 ceiling that is headroom nobody hears.
- **Not claimed: that this is the right length.** The issue said as much when it was filed — it is a call on feel, and the number was simply large enough to be worth an ear. What is claimed is that the tempo, the tail, the mode, the fall, the density thresholds and the loudness are all untouched, so what is left to judge is the length alone. If it wants to be shorter still, the next honest step is a two-bar version for a poor run — but at the current gate the last melody note is withheld below 0.35, so two bars would leave a single trombone note in ten seconds, which is the "dismissive" failure the issue warns about rather than a shorter piece.
- **A caution for anyone re-measuring it:** three hand-picked small seeds (1234, 7, 99) all rolled the **same key** and a tempo inside 0.8 BPM of each other — `Random(int)`'s first draws are correlated for nearby small seeds, and the game's own seed comes from `_seeds.Next()` rather than from anything like that. Across eight spread seeds all four roots (E2/F2/G2/A2) and the whole 62–70 BPM band appear. Held seeds are right for comparing two builds and wrong for asking what the piece's variety is.

**The fanfares.** Into that silence goes a piece announcing the result — `PlayVictory`/`PlayDefeat`, in the same file because they are played by the same instruments. Both are generated like the track: baked on a background thread (this fires on the exact frame a level ends, which is also the frame the camera is released, the fireworks start and the result screen is built — the last moment to spend on synthesis), rolled fresh each time, and on their **own** `SoundEffectInstance` so `Stop()` silencing the theme does not cut off the piece announcing the outcome.

- **The victory is a eurodance DROP now, not a herald's call** — the first version was a lone trombone rising over a pad, and it was reported as exactly that: dignified, old, cheap, nothing to dance to. The game's musical language is the theme's eurodance, and in that language a celebration is the drop: a snare-roll **build** with a climbing arpeggio ladder in front (once the win is worth announcing), then four bars of **I–V–vi–IV** — the most euphoric progression pop owns, its one minor bar making the IV lift after it — with a four-on-the-floor kick and the off-beat bass pump running under a **supersaw hook in the 3-3-2 tresillo** (hits on 0, 3, 6 / 8, 11, 14 — the most danceable eight counts there are), closing on the held tonic with the sparkle run climbing away.
- **They scale with the score, and the scaling changes how much of the floor is moving, never the tune.** `intensity` is the score against `FANFARE_FULL_SCORE` (6000 — there is no natural ceiling to a score, so the reference is a stated constant, and it is the one dial to turn if the fanfares stop matching how the player feels). **The kick, the bass and the hook are always there** — the beat is the celebration now, not a garnish a modest win goes without; past 0.2 the claps arrive, past 0.35 the hats and the build bar, past 0.45 the sixteenth arpeggio, past 0.5 the hook's octave doubling, past 0.6 the sparkle run, past 0.75 the higher held finish, past 0.8 a tom run into it. Measured across 0.1 → 1.0: RMS 0.160 → 0.203, energy below 200 Hz at **59–61 % throughout** (the old build's low band only *arrived* with a good score — now the floor is under every win), length 10.6 → 12.4 s. The player hears what kind of win it was before the result screen has said a word.
- **Defeat is the inverse in every dimension that matters** — mode, direction, tempo and register: minor rather than major, falling rather than rising, ~62–74 BPM rather than ~134–150, and low rather than high. It has **no percussion at all**, because a beat would give it momentum and momentum is the one thing it must not have. It takes the same intensity from the other end: a good score that still lost gets a harmony under the melody and a resolution at the bottom, while a poor one **does not play its last note at all** and simply stops, unresolved — the bleakest thing in the piece, and it costs one condition. Measured 0.1 → 1.0: RMS 0.093 → 0.132, and note onsets *falling* 22 → 12, because the fuller pad holds the sound continuously instead of leaving separate attacks.
- **The closing chord needs room to ring out past the last bar** (`FANFARE_TAIL_STEPS`). Both pieces hold their final chord for well over a bar, and without the tail the buffer simply ends mid-sustain and the fanfare finishes on a **click** — a poor way to be told anything. Verified: the last 150 ms of all six measured variants peak at exactly 0.0000, so they decay to silence rather than being cut.
- **The DEFEAT is played by a TROMBONE, not by the track's synth lead** (`Brass`), and four things make it one. The load-bearing one is a **filter envelope that opens with the note**: brass gets brighter as it gets louder, so the cutoff sweeps from a muffled 550 Hz to wide open over the attack and settles back as the note holds. A fixed filter over the same oscillators is a synth pad; that one line is most of the difference. The rest is a **slow speech** (~55 ms of attack with a touch of breath noise across it — a trombone does not start instantly), a **sub an octave down** which is the whole of the depth, and **two filter poles rather than one**, since 6 dB an octave leaves the top end hissing through and that is exactly what stops a saw sounding like an instrument. The victory used the trombone too, once — that is the version that read as a parade ground — and plays the theme's own supersaw `Lead` now: the instrument the player has been dancing to is the instrument that congratulates them.
- **Register belongs to the instrument.** Up at C4–G4 the *trombone* fanfare was a whistle with a chord under it, and dropping it an octave was the fix — a lesson that stays true for the defeat (E2–A2, 73–86 % of its energy under 200 Hz). The supersaw victory goes back **up** (roots A3–D4, the hook an octave over them), because the theme's own chorus already proves that voice carries that register over a full mix — it was the trombone that could not.
- **Legato, and the arithmetic of it is the fix.** A melody whose notes die before the next begins arrives as a row of separate events — it read as choppy. Every line here overlaps: the victory's hook notes run a step past the next hit's start, and the defeat's seventeen against a sixteen-step gap (its holes are bigger, being slower), so the voice releases across the join and each phrase is one gesture.
- **The fireworks duck under the fanfare** (`ProceduralAudio.FireworkDuck`, to `FIREWORK_DUCKED` 0.35). Both arrive on the frame a level ends, and a report is broadband and loud enough to bury a tune under it — the bang is an event, the fanfare is the point. It is a gain on the *next* play rather than on the ones already sounding, and the reason for that changed with #75 even though the behaviour did not: the burst voices now *do* have handles that could be turned down mid-flight, but walking 32 of them every frame to write a volume that is almost always the same one is the per-frame work `BestPractices.md` forbids, for a difference that lasts as long as one report. The host reads `IsFanfarePlaying` per frame rather than latching it, so the ducking lifts by itself when the piece ends.

## The victory fireworks (Game)

Clearing a level sets off a display: shells rise from a ring around the island, whistle, burst over the arena in colour and rain down, with the report arriving as each one goes off. `Game/Effects/Fireworks.cs` owns it and `Testbed/Content/Shaders/Fireworks.fx` draws it; the sounds are three more procedural bakes (see "The sound").

- **It lives on the HOST, not on the gameplay screen, and that is what makes it work at all.** A cleared level pushes the result page *over* the session, and a covered screen is not updated (see "The screen stack" in `docs/game-shell.md`) — so a celebration owned by the session would freeze at the exact moment the player is meant to be watching it. Owned by the frame it keeps running whatever is on the stack, exactly as the clouds and the wall clock do, which is why `BS3DGame.Update` advances it above `_screens.Update` and `FinishSceneDraw` draws it. It is started from `CheckLevelCleared` (not from the result screen), so the first shells are already climbing while the last of the cluster is still falling — the celebration overlaps the moment it is celebrating instead of following it — and `BuildLevel` stops it, or the next level opens with the last one's fireworks still bursting over it.
- **One static vertex buffer and ONE draw call for the whole display**, the idiom `Snow.fx` and `Spray.fx` established: every shell and every spark is animated in the vertex shader from a handful of per-shell uniforms (`ShellOrigin`/`ShellBurst`/`ShellColor`/`ShellShape`, `MAX_SHELLS` = 32), so nothing is rebuilt or re-uploaded per frame. The C# side owns only what the shader cannot know — when a shell goes up, where it bursts, what colour it is and when to make a noise. A dead slot is `ShellColor.a == 0`, which collapses its quads to a degenerate triangle: an idle display costs the vertex work of the buffer and **not one pixel**.
- **The same buffer is the rocket on the way up and the burst on the way down.** While the age is negative the sparks are strung along the flight path by their trail rank, which draws the rising comet and its tail for free; at zero they are released. A spark's whole path is a **pure function of its age**, which is precisely what lets the buffer be static — and why the burst directions are baked in at build time rather than hashed in the shader, since a direction that changed between frames would make every spark jitter.
- **It waits for the fanfare, and it outlasts the result screen.** The display hangs back `CELEBRATION_DELAY` (2.2 s) before its first shell, so the victory fanfare gets its opening statement to itself — the level ends, brass announces it, and only then does the sky start going off; everything at once is nothing heard. And it runs for a **minute**, because it is meant to still be going while the player reads their score and the released camera swings around the arena. At nine seconds it ran out while the result screen was still being read, and a celebration that stops before its audience does reads as the game losing interest. `TearDown` stops it, or a win followed by "Main Menu" would go on bursting over the front end.
- **The brief is a sky FULL of them.** 32 concurrent shells, a launch every 0.17 s once the opening barrage settles (0.07 s during it), bursting between 44 and 122 up and spread right across the sky rather than clustered over the arena — which puts about twenty shells in the air at any moment. Past `RELAXED_AFTER` (16 s) it **eases off** to a shell every half-second: a full minute at the barrage's density is exhausting rather than celebratory, and the point of the later stretch is to keep the sky busy without shouting. Verified alive and varied at 4, 12, 25 and 45 seconds in.
- **The party popper fires when the display STARTS, not when the celebration was asked for** — it is the fireworks' own opening crack, announcing them rather than the win, which the fanfare has already done. It is flagged rather than hung off the delay counting down to zero, because at a delay of nothing that branch never runs at all and the `celebrate` test argument asks for exactly that case. The ceiling on `MAX_SHELLS × SPARKS_PER_SHELL` is the **16-bit index buffer**: times four vertices it must stay under 65 536, so raising either without checking that is how far triangles quietly start referencing the wrong vertices (the lesson `CreateGridMesh`'s grids taught, from the other direction). At 32 × 200 it is 25 600.
- **Measured cost: nothing worth a number.** City, in a level, 3840×1600 fullscreen, `quality=high`: 7.66 ms with no display, 7.35 with the first (14-shell) version and **7.63 with the shipping 32-shell one** — all three inside the run-to-run spread, and the worst second stays above 100 FPS. Space measures 5.77 ms. One additive draw call is simply not what this frame is made of.

Three things that were wrong first, all worth keeping:

- **A burst has to be BIG and DENSE, and the arithmetic is not obvious.** At 120 sparks the display read as three or four lonely glints: a burst subtends about 19° from the play camera, so a hundred-odd sparks spread over that disc land a couple of hundred pixels apart. It ships at **320 sparks** and a **1.55-unit** spark — the size is in *world* units and a burst is 40–90 units up, so it has to be far larger than it sounds. Spark count and spark size both have to scale with the burst radius, or a bigger shell looks *emptier* than a small one, which is exactly backwards.
- **A SPARK IS A LINE, NOT A DOT, and this is the single thing that makes it read as an explosion.** A burning star crossing the sky faster than the eye or a shutter can resolve is seen as a streak, and it is those streaks radiating from a point that say *something is blowing apart*; a cloud of round dots, however many and however bright, only ever reads as a swarm. The billboard is stretched along the spark's own **screen-projected velocity** (`SparkStretch`), which falls out in closed form because the position already is — `d(reach)/dt = (1 − INITIAL_SPREAD)·DRAG·e^(−DRAG·t)`, fastest at the flash and gone within a few tenths of a second. So the shot is a *line*, then a break-up, then a drift, without a stage or a state machine anywhere. Projecting the velocity is what keeps a spark coming straight at the lens round instead of stretched along an arbitrary axis, and the pixel shader tapers the streak's brightness towards its leading end so it reads as a comet rather than as a stick.
  - It also disposes of the flash problem the first version had. Every spark being coincident on the burst frame used to stack additively into one blown-out sample that the glare pass drew as its six-armed star — a burst that read as a flaw in the lens. `INITIAL_SPREAD` was raised to 0.12 of the radius to fix that, and it introduced a worse fault: it *pre-arranged* the shell into a formed sphere that then inflated rigidly, which is a bottle brush on a wire and not an explosion. It is 0.015 now, and the streak spreads the flash's energy instead — on that frame every spark is nearly coincident but moving at its fastest, so each is drawn at its longest.
- **A Fibonacci sphere is TOO regular for this.** Even coverage is right for placing sparks, but expanded uniformly the lattice holds its pattern and the eye picks the spiral out as a rigid structure inflating. A quarter-radian of scatter on each direction keeps the coverage and destroys the lattice; and the speed spread was widened hard (`0.34 + 0.66·rand^0.6`, from a tight bunch near the full radius), because a shell whose sparks all reach the same distance stays a clean hollow surface — real, but not a thing coming apart.
- **Two colours per shell, split per spark** (`ShellColorB`, chosen a random step round the palette so it is never the same one twice). A real shell is one chemistry and one colour; a *display* is not, and a burst that comes out half magenta and half gold reads as far more of an event than either alone. The split is hard rather than a blend, so the two are seen as two.
- **The hot core is mostly WHITE**, for the reason the cluster's ripple whitens: these are driven to about 4 in linear radiance, far over `GLARE_THRESHOLD`, and a saturated hue at that level just clips one channel and reads as a flat coloured blob. A spark is white at its brightest and only shows its own colour as it cools, which is what makes it read as *burning*.

`celebrate` on the command line fires the display on the front end. Clearing a level is the only thing that normally starts it and clearing one cannot be scripted, so without it the fireworks can be neither screenshotted nor measured — the same reason `autoshoot` and `aimshoot` exist.

## The end of a level goes out of focus (Game, #178)

A level's ending used to be read through smoked glass. The result page dimmed the whole frame at `PAUSE_SCRIM`'s weight (alpha 176) on the pause screen's argument — a page over a stopped game — and that argument does not survive being looked at: a pause is a game *put down*, where this is the game's own ending playing out. The fireworks are climbing, the camera is letting go of the gun and swinging out around the island, the last of the cluster is still running down the drain, and all of it was behind a scrim at the one moment it was worth watching. So **`ResultPage.DimsFrame` is false**, and what holds the page's numbers legible over a lit, moving arena instead is the frame **going out of focus** a few seconds in.

- **The ending is watched first and softened afterwards, and the delay is what makes it an effect rather than a transition.** `BLUR_DELAY_SECONDS` (3.4) sits past both things this page does on arrival — the camera's release (`ORBIT_EASE_SECONDS`, 2.5) and the last star landing (`RevealTotalSeconds`, about 2) — so nothing is blurred while it is still arriving. Then `BLUR_EASE_SECONDS` (4) of smoothstep, whose rate is zero at *both* ends: a linear ramp starts with a visible lurch out of a still image, and the eye reads the moment a movement stops as sharply as the moment it starts.
- **It is driven from the page, like the camera release and the star reveal, and for the same reason** — the session under it is covered and therefore frozen, so the result page is the only screen still updating. `MenuPage.FrameBlur` is the whole interface (zero for every other page), the host reads it off the **active** screen at resolve time, and it is a pure function of one clock the page's `Update` advances — so nothing can write it from two places, and a retry that lands back here gets its own few seconds in focus.
- **The blur happens in LINEAR RADIANCE, before the tonemap curve, and that is the whole reason it is worth having.** `PostProcessPipeline` builds a blurred copy of the very HDR target the resolve is about to read and `Tonemap.fx` mixes it in ahead of the glare add (see "Color management" in `docs/rendering.md` for the stage itself). Blurring the *finished* picture instead would average tonemapped pixels: a firework spark sits clipped near white, so spreading it into the dark around it gives a grey smudge. Spread in linear light its energy goes with it and it stays a glowing orb — and the glare, added afterwards from the sharp scene, keeps every bright thing blooming through a frame that has gone soft.
- **A pause blurs too, and it starts at once.** The result screen's delay exists because its ending is worth watching; a pause has nothing arriving — the game stopped mid-move and the menu *is* the subject from the first frame — so `PausePage` ramps from zero over `BLUR_SECONDS` (0.35) and no further. It is still a ramp and not a cut, for the reason the title card fades rather than cutting in. The pause **keeps its scrim** on top of the blur: what is behind it is a stopped game rather than an ending playing out, and #178 explicitly allows the darkening to stay where it is the blur doing the work. Its clock is the wall clock rather than its own elapsed time, and that is not a style choice — see the `FrameBlur` bullet in `docs/game-shell.md`. Resume takes the blur off **at once** rather than easing it out, and the reason is structural: the ramp belongs to the page, and by the time the frame is sharp again the page has left the stack, so there is nobody left to ease it.
- **Looked at, not assumed** (`result celebrate` on the command line, which exists so this moment can be seen at all — see "Controls" in `docs/game-session.md`): at 8 % of the ramp the arena is barely soft and shows no ghosting, at 43 % it is plainly out of focus with the shells reading as soft bursts, and at 100 % it is a field of colour and glow. It costs nothing measurable — see the DEFOCUS notes in `docs/rendering.md` for the figures and for how the blur is built.
- **The page's own chrome is untouched by it.** Everything the player reads is drawn after the resolve, in display space — the Myra desktop, the heading, the star row, the breakdown's plate — so it stays sharp over the softened arena. That is also why the in-play HUD had to be gone already for this to work at all, and it is: `GameplayScreen.Draw` stops drawing it the moment the level is decided (see "The in-play HUD"), so there is nothing pinned to the corners of a frame whose camera has been released.

## The drop cinematic (Game)

The reward for a good shot. When one lands and cuts loose more than the player's **biggest drop of this level so far** — by `DropCinematic.MustBeatBestBy` (1.25×), and never fewer than `MIN_BALLS` (12) whatever the history — the camera lets go of the gun and follows the wreckage down: the fall, the run into the glass drain, and — where the scene has anywhere to fall to — the balls pouring out of the hole under the island. `Game/Effects/DropCinematic.cs` owns it; `GameplayScreen` owns the trigger, the subject, the running best (`_biggestDrop`, cleared per level by `BuildLevel`) and the fact that the gun stops answering.

- **The bar is a record and not a count, because a count cannot survive a new level.** The threshold was a flat 6, and on `One.json` that measured as three cinematics in ninety seconds — genuinely rare, because most shots there drop fewer. The pattern pack is built out of large primed groups (Pinwheel drops 92 on a good shot, Crown 72, Bullseye 100), so the same 6 fired on essentially every shot that landed and the reward for a good shot became the tax on every shot. **Raising the number does not fix it**: any figure that keeps Pinwheel rare is one Mosaic can never reach, since Mosaic's best possible shot is 24 balls — and a level that never shows a cinematic is as wrong as one that always does. Nor does authoring a figure per level, which is a dial to get wrong in every future level for an answer the game can work out for itself. "The biggest thing you have done here yet" is what big means, whatever the level is made of.
- **The margin is what makes it rare rather than merely monotonic.** Without it a level whose drops creep upwards fires on every one of them; at 1.25× a record has to be *beaten*, not edged. The first qualifying drop of a level always fires, since there is no record yet — one early cinematic is how the effect introduces itself. Expected frequency across the pack: one on Pinwheel (nothing beats 92 by a quarter), two or three on Bullseye (42 → 80 → 100), three or four on Mosaic as its 8s climb to its 24s.
- **The record is raised by every release, including ones the gates refuse** (a landing during another cinematic, or as the level ends). A collapse the player watched has moved what counts as big here whether or not the camera went with it — and the `[cinematic]` line prints the bar it beat, because "why that one and not the last one" is now a question about the level's history rather than about a constant.

- **It is a pose, a time scale and a blend, not a mode.** The class holds no camera, no simulation and no input: it is handed the subject's position each frame and answers where the lens goes, how fast the world runs and how much of the frame is its. `UpdateCamera` then Lerps between the ordinary gameplay pose and that one — a **second** Lerp stacked on precise aim's, on the same one-reversible-scalar idiom and for the same reason: at a blend of 0 the three lines return the gameplay pose bit for bit, so a cinematic that ends, or is skipped halfway, cannot leave the camera anywhere the game did not put it.
- **The arc is driven by the balls, not by a stopwatch.** Every framing parameter is a three-key curve over how far the subject has fallen, measured against three anchors — the release height, the island's stone and the drain's throat — so the camera cannot run ahead of a slow collapse or lag a fast one. The legs are sequential by construction (each only starts once the one before it has finished), so averaging them gives one monotonic progress and there are no stages to snap between, only weights that move. `MAX_SECONDS` is the only clock, and it exists because a ball the game never culls can still come to rest — rarely now that the stone ring is a dish that rolls a landed ball into the drain (see "The drain funnel" in `docs/scenes.md`), but a pile propping itself up near the mouth still can, and the game only culls at the kill plane (see `RemoveFallenBalls`) — so "the subject is gone" is not a condition that always arrives.
- **Slow motion is applied to the accumulated time, never to the timestep.** `StepPhysics(elapsed * TimeScale)` — the fixed 1/120 s step is untouched, so a slowed world is exactly as stable and as deterministic as a full-speed one. It exists because a released group falls a handful of units under real gravity and is on the stone in about a second, which is too quick to read. It eases in after the release has been seen at speed and is let go once the balls are through the throat, so control is handed back at 1× and nothing lurches. The ceiling's descent is deliberately **not** scaled: that is a rule of the level playing out, not part of the spectacle.
- **Skip is LMB / Space / gamepad A, and Escape deliberately stays pause.** Taking Escape would give one key two meanings *and* leave the player unable to pause during a cinematic; the skip belongs with the buttons that already mean "yes, go on", which are the ones the hand is on. `SKIP_LOCKOUT` (0.3 s) ignores it at the start — the shot that triggered the cinematic was fired with the very button that skips, so a player firing in bursts would otherwise never see one. That is the splash's `SKIP_AFTER` reasoning, and it is not hypothetical: the first verification harness fired every 1.6 s and silently skipped every cinematic it triggered.
- **Both level endings wait for it.** `CheckLevelLost` and the cleared countdown are both held while it is engaged, because either one puts the result screen over this one and a level that ends mid-collapse takes the collapse the player earned off the screen before they have seen it — the same reason `LEVEL_CLEARED_BEAT` exists at all. A shot that clears the field is the one most worth watching, so the trigger runs *before* `CheckLevelCleared`.
- **The subject is held by body handle, not by list index.** `ReleaseSameTypeCluster` appends the released balls to `_fallingBalls`, so they are its last `Matched + Orphaned` entries at the instant `OnBallLanded` fires — but the kill plane then removes them one at a time and the indices shift. Bepu recycles a handle once its body is gone, which would let a later ball inherit a dead subject's identity; that cannot bite **here and only here**, because nothing is added to the simulation while a cinematic runs — the gun is locked, so there is no shot to land and therefore no further release.
- **Variety is rolled per shot.** Distance, the swing around the drop, a slow arc, the elevation the arc starts and ends at, the tilt and how slow the slow motion goes are all drawn per cinematic; a third of shots stay level, because a tilt that happens every time stops reading as emphasis and starts reading as a broken horizon. The swing is measured from **the player's own bearing** and never exceeds a quarter turn, or the player loses which way the field is facing and has to find it again when control returns.
- **Everything else is bounded by one requirement: the balls stay on screen for as much of the drop as possible.** That is not a preference, it is what the whole shot is for, and it decides the two things below.
  - **`SceneRenderer.OpenBelow` splits the scenes in two, and the question is what is opaque under the island.** The two cities continue underneath the arena and fall away into a canyon, and the sea is water the lens can dive into — in both, the drain's glass is translucent, so from below the balls read right through the cone and then pour out of the hole. The five solid-terrain scenes are a flat clearing with the island's footprint cut out of it **and a near-black pit shaft hugging the glass**: the ground is a lid, the shaft is opaque, and there is no vantage down there that can see a ball at all. That is why `OpenBelow` is defined as the exact complement of `IsSolidTerrainScene` rather than as a second hand-kept list — the shaft those scenes need is precisely what closes the view from below (#75; see "The setting, in one copy" in `docs/scenes.md`). So those shots climb instead of descending — the elevation keys run to +48…72° rather than −38…−18° — and end looking straight down the throat.
  - **The mouth is the only hole in the stone, so a lens above it can see a ball below it only by looking through that hole.** The lens has to lie in the cone that runs from the ball up through the rim, and clamping its horizontal reach to satisfy that is what swings the shot overhead as the balls run deeper: the further down they are, the narrower the set of places that can still see them. It is the whole of how a solid-terrain scene is filmed and a safety net in the others. Two more guards go with it — while the balls are still *above* the stone the lens is too (the island's top is an opaque annulus, and from under it a shot looks at the underside of the platform with the drop hidden behind it), and the platform is treated as the **solid it is**, so a low swing is pushed out of the drum rather than driving through it. A first pass had only a per-scene floor on the lens Y, which put the camera inside the stone in the terrain scenes and left the balls invisible behind it.
- **The shot ends when the drop stops being one.** Waiting for the kill plane to take the last ball leaves the camera holding on a straggler — one wedged in a slow-draining pile, or (before the stone dished) resting on the flat ring where nothing would ever remove it (see `RemoveFallenBalls`) — the sea's final frames were an empty blue rectangle with one ball in the corner. `Stalled` ends it when the subject descends less than a unit over ~0.9 s, and that is measured in **simulated** seconds, not wall-clock ones: in slow motion a group that has only just been released covers well under a real second's worth of fall, and a wall-clock timer would call that a stall and cut the shot at the moment it began.
- **The sea's underwater murk came over from the Testbed for this.** `Tonemap.fx` already had it (the shaders are shared); the game had it pinned at zero because nothing here ever went below the island. Now the shot follows the balls through the surface and the frame has to say so: `FinishSceneDraw` ramps the amount by how far the lens is under `SceneRenderer.SeaLevelY` and hands it to the shared pipeline's `Resolve` (the two murk colours live on `PostProcessPipeline`, set once). The result is the drain's glass cone full of balls seen from underwater with the waterline across the frame — the best shot in the game, and the reason the sea is in `OpenBelow` at all.
- **`RecoilCamera` grew a `BaseRoll`** for the tilt — a deliberate roll of the base pose, as against the involuntary one the shake adds. It is added to the shake's roll rather than applied separately: they are the same degree of freedom, and rolling twice about an axis that moved in between is not a roll of anything. At 0 the camera is byte-for-byte what it was.
- **One `[cinematic]` line per shot**, in the manner of the `[level]` and `[score]` lines — a rare event, not a per-frame one, and since the shot is rolled it is the only record of what a badly framed one actually chose. ASCII and `InvariantCulture`: an arrow and a degree sign both came back as `r` on a Czech console and made the figures unreadable.
- **Testing it needs the game driven through its menus, and Myra reads its input once per frame** — so a scripted mouse press and release sent back to back is never seen as a click at all, exactly as a keyboard down+up is not seen as a key press. Both have to be **held across a frame**. Measured on `One.json` back when the bar was a flat 6: three cinematics in 90 s of firing, at 109, 46 and 34 balls. Note those three are a rising sequence only by accident — under the record rule that same run would have fired on the 109 and then on nothing else, which is the intended shape.

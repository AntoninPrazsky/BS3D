# The game: a session

What a level actually is while it is being played: the simulation, the cluster, the gun, the level files and the score.

Part of the BS3D documentation. CLAUDE.md holds the project overview, the build commands, the ball grid and the repo-wide conventions, and says which of these documents covers what.

## The game (Game)

The shipping product, founded as a thin executable over the shared libraries rather than as a fork of the Testbed monolith. It is split along the line between what outlives a session and what *is* one (#65): `Game/BS3DGame.cs` is the **host** — the frame, the device and content, the shared setting every screen stands in, the post-processing and the screen stack — and `Game/Screens/GameplayScreen.cs` is the **session**, which owns the gun, the shot, the simulation and the level. Both borrow the instanced ball shader, the procedural `City`, the shared `SceneRenderer`'s self-lit backdrops, the `SkyDome`, the `CloudField` and the linear-radiance pipeline from `Prazsky.Core` exactly as the other two executables do. It builds `InstancedModel.fx`, `Sky.fx`, `Glare.fx`, `Tonemap.fx`, `ShotTrail.fx`, `Fireworks.fx`, the thirteen scene shaders (`Sea`/`Savanna`/`Desert`/`Outback`/`Acacia`/`Flame`/`Birds`/`Mountain`/`Snow`/`Spray`/`Meadow`/`Forest`/`Space`) and the overlay's `Fonts/segoeui.spritefont` out of the Testbed content directory (the island's marble, `GameObjects/Ground_8.png`, was built here long after both islands had moved to procedural `SurfaceTexture`s — dead content, removed; the eighteen `Skyes/SkyDome*.dae` went the same way in #113, when the one dome geometry they shared and their colour palettes moved into `Prazsky.Core.SkyDome` itself). The only files it owns are the menu's three TTFs — **Anton** for the loud type and **Inter** for the small print — which are `EmbeddedResource`s rather than content (see "The front end" in `docs/game-shell.md`).

Since #72 the session is **partial across nine files**, all in `Game/Screens/`. `GameplayScreen.cs` holds every field, the constructor, `CoveredChanged`, `Update` and `Draw`; beside it are `.Rules.cs` (what a landing does, when a level is cleared or lost, the ball census, the result), `.Session.cs` (a session's lifetime — install, the field's derived figures, tear-down), `.Ripple.cs` (the wave a landing sends through the cluster), `.Input.cs`, `.Ceiling.cs` (the descending plate's state machine), `.Camera.cs` (the pose, precise aim, the fit solve, the recoil stroke), `.Physics.cs` (the Bepu world and its fixed step) and `.Draw.cs` (the balls in the frame). **No state moved** and `Update` did not move, both deliberately — see "Both halves are partial across several files now" in `docs/game-shell.md` for what that split is and is not, and read the partial's own doc before working in it: each names the extraction it is staging for and the invariant that survives there.

What it is: the round stone island the gun stands on with the Testbed's **glass drain funnel** in its middle, standing in **any of the thirteen settings** — the same `SceneKind` set the Testbed and the map editor draw, picked at random each launch and switchable from the menu — under a sky of **drifting procedural clouds**, with a **hanging cluster** (a stepped square pyramid, apex down, in four colours) hanging from the Testbed's own **translucent glass ceiling**, the procedural cannon with its magazine showing through the barrel's slot — **glazed** in a dark blue pane, with only the round about to fire left open in an oval notched to its own shape (see "The gun's hardware, in one copy" in `docs/testbed.md`) — the shot at `SHOOT_SPEED` (200 u/s) with its launch smear, each scene's own point lights, and the recoil below — which moves the gun as well as the camera. Post-processing is the Testbed's own: supersampled HDR target → glare → ACES tonemap, with the same constants, so the two agree — including the sea's **underwater murk**, which arrived with the drop cinematic (the one thing here that takes a lens below the waterline).

**The game runs a real Bepu simulation** — the founding slice's biggest deliberate omission, and it is gone. The cluster is bodies held to each other and to a kinematic glass ceiling by `BallSocket` constraints, so it sways and a shot shoves it; a shot is a dynamic body thrown down the bore; the island's drain is a collision mesh balls run down and fall through; and a group of three or more touching balls of one colour lets go and falls, taking anything it was the last anchor for with it. See "Physics in the game" below.

**The cluster is a level off disk**, read through the shared `Level`/`BallsMap` loaders from the files a `LevelSet` orders — see "Levels in the game" below.

**Clearing the field wins the level and advances through the set**, and every clear earns a **1–4 star rating** whose campaign total is one of the two things that unlock later levels — the other, since #347, being that the campaign opens one level at a time. See "Levels in the game" and "Scoring" below.

What it still deliberately does **not** have: no aim feedback beyond precise aim's crosshair (#47). It is no longer silent — the shot, the landing and the fireworks are synthesized from raw PCM at startup (#46, "The sound" in `docs/game-feedback.md`), and a level plays over a synthesized eurodance loop ("The music" in `docs/game-feedback.md`). The score, the streak and the balls left are on screen while a level runs (#60, "The in-play HUD" in `docs/game-feedback.md`), the outcome is a result screen over the stopped frame (#59), and a level can be lost as well as won (#58) — the budget running out, or the descending glass pushing the cluster past the death line.

The first pass over it after it was founded closed four gaps against the Testbed — the cluster hung from nothing, the island was a flat grey band, the sky was a flat purple wash, and shot balls stuck where they touched and chained into an icicle pointing at the camera. All four are above; the icicle is below, along with the traps each one turned out to carry.

- **Recoil and camera shake.** `Prazsky.Core.Camera.CameraShake` is the game-agnostic half: `Kick(strength)` on firing, and it produces **offsets only** — a rotation triple (pitch/yaw/roll), a lens displacement, a set-back along the view and an FOV punch — so a kick can never accumulate into the pose, and at rest every output is exactly zero. It models *two* things because a gun does two things: a **directional recoil** (thrown back along the view, muzzle rising — not random, which is what separates recoil from a generic wobble) that recovers quickly, and a **random rattle** that decays a little slower. Both decays are **linear in time, not exponential**, so the shake genuinely ends instead of leaving a permanent sub-pixel jitter; the amplitude is the trauma *squared*, because a linear decay spends most of its life in the middle amplitudes and reads as a lingering wobble rather than a jolt. The rattle's phase runs off accumulated seconds (two sines at incommensurable frequencies), never off a frame counter — a per-frame random would shake faster on a faster machine.
- **`RecoilCamera` is its own `ICamera`, and the reason is roll.** It lives beside `CameraShake` in `Prazsky.Core.Camera` (hoisted from the Game in #77 — nothing in it was game-specific). `BasicCamera3D` rebuilds its view with a fixed world up, which is exactly right for a level horizon and exactly wrong for a camera being knocked sideways; `RecoilCamera` keeps the base pose (which the game rewrites every frame from where the gun stands) separate from the shaken one, rotates the view basis by the shake and **rolls the up vector about the view axis**, so the whole frame tips. Rolling is what makes the eye read "the camera was hit" rather than "the aim moved a little", so `MaxRoll` is the largest of the three angles. Measured at full strength: 40 ms after a shot the mean frame difference is ~9 %, and by 200 ms it is back under 1 % — a hard jolt that is gone before the next shot. `BaseRoll` is the *deliberate* roll on the same axis (the drop cinematic's tilt), added to the shake's rather than applied separately, so a gun fired mid-tilt still reads as a kick on top of the tilt.
- **The camera's ceiling was lowered, and the gun took over the difference.** At full strength the shake read as the *lens* being hit rather than as a gun going off, so every dial of `CameraShake` is scaled by one number in `Initialize` (`CAMERA_SHAKE_SCALE`, 0.45) — the shape of the response is kept, only its ceiling comes down, and that is the one number to turn. It is the **ceiling** that is scaled and not `RECOIL_KICK`, which stays 1: `Kick` accumulates and saturates at 1, so a fractional kick would let two shots in quick succession climb straight back to the response that was too strong. What replaces it is the gun's own recoil, and since #115 the stroke is the shared **`Cannon`'s** (`Cannon.RECOIL_BACK`/`RECOIL_DECAY`/`CARRIAGE_RECOIL_BACK`) rather than this executable's: the tube is thrown straight back along its bore and slides home over ~0.24 s, carrying the balls loaded in it, and the **undercarriage takes a smaller, later share of the same shock** — `CarriageRecoilBack`, the hump `4s(1−s)` over the tube's own normalized stroke, zero at the shot itself, peaking ~0.07 s in and settling softly, so the carriage answers a beat *after* the tube the way a real gun's frame shows what the recoil mechanism did not swallow. The whole gun rides the shove (`BarrelWorld` and `CarriageWorld` both take it, so the trunnion pins stay married to the cradle; the queue reads `DrawnMuzzlePosition` through `Magazine.Pose`; the wheels roll by `WheelTravel`, the walk's ground plus the shove). Two responses off one clock only stay one clock if the gun owns it — which is why the stroke moved out of this file; the executables keep the ticks (`KickRecoil` on firing, `StepRecoil` beside the magazine's glide), and the Testbed's game mode fires the same stroke now. Squared in the tube's stroke, so the throw is instant and the return eases — the same reasoning as the shake's amplitude. All of it is **drawing only**: a shot leaves along the true aim on the frame it is fired, before the barrel moves, so nothing about where a ball goes depends on it (the trail stays anchored at the un-recoiled muzzle, ~1 unit from the drawn one at the peak, which is invisible against a 7-unit smear).
- **The camera pose** is the Testbed's game camera, and since #76 literally the same expression: `GameCameraFit.CameraPosition`, back from the field centre along the gun's own bearing (`Cannon.StandBearing`) and `CAMERA_HEIGHT` below its trunnions, looking at the cluster. The bearing is **flattened to the horizontal** for the same reason as in the Testbed — taken straight from the gun's offset it tilts down by however far the gun stands below the cluster and eats the camera's height. The stand-off and the aim height are solved per level and per display, not tuned; see "Levels in the game (Game)".
- **The shot is a swept sphere test, not a point test.** At 200 u/s a ball crosses several diameters per frame, so a point test at the end of the frame tunnels clean through the cluster; `TryHitCluster` solves for the **moment of contact** instead — the quadratic in the step parameter whose smaller root is where the two spheres first touch. It first ranked candidates by their *clamped closest approach*, which is wrong in exactly the case that matters: on the frame a ball first comes within reach it is still approaching, so every candidate's closest approach lies past the end of the step and clamps to 1. The whole field ties, and "first touched" then falls out of the array order rather than the geometry. Cheap either way — a few hundred quadratics per flying ball.
- **A landed ball snaps into the lattice; sticking it where it touched is what made the game read as broken.** `ShotPlacement.TrySolveAgainstBall` answers with the free cell touching the ball it hit that lies nearest the contact point, and the handler places it there, so it lands flush in the packing. (It was `BallsMap.PutBallAtClosestEmptyPositionNextTo`, which decided and wrote in one call; deciding without writing is what lets the aim preview ask the identical question, and since #68 merged the last caller of the combined form away, that method and its ceiling twin are gone.) Stuck at its raw contact point it stands off the surface — and since `_clusterBalls` is also what the next shot is tested against, each ball then sticks to the one before it and they chain into an **icicle pointing back down the line of fire**. Three things follow from doing it properly:
  - The `BallsMap` is now kept alive as the truth about the cluster (it was a local in `BuildCluster`), and `_clusterBalls` is a flattened copy of it — each ball carrying its cell — rebuilt by `RebuildClusterBalls` whenever a ball lands. Rebuilding the lot rather than appending is what keeps the **neighbours'** occlusion right: a ball that arrives also boxes in everything it arrived next to.
  - **The layout hangs at the top of a taller field**, exactly as a map file's does, so the empty levels underneath are room to grow downwards. Because a level's height is its index over √2, the layout would ride that many levels higher, so the cluster offset (`_clusterWorldOffset`, solved per level by `FitFieldToMap` — see "Levels in the game") hangs the field's top where the frame wants it: the grid is the truth, and the offset is applied where the lattice frame meets the world and nowhere else (the general-purpose `MapToWorld`/`WorldToMap` pair this used to go through is gone — see "Physics in the game" for the two crossings that replaced it). What constrains `CLUSTER_EXTRA_LEVELS` is that **`FIELD_LEVELS` must come out even**, i.e. the field's topmost level must be one of the shifted (odd-index) ones. `BallsMap.Center()` centres the map on that top level and offsets by its bounding box's *half-extent* less a ball radius — on a shifted level, whose cells run from 0.5 to N−0.5, that lands exactly on the origin; on an unshifted one it lands half a unit off the axis the gun orbits and the camera looks down. (It was 12 + 6; it is 9 + 7 now that the layout is a nine-level pyramid.) The old rule of thumb that `CLUSTER_EXTRA_LEVELS` itself had to be even belonged to a layout whose shape was written in raw cell indices; `BuildCluster` measures each level's width against the *shifted* position of its cells instead, so the pyramid comes out the same at either parity. **Extra room in X/Z is *not* free the same way** — the same half-extent offset only lands on the origin when the top level's occupied cells start at index 0, so insetting the layout laterally would silently push the whole cluster off centre by the inset.
  - **The first level is a stepped square pyramid, apex down, in four colours.** Widest at the top level (the full `CLUSTER_X` × `CLUSTER_Z` base) and half a unit narrower on every side per level down, so the side count steps 9, 8, 7 … 1 — half a unit, not a whole cell, because consecutive levels are offset by +0.5 in X and Z and shrinking by a cell would put every second level off the axis and zig-zag the flank. `DEFAULT_BALL_TYPES` holds four of the thirteen types and **the magazine draws from the same list**, since loading a colour that is nowhere in the cluster is a shot that cannot be spent; a fixed `LEVEL_SEED` makes it the same level every run, which until map files arrive is the whole of its authoring.
  - **The first ring is not always enough.** The ball a shot reaches first is on the cluster's outer face, and where that face is the field's own wall there is no cell beyond it — so the pocket around an edge ball fills after a handful of shots and every ball after that was silently eaten (measured: 12 of 21 shots on one fixed aim). `TryFindCellInSecondRing` widens the search to the free cells touching the balls that touch the one it hit, nearest the contact first: local by construction, so the ball never lands somewhere it could not have rolled to. That took the same test from 9 of 25 attaching to 25 of 25.
- **The landing preview, and why the field's edge needed one (#70).** The field is a **box**, and the levels shipped with it have **no lateral margin at all** — `One.json` is `sx`/`sz` 10 against a layout occupying x 0…9, z 0…9, and `Colossus.json` is 12 against 12 — so every ball on the cluster's side face *is* on the field's wall and the room a map gives is only downwards. Aim into a pocket in that wall once both rings are full and the shot sticks nowhere: the ball bounces off, falls through the drain, and costs a ball from the budget **and** the streak (`RemoveFallenBalls` scores it a miss), for a refusal the player had no way to see coming. Three things were considered and rejected before the preview. **Widening the search** to a third ring is the defect #70 was opened for, arriving from the other side — the locality is what keeps a ball from attaching visibly behind what was aimed at. **Refunding the shot** is silent, reads as a bug, and the shot count is what drives the ceiling's cadence. **Giving the field margin** moves the edge without removing it, and it is not free: the glass plate is fitted to `StageSizeX`/`StageSizeZ`, so it would hang visibly wider than the cluster, and the camera fit would stand further out and shrink every level. So the edge stays where it is and becomes **legible** instead.
  - **One function decides, twice.** `Prazsky.BS3D.Physics.ShotPlacement` owns the rule; the contact handler asks it when a ball really touches and attaches with what it gets back, and the preview asks the identical call every frame from the barrel's own line. This is a correctness requirement, not tidiness — #70 records that a line drawn over behaviour it does not share would disagree with the outcome and make the frustration *worse* than showing nothing. `BallsMap` grew query counterparts (`TryFindEmptyCellNextTo`, `TryFindEmptyCellInSecondRing`, `TryFindEmptyCeilingCell`) so the question can be asked without writing, and the `PutBallAt*` methods are thin wrappers over them. That retired a real trap on the way: the ceiling placement used to fill its `out` cell in from the rounded contact *before* testing bounds and occupancy, so a refusal handed back a plausible-looking cell — and a caller testing the cell rather than the returned position indexed the structure array out of bounds or overwrote a live ball.
  - **A swept sphere, not a ray.** The shot has a radius, so it touches the first ball whose surface its own surface reaches; a thin line down the middle of the bore misses a ball just off to the side that the ball itself would clip. Testing the aim against each structure ball grown by the sum of both radii is exactly that, in closed form. It walks the live `PhysicsBall` array rather than the simulation, for two reasons: it is the same array the attach path consults, so the two cannot disagree about which balls exist, and a Bepu sweep would want a shape, a pool and a hit handler per frame where this wants a few hundred dot products and **allocates nothing**. What it cannot promise is the stepped simulation's own answer — the shot travels 1.667 units a step and attaches on the first step that overlaps, and the cluster sways through the ~0.1 s of flight — which is why it is drawn as a ghost rather than a promise.
  - **The ghost is the signal; the crosshair only confirms it.** A mostly-dissolved ball in the cell it would land in, in the colour actually loaded at the muzzle — the useful question is not only "does this stick" but "does it stick *next to two more of its own*", which a neutral grey would hide. It rides the shader's existing **dissolve** — a dither cut in blocks of the screen rather than a transparency (see "The ball dissolve dithers in display pixels" in `docs/rendering.md`), so it needs no blend state and no sorting: opaque geometry with half of itself missing, which reads as "not there yet". The ghost appears exactly when a shot sticks, so its absence covers every way one does not — a full pocket, open sky, the glass — with no case where it says the wrong thing. The crosshair reddens only when a refusal is **certain** (the aim reaches a ball and neither ring has room), and only in precise aim, because that is the one mode where the screen's centre is where the gun points: in the overview the lens looks *at* the cluster rather than along the bore, so a mark in the middle of the screen would name a spot the barrel is not aimed at. `PREVIEW_DISSOLVE` is tuned against the **overview**, where the ghost is a few dozen pixels — 0.62 was easy to miss there, and much lower reads as a ball that is already present, which would send the player aiming elsewhere.
  - **The ghost blinks, because it was measured and it is wrong more often than it is right.** Fired with Space — which leaves along the *bit-identical* pose the ghost was solved from, so input staleness is excluded entirely and only the physics is left — 26 shots gave 10 landings of which **3 landed in the cell the ghost showed**. Five of the seven misses were one cell or one level out, which still points at the right pocket; two were two and three levels out, which does not. None of the causes is a bug: at `SHOOT_SPEED` the shot crosses 1.667 units per physics step, so the touch is reported up to a step late and the ball has *slid* around the struck surface by then (friction 1, a 30 Hz contact spring — the speculative contact kills the approach along the normal while the tangential velocity survives); a glancing pass the analytic sweep counts can fall entirely between two step boundaries, so a **different ball** anchors the solve and offers a different ring of candidates; and once the first ring is full the second ring scores some thirty cells two levels wide by plain distance, which multiplies every one of those perturbations. So the ghost may not stand still and be read as a promise: `PREVIEW_BLINK_DEPTH` swings its dissolve ±0.22 about `PREVIEW_DISSOLVE` at `PREVIEW_BLINK_HZ` (2.2), on the wall clock. A **smooth swing and not a hard on/off**, for a reason the ghost's second job forces: its *colour* is what answers "does it stick next to two more of its own", and a ball that is absent half the time cannot be read for that. Faster than the cluster's 1.1 Hz heartbeat on purpose, so it reads as unsettled rather than as alive.
    - Two further terms are known and deliberately not chased. The **mouse path is one frame stale**: `MouseAim.ApplyCursor` applies the frame's delta *before* the left-button edge is tested, so a mouse-fired shot leaves along an aim one frame newer than the ghost the player clicked at — 0.002 rad per pixel over a 12–24 unit flight is a whole cell for ~20 px of travel in that frame. Space is already correct (it fires before the aim moves). It is not reordered because it would buy little: the physics terms above already miss 7 of 10 with *zero* input staleness, and a preview cannot be made exact. And two shots can be in the air at once (there is no fire-rate limiter), so a landing can take the previewed cell or a matched release can delete the very ball the ghost was solved against.
  - **Where the ghost is drawn is a second question from which cell it is, and the ceiling is what proved it.** A cell is an index into a lattice hung once per level (`_clusterWorldOffset`); the balls are somewhere else, by two amounts. The structure hangs *stretched* — the ceiling anchor holds the top level a whole ball diameter under the plate's centre, which is a unit above its own lattice row, decaying to nothing further down (measured on the balls actually hit: +1.10 / +1.03 / +1.02 / +1.00 high in the cluster against −0.04 low in it). And the glass **descends**: `CEILING_DESCENT_PER_STEP` (0.6) per step drags the whole cluster down while the lattice does not move at all, so the two come apart by 3.6 units over `One.json`'s six steps and 6.6 over `Colossus.json`'s eleven — nine lattice levels, against cells 1.0 across. Drawn from the lattice alone the ghost therefore sat a cell and a half *below* its pocket at the start of a level and climbed steadily *out through the top* of the cluster as the level went on, which is what "the preview does not respect the ceiling at all" looks like on screen. It is drawn in the cluster's **live** frame instead (`ShotPlacement.CellWorldPosition`), off the same local drift `TrySolveAgainstBall` already measured to choose the cell — so the ghost cannot be anywhere the decision did not mean, and the descent, the resting stretch and the sway are one term rather than three corrections. The drift is taken **whole** and not just its Y: the ghost then sways *with* the pocket it sits in, where a ghost standing still while the balls around it move is the thing that actually reads as jitter. Measured against where a landed ball's body comes to rest, over fourteen landings across five plate heights: mean error 0.21 with the correction, **0.78** without, and two of the fourteen land inside 0.04.
    - **The same stale frame reached two more places, with different symptoms, and both are fixed with it.** `PhysicsBall.StartRenderGlide` is handed where the ball will end up, so a target from the lattice launched the arrival glide from up to 6.6 units *below* the ball's own impact — a landed ball dropping a stride and rushing back up. And `BallLanding.World` is what the landing is *heard and seen* at: the thunk's panning, the release's, and the birth point the score popup flies into the corner from, all of which were placed above the cluster's roof late in a level. Neither is the preview, and both were the same one line.
    - **What was never wrong is which cell.** Substitute the drift into `TrySolveAgainstBall`'s anchored contact and `worldOffset` cancels outright: the expression reduces to `worldContact − hitBallPose + idealHit`, so the *decision* was already immune to anything that moves the whole cluster, the descent included. `TrySolveAgainstCeiling` is immune for a different reason — `BallsMap.TryFindEmptyCeilingCell` pins the level to the field's top and reads only X and Z, and the plate only ever moves in Y. That is why this was a drawing defect and not a gameplay one, and why fixing it cannot change where a shot goes. It is also why the ceiling path needs the plate's **live** centre handed in (`BallsConstraintsBuilder.CeilingRestY`) — not for its cell, only for where that cell now is.
  - **The beam is what the overview gets instead of a crosshair** (`Prazsky.Core.Render.AimBeam`). A dashed line of light out of the muzzle, crawling towards its far end, ending where a shot fired now would arrive. It exists because the overview lens looks *at* the cluster rather than along the bore, so before this the only clue to where the gun pointed was the barrel's foreshortened angle — and because it is what carries the **refusal** to that mode: a red beam stopping on the ball it cannot stick to says there what the reddened crosshair says in precise aim. Its far end carries the meaning, so it ends at the **touch** and not at the cell — that is where the ball actually stops, and it puts the tip on the surface it strikes instead of pushing it through into the cluster. Aiming at nothing it is drawn `openEnded`, fading out over its tail so the line dies away rather than stopping at a phantom.
    - **The beam and the crosshair hand over to each other**, rather than both being up at once: the beam's opacity is `1 - PreciseAim.Blend`, the inverse of the crosshair's own. That is a measured need and not symmetry for its own sake. Leaning in over the barrel the lens looks *along* the bore, so the beam foreshortens into a stack of dashes piled over the very cell it is pointing at — it was obscuring the ghost in the one mode where precision is the point, and it had nothing to add there either, because a crosshair on a lens aimed down the shot ray already *is* the trajectory. Each mode ends up with exactly one signal.
    - **Its width and brightness were set by looking, and the first guesses were wrong by a lot.** 0.085 half-width and 1.35 radiance were reasoned from the ball's radius and from the smear's boost, and the beam came out **invisible** at the overview stand-off — the mode it exists for. It was then bracketed from the other side (0.45 and 8 are unmistakable and far too heavy) and settled at 0.15 and 3.5, which is *above* `LaunchSmears`' boost even though a permanent guide ought to be quieter than a shot going off: the smear is briefly enormous where this is permanently thin, and a thin additive line over a lit cluster needs the radiance to read at all. Anything derived from a ball's size rather than from what a few dozen pixels of additive line look like over that cluster should be expected to be wrong the same way.
  - **It borrows `ShotTrail.fx` from `LaunchSmears`, and that had a consequence worth knowing.** The shader is already a camera-facing billboard between two world points that fades each segment in over its first quarter and out over its last — so a *short* segment of it is a dash with soft ends, and the beam needed no shader of its own and no new blend state. But `LaunchSmears` set the trail's two widths **once in its constructor**, correctly, on the reasoning that a compile-time constant need not be re-sent (`BestPractices.md` §1 names those two parameters as its own example). That stopped being true the moment a second component shared the effect: parameter values belong to the `Effect`, the two want different widths — the smear tapers, the beam is parallel-sided — and whichever constructor ran last would have decided how both look. Both now push them once per `Draw`, which is per frame per component and still not per primitive. **A shared effect turns any "set it once" into a race**, and this is the second component to share one.
  - **Shown exactly when a shot would leave the barrel**, which is what keeps it from promising: silent under a drop cinematic, silent once `_score.OutOfShots`, and silent once the level is decided — the *same* tests `Shoot` refuses on, so the two cannot drift. The last of those was deliberately absent while firing through the collapse was allowed; #177 stopped allowing it and the preview followed in the same change, which is also what takes the beam and the ghost out of the frame the result screen is read over — a test rather than the stack's doing, since that page no longer stops the session at all (#241) and even a stopped one goes on drawing its last answer.
- **The ambient-occlusion direction is a sum of unit vectors and must be divided by `BallRenderSet.MAX_OCCLUDERS` before the shader sees it.** Handed over raw it is up to twelve times too long, the shader's dot against it saturates over most of the ball, and every surface ball wears a hard-edged black crescent instead of the soft inward shading that makes the cluster read as one body rather than a heap of spheres. **That is history now rather than a rule to hold to, and it is worth keeping because of what it cost:** the Testbed divides — as all three executables do since #76, through the shared `BallRenderSet.OcclusionTarget`, which is the only code path that can build the vector at all and divides inside it; the game did not, and it cost the cluster its whole look, the crescents going from every surface ball the moment that one path became the only one. Nothing can differ about it again, because there is nowhere left for it to differ (see "The rest of the hardware, in one copy" in `docs/testbed.md`).
- **The stone ring is a dish now, and that is a big-map fix before it is a look.** The walkable top between the drain's mouth and `ArenaIsland.FLOOR_RADIUS` falls `ArenaIsland.DISH_DEPTH` (1.2, ~6.4° over the 10.7-unit run) from its outer arris to the rim, drawn and collided from the same figures (`IslandMesh` takes the depth, `FunnelPhysics.Build` takes the same constant), and the drain's rim, its gold bead and the pit's mouth all sit that far below `TOP_Y` with it. It exists because the flat ring only ever worked for small maps: a ball that lands on flat stone stays where it stops, and the wider the field the larger the share of released balls that came down outside the mouth's radius 14 and simply parked there. On the dish they roll in — Bepu spheres carry no rolling resistance (friction resists sliding, not rolling), so the shallow grade is plenty, and the drain finally swallows what a big map drops. Verified with the Testbed's `End` release of `Full.json`: the ring stays clean while the funnel floods and drains, where balls used to rest across the stone. The gun **stands on the dish** now rather than floating over it: `Cannon` re-seats its height off `CannonRig.TrunnionHeightAt` (the local stone per `ArenaIsland.FloorHeightAt` plus the wheel stack) on every move, the carriage pitches to the stance's own grade, and the fit keeps the stand and the walk's near end off the glass (`GameCameraFit.CANNON_DRAIN_CLEARANCE`) — it still has no collider, so all of it is a drawing fact (see "The gun's hardware, in one copy" in `docs/testbed.md`). Three floors moved with it, each measured rather than assumed: the overview lens is floored at the height it had when the gun grazed the arris (`GameCameraFit`'s `LENS_FLOOR_Y` — a lens riding `CAMERA_HEIGHT` below the sunken gun sat below the arris and the coping swallowed the bottom half of the frame), the ADS lens floor follows the local stone (`PreciseAim.FLOOR_CLEARANCE` over `FloorHeightAt`, since no single height serves a gun that stands anywhere on a dish), and the gun renderers' ground-darkening anchor rides the stone under the pose per frame (a gun a `DISH_DEPTH` below a fixed anchor saturated the darkening over the whole undercarriage).
- **The island is a washer again, because the drain arrived to fill it (#48).** The platform's bore came over from the Testbed's rim radius (`ArenaIsland.FUNNEL_TOP_RADIUS`, 14) before the funnel did, which left a 28-unit hole dead centre under the hanging cluster, open onto the city below; it was pinned to 0 until the funnel could fill it, and it is back at the rim radius now that it does. The glass cone, its two gold beads and every figure of them were the Testbed's unchanged, and since #75 they are **literally the same object**: `ArenaIsland` builds the whole assembly and both executables draw it, in the same order — the opaque rims with the opaque scene and the one-sided glass after them, inside one `CullNone` sandwich, before the glass ceiling. The two things this file used to say had **not** come over have both since arrived. There is a real Bepu simulation here, and `FunnelPhysics.Build` hands it the drain's own surface as the island's whole collision floor, so a ball landing on the stone rolls down its dish into the cone exactly as it does in the Testbed (see the dish bullet above). And the dark **pit shaft** is drawn too, in the ten `SceneRenderer.IsSolidTerrainScene`s — in the city, which is the scene this was first written against, the drain simply looks through onto the canyon below and no shaft is wanted. Note the play camera sits 0.6 above the island, so the drain reads from it as a gold ring low across the bottom of the frame with the dark dish behind it — it opens up as the camera moves, the same as in the Testbed's game mode. The platform is not merely like the Testbed's, it is the same `ArenaIsland` — one `IslandMesh` profile, two materials over `SurfaceTexture`'s procedural stone and concrete, one set of figures (see "The setting, in one copy" and "The city and the glass arena" in `docs/scenes.md`). The trap CLAUDE.md flags elsewhere is real and was exactly what left it a flat grey band at one point — **without a `DetailTexture` the renderer falls through to the plain technique and every `SlabSize`/`CavityStrength`/relief setting is silently dead** — and the texture it was then given, `Ground_8.png`, turned out to be black over half its canvas, which is why nothing is loaded here now.
- **Precise aim (ADS) is the Testbed's — since #76 the same `PreciseAim`, not a port of it — and so is the reason for it.** Held on the **right mouse button** (or the gamepad's left trigger), the lens leans in over the barrel — `BACK` behind the muzzle along `-aim`, `RISE` above the bore — and looks at a point *on the shot ray*, so the shot goes where the screen-centre crosshair points; `PreciseAim.FOV` (36°) is a modest 1.19× lean-in on `GAME_FOV`. It exists because how much angle onto the map the overview can give is fixed by where the camera stands, and a lens looking *along* the aim sees whatever the barrel points at, head-on. One reversible scalar `PreciseAim.Blend` eases 0→1 with the `Magazine.Slide` idiom and `BlendedPose` returns `Lerp(overview, leaned, Blend)`, so at 0 the overview pose comes back bit for bit and an interrupted hold never snaps. One real difference from the Testbed, and it is why the component hands a pose **back as a value** rather than writing a camera: `RecoilCamera` takes a base pose rather than `BasicCamera3D`'s ordered setters, so the FOV → position → target ordering that file insists on has no counterpart here — the drop cinematic goes on lerping over the returned pose, and the shake is applied on top of whatever came out. The focus gate is the *same* rule by a different route, not a difference: the Testbed rebuilds its held flag with an `IsActive` term every frame, while here `UpdateAim` simply does not run while the window is inactive, so `_adsHeld` is cleared in the inactive branch instead — `PreciseAim.Step` still runs, which is how the lean eases out rather than dropping. Either way a gamepad trigger — which XInput reports to an unfocused window — cannot keep an alt-tabbed game leaned in. `PreciseAim.FLOOR_CLEARANCE` floors the lens a unit above the stone directly under it (`ArenaIsland.FloorHeightAt` — the dish, so no single fixed height serves): aiming steeply up, `-aim` points down and the set-back would otherwise drop it through the stone and show the island from underneath. Since #214 the lean also takes the frame's **periphery** out of focus — the pipeline's defocus in its centre-sharp shape, riding `Blend` directly — see "The end of a level goes out of focus" in `docs/game-feedback.md` for the moment and `docs/rendering.md` for the shape and the measured cost.
- **The FPS line and the crosshair.** `Prazsky.Core.Render.InfoRenderer` draws the FPS as a `DrawableGameComponent` (so `base.Draw` puts it out last, in display space, with its own `SpriteBatch`), off the Testbed's own `segoeui` font built out of the Testbed content directory; **F10** hides it — it was F12, the key that also hides the Testbed's text, until #191 gave F12 to the screenshot; F11 could not be given up for that, being fullscreen in all three executables, so the Game's display keys are a contiguous F10/F11/F12 and the Testbed keeps F12 for its own overlay. It is authored for 2160p and scaled by `Viewport.Height / 2160`, so `RecomputeScale` has to be re-run on a resize *and* on a fullscreen switch. The crosshair needs no bitmap — four bars struck from a 1×1 white texel, scaled the same way — and since #76 it is not the game's own either: `Prazsky.Core.Render.Crosshair` makes its own texel and the Testbed draws the same bars, `Bitmaps/Aimer.png` having been retired with the hoist (and the host's white pixel with it, the crosshair having been its only consumer). It is drawn **only while precise aim is leaning in**, fading up with the blend, because that is the only pose whose lens looks along the shot; in the overview a screen-centre mark would point at nothing in particular. Nor is it drawn once the level is over, whatever the blend still reads — the lean is frozen under the result page (#241 runs the world there, not the game, so nothing steps the blend out), and the mark goes out with the HUD rather than sitting at full opacity over the numbers (#259). That is the caller's decision and not the component's — it takes an opacity, and the Testbed passes a plain 1 in the free mode where the lens *is* the shot.
- **The clouds are the Testbed's weather, minus its ambient half.** One `Sky.fx` entry, `_sky.Effect`, and `_clouds.ApplyTo` on *both* the sky and the instanced effect each frame off the wall clock — which is what keeps the cloud the player looks at and the shadow it throws the same cloud. The ten cloud look values it used to keep of its own are `CloudField`'s since #75, pushed once by `ApplyStaticParameters`. The cloud shadow on the balls, city and island then costs nothing extra: `CloudSunlight` is already called unconditionally in `InstancedModel.fx` and returns a flat 1 while `CloudCoverageGain` is 0. `CloudField.ApplyDome` is **not** optional despite the fixed dome — the two cloud colours have no defaults, unset means a black deck, and since #220 it is also what tells both shaders which way that dome's sun stands. The overcast ambient flattening (`SkyLightRig.StepOvercast`) is deliberately left out: its palette is authored for daylight and is brighter than this dusk dome's own, so lerping towards it would *lighten* a night city as the weather thickened.
- **Controls.** Mouse aims (always — game mode is the only mode), LMB / Space / gamepad right trigger fire, **RMB / gamepad left trigger hold precise aim**, A/D traverse the carriage, **W/S walk it towards the field and back** (nearer = a steeper shot up into the cluster's underside; the range is solved per level, the ends are rubber rather than stops, and the camera goes along with a retreat but never with an advance — see "Cannon controls" in `docs/testbed.md`), **F10 hides the FPS line, F11 toggles fullscreen and F12 saves a PNG of the frame** (#191 — see below), **Escape pauses**, and while a drop cinematic is running the gun does not answer at all — LMB / Space / gamepad A skip it instead (see "The drop cinematic" in `docs/game-feedback.md`) (quitting is a menu item now — a game that closes the instant Escape is tapped is one that loses a game). `quality=<low|medium|high>`, `ssaa=<n>`, `exposure=<f>`, `nocap`, `fullscreen`, `scene=<city|sea|savanna|desert|outback|mountain|meadow|neon|forest|space|dream|cavern|moon>`, `sky=<1..18>`, `logfps`, `celebrate` (fire the victory display on the front end — see "The victory fireworks" in `docs/game-feedback.md`) and `lasers` (pin the floor alarm's laser net on while a level is played — see "The floor alarm" there too) on the command line — the first three seed the settings screen, which then owns them; the scene names are the Testbed's — literally, since #75: `SceneRenderer.TryParseScene` is the one parse for all three, so one script drives any of them unchanged. `scene=`/`sky=`/`logfps` exist for measurement: without pinning, the scene is a different one of the thirteen (`SceneRenderer.SceneCount`) every launch and nothing is comparable (see "The quality tier"). **`play`** (testing only) drops straight into the first level, skipping the title card and the menu, through the very pop-and-push a player's Play click takes — the session's `[field]`/`[camera]` figures and its endings only reach stdout once a level is built, and building one honestly needs a mouse on a Myra button, which a scripted run does not have. **`result`** (testing only, likewise) puts a cleared level's result screen up over whatever is on the stack, for the same reason `celebrate` exists: everything that happens at a level's end — the camera letting go of the gun, the stars landing one at a time, the arena going out of focus behind the page (see "The end of a level goes out of focus" in `docs/game-feedback.md`) — is otherwise reachable only by winning or losing a level, which can no more be scripted than clearing one can. It is held back until the **title card has gone**, and that is not a nicety: the splash hands over with a `Replace`, which pops whatever is on top, so a result page pushed at boot was silently swallowed by the main menu arriving 2.6 s later. Pair it with `celebrate` for the whole moment.

- **The game saves its own frames now (#191), and there are two triggers because one of them could not do the job.** `F12` writes the finished back buffer to a PNG in a `Screenshots` directory beside the executable — timestamp first so a folder sorts into the order it was taken, then the scene — and prints one `[shot] <path>` line so a script can find what it just took. `shot=<t1,t2,…>` does the same at those wall-clock seconds. **The schedule is not a convenience, it is the whole point**: every external capture route can hand back the wrong picture silently (a locked desktop returns the *lock screen*; an unlocked one returns whatever window covers the game — a shot came back with a terminal in it; and `PrintWindow` returned a blank client area from `BS3D.exe` eleven times running while returning the real picture from the Testbed the same day), and on a locked desktop **no keystroke reaches the application at all**, so `F12` cannot serve the case the facility was asked for. Verified exactly that way round: the schedule's shots landed through a locked desktop with the real picture in them, and the key could not be exercised from a script at all. What lands in the file is the frame **as presented** — the FPS line and whatever page is up included, which is usually the point; `F10` hides the overlay first for a clean plate. The readback stalls the pipeline and `SaveAsPng` encodes on the calling thread, so a shot is a long frame (~0.1 s at 1600×900) and the two callers are deliberately an edge and a clock, never anything per-frame. Every edge-driven input — Space, LMB **and** the gamepad trigger, which XInput reports whether the window has focus or not — is skipped for one frame after focus returns, or the click that refocuses the game arrives against a stale "released" and fires a shot the player did not ask for. `ssaa=1` falls back to 8× MSAA on the scene target the way the Testbed does (multisampling antialiases geometry edges but not shading, so it only earns its memory with supersampling off) — that is the setting a weak machine reaches for first, and it had no antialiasing of any kind. `SetGraphics` reads `GraphicsAdapter.DefaultAdapter.CurrentDisplayMode` rather than `GraphicsDevice`: it also runs from the constructor, before the device exists, and reading the device there made `fullscreen` mode-switch the display down to 1600×900 instead of filling it.
- **A resize re-solves the framing, and it must also drop the aim's baseline (#52).** `OnClientSizeChanged` and `SetGraphics` both go through `UpdateCameraAspect`, which re-runs `FitCannonAndGameCameraToLevel` — the fit is checked on *both* frustum axes and only the vertical one is aspect-independent, so a resize has to re-solve the stand-off rather than just the projection (measured on `One.json`: 4:3, 21:9 and 16:9 all settle at 28.0 out, the vertical axis binding at each, which is what "Resolution and aspect" in `docs/rendering.md` says should happen). What that alone still got wrong is the **aim**: it is a delta measured against the viewport centre, and a fullscreen switch moves the centre while the cursor is still parked on the old one — so the first frame after F11 read a delta of half the screen and slammed the barrel into its elevation clamp, leaving the gun pointing at the sky. That is what the bug report's "camera position and other relevant constants fail to recompute" actually looked like on screen. So the resize does not only re-solve the fit: `UpdateCameraAspect` hands the change to the session (`GameplayScreen.OnViewportChanged`), which calls `MouseAim.Invalidate()` **before** re-solving, so the next frame recentres and applies nothing — the same thing `CoveredChanged` does on the way into play, and `MouseAim.Initialized` gates the shot test **explicitly** (it used to sit inside that block by nesting rather than by intent), so no phantom shot goes off either: on the frame the baseline is dropped there is no aim to fire along. It is reached through `_gameplayScreen?.`, which is exactly right: with no session there is no aim baseline to invalidate.
- **The cursor is taken on arrival at play; after a focus loss it is the desktop's until the player clicks in the picture (#99, #154).** A frame that warps the pointer back to the viewport centre is a frame in which the window cannot be dragged by its title bar, resized, or left for another application — which is why the capture is a **state the session enters**, never something a frame just does. #99 made every arrival free and demanded a click in the picture; #154 walked that back for the arrivals, because every way onto the top of the stack — Play, a level tile, Resume, Retry, Next Level — is a menu entry the player just pressed, so `CoveredChanged` takes the cursor at once — but only a pointer **already in the picture** (the capture click's own viewport-bounds test): the entry may just as well have been pressed by the pad or the arrow keys with the mouse parked on another monitor, and warping in a pointer nobody offered is the hostage-taking #99 exists to prevent. An arrival with the window unfocused stays free too, there being no press to read intent from. The pressing click cannot fire a shot — the shot edge sits behind `MouseAim.Invalidate()`'s dropped baseline and is measured against the mouse snapshot `CoveredChanged` takes, which already knows the button is down — and the aim cannot yank, because `ApplyCursor` applies nothing until the first `Recentre` has re-based the delta. **A focus loss keeps #99's whole bargain**: the capture is dropped, the cursor stays visible, nothing calls `Recentre`, and a **left press edge inside the viewport** re-takes it. Three things about that edge. It is gated on `EdgeInputAllowed` like every other, which is what stops the click that merely brings the window forward from also capturing — that one lands on a frame the window was not active for, so a fresh press is needed. It tests the viewport bounds explicitly, because a `MouseState` reports negative and past-the-edge coordinates quite happily when the pointer is over the title bar, and those are exactly the clicks to leave alone. And **it cannot fire a shot**, for the same invalidate-and-snapshot reasons the arrival cannot. The pad is untouched throughout and aims and fires with the pointer free; only the right button's precise aim waits for the capture, since a free right button belongs to the desktop. #99's `GetCursorPos` verification stands for the recovery half (free after a title-bar activation, pinned to the client centre after the click in the picture, a title-bar drag moving the window 1112,306 → 1232,346 where it previously did not move at all); #154's is under it in the same terms (pinned from the level's first frames with no click ever sent). The trade #99 recorded now starts at the level's first frame: once captured, the title bar is out of reach until a pause or an alt-tab, which is the same bargain every mouse-look game makes.
- **Two traps worth remembering when testing it.** Once the cursor is captured it is recentred every frame, so **any external `SetCursorPos` reads as a huge aim delta** — the `screenshot.ps1` title-bar click yanks the aim to the elevation and traverse clamps, which is a test artefact, not a bug (a shot then flies 45° wide of the cluster). Compensate by setting the cursor a known offset from the viewport centre afterwards; the aim moves ~0.002 rad per pixel, and there is no auto-recentre to fight. Since #154 a session entered through the menus arrives with the cursor already captured whenever the pointer sits in the picture — the aim answers at once and no click is needed — so a title-bar focus click sent *while a level is up* is back to being the yank hazard; send it before the level is entered, or compensate as above. Only a focus-loss recovery still has the window where a click is needed first (#99). And the keyboard is polled **once per frame**, so a `keybd_event` down+up sent back to back is never seen as a press and the shot silently does not happen — the key has to be *held* across a frame (~40 ms is plenty). Both cost a wasted round of screenshots before they were spotted. **The mouse button is the same device rule and the same trap**: an instantaneous `mouse_event` down+up falls between two reads, which is what made the first run of #99's own probe report that clicking in the picture did not capture the cursor. Hold it ~400 ms, as with the keys.

## Levels in the game (Game)

The game builds its cluster from a **level file**, not from the procedural pyramid it shipped with. What orders those files is a **`LevelSet`** (`Prazsky.BS3D/Levels/LevelSet.cs`, beside `Level`): a JSON object marked `"format": "bs3d-levels"` listing entries **in play order**, the first entry being the first level. `Game/Levels/Levels.json` is the set; **Colossus** is the last hand-drawn level, a 12×12×18 field of 364 balls in six colours, a stepped square ziggurat standing on its point (plates of 144/100/64/36/16/4 stepping in one cell a side per level down) whose colours are laid out in 3×3 blocks *rolled per level*, so every level has findable groups and no single colour stacks into a vertical column that would go all at once and read as a bug. Colossus is authored tighter than One rather than merely bigger: 45 shots against a ceiling that steps every 4, played under the Moon — the one scene no other level had used, a cratered surface under a black starlit sky with the Earth at its real size. They are plain `Content` copied beside the exe (`CopyToOutputDirectory`), **not** MGCB assets — they are data read with System.Text.Json at runtime, and being editable next to the binary without a rebuild is the point.

**The set is 105 levels in eleven blocks — ten chapters of ten and #295's five-level Eruption slotted in at eight** (#194, #207, #182, #253, #255, #295, #325 — a block is one scene, one dome, one music theme, one ball material and one statable style; see "The campaign is eleven blocks" in `docs/formats-and-tools.md` for the table and for what moved). `Colossus` still closes the Quarry as the last hand-drawn level. Since #300 swapped the two city chapters into day order the Spectrum's dawn plays ninth and the Arcade's neon closes the day after dark; **the campaign's last word is `Obsidian`'s since #325 appended the Mirage** — the eleventh chapter, in the dream, and the first whose subject is a *rule* rather than a shape: five levels of the transparent ball and five of the rock, one new kind a level, standing outside the light ramp on purpose (what follows a day is not a later hour of it). (The Eruption before them was inserted, not appended, precisely so the last word would not move; #300 then moved it deliberately, on the owner's twice-raised ruling.) It was nine generated levels and then Colossus when this was written, fourteen entries in one flat ramp before the blocks, twenty-five before the desert was slotted in at block 3, thirty before the Nebula was appended — the edit that moved the campaign-complete moment off Colossus onto Garland (#182, deliberate) — and thirty-five before the Arcade was appended behind it, which moves that moment once more, onto `Globe` — and forty before #253 appended the Spectrum behind *that*, which moves it a third time, onto `Turbine`: five tall levels, each one hue family swept through its whole body as a gradient, in the Arcade’s own city at morning with the neon off. `One` was hand-drawn too and is generated now, at the author's request: it opens the campaign as a perfect pyramid — a centred square course against the glass, one ball narrower a side per level down to the single apex ball, in three colours laid on its walls (12×12×16, 385 balls; the meadow under dome 1 since #194, the savanna under dome 14 before it). Three things went with the regeneration. Its **tail** — the old layout narrowed to a single ball nine levels down and then *widened again*, 4, 8, 12, 25, 16, sixty-five balls hanging under the point, which read as something stuck to the pyramid rather than part of it. Its **eight colours**, on the level that teaches the game and against a magazine that draws evenly among the colours alive, so the wanted ball came one time in eight. And its **missing lateral margin**: 10 wide against a slab occupying x 0…9, the wall trap below. **The square slab it opened with is gone since #234**, and the white ring that bordered it with it: the owner played the opener and reported those balls as the ones needing several hits each, which the tool's report confirmed exactly — 32 white balls in groups of at most 11, where red went in one shot of 81 and yellow in one of 112, because the square ring rounded off into four disconnected arcs. #234 then took two passes more. The cone the slab left behind did not read as a pyramid — its steps were 0.375 of a cell wide against the 1/√2 a level climbs, a spike rather than tiers — so the sides now step 1..10, half a unit a side a level, each course nesting into the pockets of the wider one above it: cannonball packing, and the same rule the game's own fallback map draws. And the colours are the owner's prescription, red, green and blue **on the pyramid's four walls**, turned a step round the palette every shell inward — so peeling a face uncovers a different colour, and the innermost 30 balls are a small solid pyramid under the middle of the plate. Two failed colourings are why it is stated that precisely. Concentric shells peel but hide two colours of the three under the skin, and a player holding three blue balls against a red wall has nothing to shoot at. One solid band a course shows all three at once, but a course of a pyramid is **load-bearing** — everything under it hangs off it — so the band that went took the tip down with it, and the level's biggest single shot was 74 % of the cluster. Walls are load-bearing in neither direction: a shell hangs off the plate it reaches, not off the shell inside it. Four walls against three colours puts one colour on two of them, and it goes on the pair that **face** each other (−Z and +Z), because the gun starts on +Z: the two colours the player would otherwise never see together are then on the flanks, in view from the first shot. Measured on the same shape and the same 385 balls: 6 standing groups, best single shot 30 % (the one-shot gate is 90), colours 134/147/104, nothing alone, no repairs. Colossus (once "Two", back when it was the second level) is the hardest level in the game by a distance — twelve wide, eighteen deep, six colours in 3×3 blocks rolled per level so nothing is ever a big easy plate, on 45 shots against a ceiling stepping every 4. Meeting that second was meeting the wall before the game had taught anything, so it moved to the far end instead — it closed the campaign until #182 appended the Nebula behind it, and it closes the Quarry now. `LevelGen` writes the order; **Colossus is the one level it does not write**, so that entry's rules are stated in the tool verbatim and its scene, dome and `music` are authored in its own file.

**Three to Seven are the pattern pack**, and like every other level they are full `bs3d-level` files, so each carries its own scene and dome and the set is a tour of the backdrops rather than five clusters over whichever one the player last picked: **Bullseye** (15×15×16, 449 balls, a stepped cone whose rings are cut into three sectors, meadow under dome 1), **Mosaic** (13×13×16, 387, a cylinder tiled in 2×2×2 blocks, **cavern** under dome 13), **Pinwheel** (15×15×16, 413, four spiral sectors drawn down a cone, desert under dome 7), **Crown** (15×15×16, 408, a hollow ring in six vertical bars with the drain visible up through the middle, mountain under dome **8**) and **Gem** (17×17×16, 413, an octahedron point-down in concentric *taxicab* rings over a thin four-course column, dream under dome 13). Three of those counts and shapes are #234's second pass, which took Bullseye and Pinwheel from four courses to ten and Gem from six — see "Depth is not what makes a level last" in `docs/formats-and-tools.md` for why the depth alone was not the fix. **The scene and dome named for Mosaic, Pinwheel and Gem here are pre-#194 and stale** — the three moved to the Moon and to the meadow with the blocks; the table in `docs/formats-and-tools.md` is the one to trust. Two of those backdrops were changed after playing them. Mosaic was over the **sea**, which is the wrong backdrop for the brightest cluster in the game twice over — the sea mirrors its dome, so it is a large area of whatever the sky is doing rather than a ground for anything, and against a lit dusk the CMY blocks had nothing to be vivid against; the cavern is enclosed and dark, so the mosaic is the only saturated thing in the frame. Crown was under dome **10**, where the peaks came out pale sand against a candy-pink sky and the whole frame read as kitsch; under 8 they read as snow and the sky as weather. Every one is 16 field levels deep, which is the deepest field that still hangs at `FIELD_TOP_Y` unraised, so they are all framed exactly as One is. Four things govern them and are worth knowing before a sixth is authored:

- **The anchor layer must not be one colour.** Balls hang off the field's *top* level, so a same-colour group that spans it is the last anchor for everything beneath it: one matching ball cuts the whole cluster loose and the level is over on the first lucky shot. Horizontal colour bands — the obvious way to make a striped cake — do exactly that, and both Mosaic and Gem started as banded designs that dropped all 387 and all 295 balls in one shot. Blocks and rings put several colours on the anchor layer instead. The number to check is *the largest same-colour group plus everything it anchors*, as a fraction of the cluster: the pack runs 5–45%, and anything near 90% is a firework rather than a level.
- **The layout must not reach the field's wall.** The field is a box, and a ball on the cluster's flank that is *also* on the wall has no lateral neighbour to offer: aim into that pocket once the cells under it are taken and the shot finds nothing in either ring, does not stick, and bounces off — costing a ball and the streak, which is the trap the landing preview exists for (see "The landing preview" above). The whole pack was drawn straight into it — a disc of radius 5.5 in a 13-wide field reaches the wall on the unshifted levels, and the Gem's taxicab rim reached it on all four sides — and it was **reported from play** as "the ball bounced instead of sticking", on Pinwheel and on Static. Reproduced at one refusal in 34 varied-angle shots, and zero in the same run after the fix. `LevelGen.LateralMargin` refuses it now: every field is two cells wider than its shape needs (the Gem seventeen, its diamond reaching seven cells along each axis where a round 5.5 reaches five), so the shapes are unchanged and the cost is a slightly wider glass plate and a slightly longer camera stand-off. The `[shot]` line in `BallContactEventHandler` is what caught it and is kept for the next one.
- **No ball may stand alone, and the lattice makes that easy to get wrong.** Cells on one level touch only their four **orthogonal** neighbours, never their diagonals, so a colour band one cell wide running diagonally is a string of balls that do not touch each other at all. A lone ball needs *two* landed balls to reach `MINIMUM_CLUSTER_SIZE` — a chore rather than a puzzle, and the one defect here that was found by playing rather than by measuring. The first Gem coloured its diamond by `floor(m/2.2)` on the taxicab radius, which left its top layer's rim as the single shell `m = 7`: twenty-four isolated balls, and being *on* the top layer they had no level above to connect through either. Taxicab and round rings are two units wide now and each layer's rim stops on an odd radius.
- **The magazine draws evenly among the colours still alive, not weighted by how many are left** (`RandomBallType`), so the colour count *is* the difficulty. Three colours means the useful ball arrives a third of the time; the pack uses three except Pinwheel's four and, since #234's second pass, Gem's — the block's last level, where the fourth colour is what buys the groups a sector cut buys the others.
- **Large standing groups are already primed.** A group of ≥ `MINIMUM_CLUSTER_SIZE` sitting in the map does nothing until a ball is *attached* to it, and then the whole thing goes. That is what makes a big-region pattern easy and spectacular at once, and it is why these levels are cleared in a handful of shots against budgets of 40–56.

**Eight and Nine are the two that have to be aimed.** Everything above is built out of plates and wedges of dozens, and a plate is triggered rather than aimed at; **Prism** (15×15×16, 351, a stepped cone in blocks of five colours, sea under dome 13) and **Static** (15×15×16, 370, a cylinder in 2×2×1 blocks and **six** colours scattered, space under dome 1) have five or six colours to draw from, so the wanted ball arrives a fifth or a sixth of the time. **Prism's blocks were one level tall and are two**, which is the "the last storey drags" it was reported as: a one-level block is four balls, and against five colours the widest step — the last thing left standing, since it is the one against the glass — took two dozen shots on its own. Two levels doubles every group to eight without touching the shape or the palette; its largest standing groups went from 13–54 to 29–57 and its best single shot from 3–15% to 8–16%. Static keeps the one-level block, which is what makes it the harder of the two. The scatter is an integer hash of the block's coordinates and not a `Random` walked in loop order — a layout that changes when the emitter's loops are reordered is a layout nobody can reason about — and it is deliberately *not* anti-clustered: the neighbouring blocks that happen to agree merge into eights, and those accidents are the level's only breathing room.

**Ten is the first tall level**, and the mechanic rather than another pattern — see below.

**The Coil is the block that swings** (#207): `Rope`, `Minaret`, `Basket`, `Pendulum` and `Knot`, five layouts in the desert under dome 6, each hanging on links slender enough that the cluster springs when a shot hits it instead of sitting there. It is the one block whose style is a statement about the physics rather than about the silhouette, and it is deliberately **not** tall — that is the Tower's. What it does take from the field is height for a different reason: the layouts are 12 or 14 deep in an **18-level** field, and the six or four empty levels under a layout are its clearance — the lowest ball starts 5.38 above the line rather than the 3.38 a 16-level field would give it. (Until the death line was lowered onto the island, the 18 also *raised* the whole field off the line, which is where the 4.74 this used to quote came from; an 18-level field is pinned at `FIELD_TOP_Y` again now, and the clearance comes from the line being 2 lower instead.) That clearance is what a swinging cluster needs, and it is #203's complaint answered before it arises. See "The coiled levels" in `docs/formats-and-tools.md`.

They were generated rather than drawn — `Tools/LevelGen`, see "The level generator" in `docs/formats-and-tools.md` — and validated before they were shipped: every ball hanging off the glass, no ball standing alone, and the drop test run per colour.

## Tall levels: a column framed from the bottom (Game)

A level may be **deeper than the camera frames**. `FRAMED_LEVELS` (18 since #135, 16 before it) is the tallest slice `FitCannonAndGameCameraToLevel` will fit: a field that deep or shallower is framed whole exactly as it always was, and a deeper one is framed from its floor up to `FramedTopY()` with the rest of it — the cluster's upper reaches and the glass hanging over them — simply above the frame. `Ten.json` (**Column**, 11×11×**34**, a 24-level layout of 540 balls in bands of 45) is the first, and it plays as a column that reaches up out of shot and comes down as the glass descends.

- **Why a cap at all.** Without one `GameCameraFit` does the only honest thing with a taller field and stands back to fit it, which shrinks everything: a level three times as deep is a level rendered a third the size, and the depth buys nothing. Capped, the depth buys what it should — the level's length becomes its *height* rather than its footprint, which is the thing that changes how long a level takes to play.
- **It was sixteen, the deepest field framed whole** (`FIELD_FLOOR_MARGIN`: the two branches of the hang meet at a 16-level field), so the cap changed nothing about any level that existed before it. **Eighteen since #135**, and the two levels are not a retune but the spending of frame that was being wasted: `GameCameraFit` framed the gun as a box of ±`BarrelReach` about its trunnions, and the point a whole barrel's length *below* them is a pose the tube cannot strike — it only ever elevates. The gun's real bottom is its wheels on the island (`ArenaIsland.FloorHeightAt` of its orbit radius, which is `CannonRig.TrunnionHeightAt` read the other way), and the difference was **1.14 world units of nothing**, measured on Onion: the frame's lower bound sat at −10.51 against a ground line of −9.37. Because the frame's *top* is capped, that reservation and not the field was deciding how little of a tall cluster fitted.
- **What the pair actually bought, measured** (1600×900, aimcheck PASS throughout). **Two** (12×12×**18**) is now framed **whole** — it was windowed to y 5.6, and it is one of the two levels #135 named. **Onion** (15×15×27): window top 5.6 → **7.0**, and the camera came **closer**, 31.9 → 30.7, so the picture is slightly *larger* rather than the trade-off a raised cap would normally cost. Every short level moves the same way — **One**: camera 30.9 → 29.7, aim Y −2.8 → −1.0 — because reclaiming the phantom is not the cap's doing and applies to them too. What it is **not** is a cure for a genuinely tall column: Onion's cluster reaches 11 levels above the old window, so two of those eleven is a dent. Standing back to frame such a column whole is the thing this cap exists to refuse.
- **The camera holds still and the column comes down through it.** `FramedTopY` measures the window from the field's *floor*, which does not move, so a mid-level re-solve (a resize) frames the same window it did at load even though the glass has descended. Measured on Column: field top Y 18.33 (raised off the death line), floor −5.00, window top **7.0**, camera 29.3 out at the same lens height as every other level. (Both figures moved with #135 above — they read 5.6 and 29.9 while `FRAMED_LEVELS` was 16 and the gun was framed a barrel's length below its trunnions.)
- **Precise aim converges on the window's middle, not the field's** (`_clusterCentreY` takes `min(topLevel, FRAMED_LEVELS − 1)`). Halfway up a 34-level field is a point the player cannot see, let alone reach.
- **The descent is the ordinary `ceilingStep`, and on a tall level it also answers the state.** Everywhere else the glass is only pressure and a shot-driven cadence is the whole of it. Here it is *also* how the level is delivered — and a delivery driven by the shot count is the wrong shape, because how much of the column a shot takes is not a function of how many shots were fired: one ball into a band cuts fifty loose and lifts the underside four levels at once, and a fixed cadence then leaves the player staring at empty lattice with the next band still overhead. So `FeedTallColumn` runs on every landing and asks for however many descents the underside has climbed since the level was authored (`_feedFloorLevel`, read off the installed map). It only ever **adds** descents, so the shot-driven pressure underneath it is untouched, and it stops at the clearance the level opened with, so it cannot push the cluster past the death line. Steps are **queued**, so a cascade owing ten of them pours down one at a time rather than lurching. It skips an **empty** map: `GetLowestOccupiedLevel` answers a cleared field with the field's top level, which read as the underside having climbed the whole field and queued 20 descents on the frame the column was cleared.
- **The aim is limited to what is framed** (`Cannon.ElevationLimit`, solved by `SolveElevationLimit`). With the gun's full ~80° the player can aim at balls above the frame — shooting blind into the part of the level that has not arrived, which reads as the level being cheatable rather than as a rule. The limit is `AimReachability`'s own geometry run backwards: the steepest *facing* shot, from the stand-off to the field's nearest corner, that still reaches the framed top. Everything inside the window stays reachable, including the near edge, which is the steepest legitimate shot there is. Solved with the fit and never authored, because both its inputs are the fit's. Measured on Column: **61.4°** against the gun's 80.2, with the steepest framed cell asking 57.7.
- **The Testbed's `aimcheck` stopped answering the reachability question for these**, and quietly: it measures the Testbed's own fit, which frames the whole field however deep and therefore stands the gun back far enough to see it — on Column an orbit of 40.3 and a comfortable 43.1°, against the Game's 15.6. So the Game runs `AimReachability` itself at every level load off the geometry it actually uses, against `ElevationLimit` rather than the hardware clamp, and **over the framed window only** on a tall field (its upper levels are unreachable now *by design* — that is what the limit enforces and what the descent undoes, and asking about them returns "unfinishable" for a level that finishes).
  - **That line was wrong the first two times it was printed, and the fix is worth knowing.** `AimReachability` measured `GetRealCenteredPosition` in the **lattice** frame while the Game hangs level 0 at `_clusterWorldOffset` — so every cell was measured up to five units above where it hangs and every required elevation came out too steep. It reported Column's top cell at Y 23.3 when it hangs at 18.3, and called the level unfinishable. `Check` now takes the offset (defaulting to zero, which is the Testbed's case, where the lattice frame *is* the world frame). Re-measured after the fix, the whole set asks **53.6–65.3°** of the 80.2 clamp where the broken frame had claimed 76–77.

- **The order lives in the set, never in the directory listing.** A filesystem has no order worth relying on, and reading the directory would silently promote whatever was dropped into it. Entries are objects rather than bare strings so a level can carry a display name now and more later without breaking a file that predates them — the unlock rule (`minStars`, #111) arrived exactly this way; paths are relative to the set's own directory (captured in `LevelSet.Directory` at load), so a set and its levels move together and nothing depends on the working directory. `LevelSet.Load` validates and throws, mirroring `Level.Load`, and `IsLevelSetFile` is the same cheap marker probe `Level.IsLevelFile` is.
- **A level file or a plain map file, both `.json`, so the loader probes** — `InstallLevel` runs `Level.IsLevelFile` exactly as the Testbed's `DeserializeMapFromFile` does. A full level's scene and sky are applied; a plain map leaves whatever the player picked from the menu standing. Every level in the set is a full level file now; a plain map (`sx`/`sz`/`l`/`b`) still loads and still plays in whichever scene is up, which is what `One.json` did before it was regenerated.
- **Clearing the field wins the level, and winning advances through the set.** The condition is `GetRemovableBallsCount() == 0`, tested **only on a landing** (`CheckLevelCleared`) — that is the one thing which can empty the field, and polling it per frame would declare a level authored with no balls won before a shot was fired, and keep declaring it. It was `GetBallsCount() == 0` until #323, and the count had to change the moment a ball could exist that no colour removes: a level holding one `BallKind.Rock` is never cleared under the ball count — the player runs out of things to shoot at and the level simply does not end — while the out-of-shots test below reads the *same* count and declares that level lost instead. Two conditions, one wrong number, opposite failures, which is why `BallsMap.GetRemovableBallsCount` is one method both of them call. What is left standing when the count reaches zero is cut loose (`ReleaseAllBalls` — by construction nothing matchable remains, so "everything" is the rocks) rather than left hanging over the celebration, and it scores nothing: the player did not clear it, it merely stopped having anything to hang from. The end is then held for `LEVEL_CLEARED_BEAT`: balls leave the map at *release* time while their bodies are still falling, so ending the level the instant the last group is cut takes the collapse the player earned off the screen before they see it. The completion bonus is awarded at the moment of clearing, not at the end of that beat, so nothing during it can eat the balls they finished with.
- **A decided level closes the gun, and that has to be said at the input rather than left to the stack** (#176, #177). Clearing the field pushes no screen for the whole `LEVEL_CLEARED_BEAT` — longer while a drop cinematic holds the countdown — so `MenuPage.UpdatesUnderlying`, the mechanism that stops this screen for a pause (#79), is not reached yet — and since #241 the result screen does not stop it at all. For that entire beat `Shoot` used to fire normally: the shot was scored as spent against a level already won, left the barrel, and landed and resolved contacts on a field nobody was playing. `GameplayScreen.LevelDecided` is that one condition named once, and `Shoot`, the landing preview and the magazine's transmute all refuse on it. It is the same shape of gap #79 found in the pause frame, reached at the other end of a level.
    - **Its third term is `LevelOver`, and leaving it out is a live bug rather than a tidiness question.** The cleared beat ends by counting its own countdown down to zero, so `_levelLost || _clearedCountdown > 0f` reads a *cleared* level as undecided again from the frame its page goes up — which was harmless only while that page froze the screen. It no longer does (#241): a shot still in the air lands under the page, and `CheckLevelCleared` would find an empty field and start the whole celebration a second time. `LevelOver` (`_pendingOutcome != None`) is the line past which the level's arithmetic is read-only, because `LevelResult` was taken off it — so the late landing scores nothing, the culled shot scores no miss, and the ball simply sticks in silence.
- **`BuildLevel(index)` is the one path** a first Play, a New Game, a retry and an advance all take. `FinishLevel` marks the clear — clearing the field *is* passing the level, the per-level `minScore` gate it used to weigh (`ShortOfGate`) having been retired with #111 — and hands the player the result screen, whose buttons hold the real build, teardown and advance: Retry rebuilds the same entry, Next Level (offered only when a next entry exists **and** the star total opens it) builds the next, Main Menu tears the session down so the front end offers a new game rather than "Continue" into a level that is already over. A campaign cleared to its end is said plainly ("Campaign complete"). `FinishLevel` runs **last** in `Update`, because what follows from an ending may tear the session down, and anything above it that frame would then be working against a simulation that no longer exists.
- **A level's rules ride on its set entry** — `shots` (the ball budget), `minStars` (the campaign star total *this* entry unlocks at, #111) and `ceilingStep` (shots between descents of the glass), all optional. `LoadLevelSet` logs every entry's rules at startup, spelling each absent one out as what it means ("unlimited shots, ceiling holds", nothing for a gate of zero), so an inconsistently authored set is obvious in one place before a level is played. All three are consumed: the budget is the HUD's "balls left" and, unspent, the completion bonus; the star gate is read by the picker's locks and by the result screen's Next Level, never by the level being played (see "Scoring" for the rating and the persistence); and `ceilingStep` steps the glass down every so many shots towards a fixed death line, which is one of the two ways a level is lost (`[ceiling] Step to …` and `[level] Lost … ClusterReachedLine` are its log lines). `shots` and `ceilingStep` are **not independent**: the descent is driven by shots against a fixed death line, so the geometry implies a budget of its own, and `LevelSet` deliberately does not try to cross-check them (the implied limit is the game's geometry, not the set's data).
  - **What that implied limit actually is, and the four levels that fell foul of it (#288).** It is one sum, and nothing checks it for you: `final headroom = clearance - floor(shots / ceilingStep) * 0.60`, where the clearance is the `[field]` line's own "above the line". It has to clear a **swing**, and how deep a swing goes is measured, not guessed — the probe recorded beside `GameplayScreen.CLUSTER_SWING_ALLOWANCE` took 35 swings over 67 s on Chest and the deepest was **0.82** units below the trend. Measured before the fix: **Pylon -1.04, Ghost +0.36, Orrery +0.37, Cube +1.18**. Pylon was not a tight margin but the two clocks disagreeing outright — an *untouched* cluster is past the line on its eleventh descent, at shot 66 of a 74-shot budget, so the last eight shots could not be played however well they were aimed, which is exactly the fault `LevelGen`'s header records for the pictures. `CeilingStep` was the lever on all four (6->9, 6->7, 8->9, 9->14), leaving 1.36 / 1.57 / 1.78 / 1.56 — clear of both the measured swing and the 1.00 allowance. (#301 has since moved two of those levels again, and by the layout rather than the step: Pylon hangs four levels shorter and starts 8.90 over the line, and Orrery's stack is one level a gap on a step of 14. Cabinet — never in #288's four — turned out to be under this very sum at step 12 all along and plays at 16. The current arithmetic lives in each design's own comment, and the sag gate in `docs/formats-and-tools.md` is what measures the part this sum cannot.) **A low figure is not by itself the fault**, which is what stops this being a gate: Horn and Turbine both sit at **-0.23** and neither has ever been complained about, because their lowest ball is a tip that goes in the first shots and the true lowest point then jumps several units up. The sum is honest about the cluster *as authored*; what a competent run leaves hanging is the designer's judgement.
- **A cluster that merely swings has not lost, and that took two goes to get right** (#239). A hanging cluster oscillates about its own descending trend, and the loss test reads the lowest ball's **live** pose — so a body still comfortably above the line ended the level in the instant of a swing's bottom. It was first reported when the line stood at −5.5 and answered by moving the line *down* onto the island; the owner then reported it again on `Chest`, and by then that lever was spent, because `CEILING_DEATH_Y` cannot go below `ArenaIsland.TOP_Y + 1` without the laser net drawing inside the island cap it hovers over ("The floor's laser net" in `docs/game-feedback.md`). So the rule stopped reading an instant instead. A ball more than `CLUSTER_SWING_ALLOWANCE` past the line loses at once — nothing that deep is a swing — and otherwise the line has to be **held** for `CLUSTER_BELOW_LINE_GRACE` without once coming back up. Both are one unit and one second, and both are measured rather than picked: a probe on `Chest` (the level it was reported on, and the second-heaviest cluster in the pack) fired a shot every 0.7 s and stepped the ceiling every 2 s, and the lowest ball **detrended against a centred moving average** gave 35 swings over 67 s — deepest **0.82 units**, longest **0.76 s**, median 0.40 s. The detrending is the part worth copying: the baseline descends all level, so a raw minimum reads the whole descent as one enormous dip and measures nothing at all. Verified in the running game — a genuine descent still loses, and the `[level] Lost` line now says which of the two clauses fired (`held for 1.00 s (grace 1.00 s)`).
- **Nothing about a missing or broken level is fatal.** `LoadLevelSet` runs once in `LoadContent`, so a bad set is reported at startup rather than at the moment Play is pressed; the set staying null, or a level failing to parse, falls back to `BuildFallbackMap` — the procedural pyramid, kept for exactly this. Measured: with the set present, `One.json` gives 315 balls / 1088 constraints; with the `Levels` directory gone, the fallback gives 285 / 1377, which are the figures the pre-level build had.
- **The field's size and depth are no longer constants.** `FitFieldToMap` derives them per level: the field's topmost level hangs at `FIELD_TOP_Y` — **or higher, when the field is deep enough that its bottom level would otherwise start past the death line**: the top is `max(FIELD_TOP_Y, CEILING_DEATH_Y + FIELD_FLOOR_MARGIN + topLevel/√2)`, with the margin a ball's radius, so a ball hung in the field's lowest cell rests its surface exactly on the line, alive and one descent from loss. The whole field is playable by construction however deep it is, and the empty levels an author leaves under a layout are the level's *starting clearance*, not dead space past the line — pinning the top unconditionally meant any field deep enough for its bottom to reach past the line started with cells below it, and a 30×30×30 map with only its top levels occupied **lost the level on the frame it was built** (`ClusterReachedLine`, a ball at −6.36 against the −5.5 the line then stood at, before a shot was fired). How deep that is moves with the line: 17 levels then, 19 now. Measured after the rule: the same 30-level map hangs at top Y 13.51 with its lowest ball 8.99 above the line and plays — **a raised field's clearance does not change when the line does**, its floor being pinned a radius over the line wherever that is, so only its world Y moved; `One.json`, `Colossus.json` and the fallback are in the figures below. A `[field]` line logs each level's top, floor and lowest-ball clearance, which is the figure an author sizing a deep map actually wants. `_ceilingY`, `_clusterCentreY` and the glass plate's footprint follow from the solved top. `FitCeilingToMap` rebuilds the plate's mesh and renderer per level (through the host's `RebuildCeilingRenderer`, since the glass is lit with the rest of the scene — through the rig's glass push, `SkyLightRig.ApplyToGlass`, since #156), which is why that push tolerates a null renderer and `BuildLevel` runs `ApplySkyLighting` again afterwards — the same dance the Testbed does around its own `FitCeilingToMap`.
- **`_clusterWorldOffset` gained an X and a Z, and that is the parity trap finally handled rather than avoided.** `BallsMap.Center` offsets by the top level's bounding-box *half-extent* less a ball radius, which lands on the origin only when that level is a shifted (odd-index) one whose cells start at index 0. An **odd** field tops out on an unshifted level, whose cells run 0…N−1 rather than 0.5…N−0.5, and the whole cluster then hangs half a unit off the axis the gun orbits and the camera looks down. That used to be a rule the hard-coded field satisfied by hand; a level is authored elsewhere and cannot be held to it — `One.json` is fifteen levels deep — so the residual is measured off the centred top level through `GetRealCenteredPosition` and folded into the offset the physics builder and the contact handler already take. No new frame crossing, and it is exactly zero for the old even-field pyramid.
- **The magazine's colours come off the loaded map** (`RecountBallTypes`), not from a fixed list: loading a colour that is nowhere in the cluster is a shot that cannot be spent, and an authored level picks its own palette (`Colossus.json` uses six). `RandomBallType` stopped being static for it. An empty map falls back to the default four.
- **The camera and the gun are solved to the field, not tuned to it** — `FitCannonAndGameCameraToLevel`, run on every level load and every aspect change, over the shared `GameCameraFit.Solve` (since #76 the Testbed's copy and this one are the same solver; see "The gun's hardware, in one copy" in `docs/testbed.md`). `_gameCameraDistance` and `_gameCameraTargetY` replaced the two constants: the stand-off is **bisected** for the smallest distance at which the field's eight corners, the top of the glass and a box around the gun all land inside *both* frustum half-angles, and the aim height comes from bisecting the resulting elevation range, which is what centres the subject. The gun is then placed `GameCameraFit.CANNON_CAMERA_STANDOFF` in front of the lens — off the *lens*, not the field, because the magazine showing through its slot is really a HUD element and has to keep its size on screen — held off by two lower bounds (clear the field's footprint; keep the resting aim inside the elevation clamp) whose binding one changes with the level. Each solve depends on the other, so they alternate three rounds, and it very nearly settles on the first — but not exactly, and both copies used to claim it did: a sub-0.1 residual survives to the third, in both directions. What this file assigns is three values the solve *returns*; the gun is moved **once**, after it, and no longer parked at each intermediate guess.
  - **The one thing that differs between the two callers is the Y frame**, which is exactly why the field's vertical extent goes into `Solve` as two arguments and never as an assumption. In the Testbed the lattice frame *is* the world frame, so the field's floor is `y = 0`; here level 0 sits at `_clusterWorldOffset.Y`. The empty growth levels are deliberately inside the fitted volume — the cluster grows down into them, so they have to be in frame before the first ball lands there.
  - Measured on the shipped levels, at the default window and 16:9, 2026-09-04 (`play level=<name>`, off the `[camera]` line): 12×12×16 (`One`) → 30.3 out, aim Y −1.2, gun orbit 15.5, walk 15.5..19.5; 12×12×18 (`Colossus`, `Cube`) → 28.8, aim Y −2.0, orbit 15.5, walk 15.5..19.5; 15×15×27 framed to its lowest 18 (`Onion`) → 29.1, aim Y −1.9, orbit 15.5, same walk; 17×17×18 (`Donut`, `Elephant`, `Trophy` — the widest field the set has) → 30.5, aim Y −1.1, orbit 15.5, same walk; 11×11×34 framed to 18 (`Ten`, `Column`) → 27.8, aim Y −2.5, orbit 15.5, same walk.
    - **Every level in the campaign rests at 15.5, and so does the near end of its walk** — that is `FUNNEL_TOP_RADIUS` + `CANNON_DRAIN_CLEARANCE`, so the drain places the gun on all of them and **the shipped campaign has no forward half to its walk at all**: W does nothing from the rest stance and S is a retreat that is walked back. It is arithmetic rather than a coincidence of level design — the stand-off bound would put the gun at `distance − CANNON_CAMERA_STANDOFF`, and the widest field in the set frames at exactly 30.5, so that bound never reaches past the drain's 15.5. A level would have to frame further out than 30.5 to buy one.
    - **The set above replaced one that had gone quietly stale**, and the cause is worth knowing: it read 9×9×16 → 32.9 out / orbit 17.9, 10×10×15 (`One.json` as it then was) → 33.2 / 18.2, 12×12×18 (`Colossus.json`) → 35.5 / 20.5, 20×20×20 → 39.7 / 24.7. The same `Colossus` field measures 28.8 / 15.5 today — nearly 7 units of stand-off — because **#135** stopped reserving a barrel's length of frame *below* the trunnions and spent it on the field, which let the lens come in; the table was simply never re-measured after it. (The pre-dome set — 30.8/15.9, 31.2/16.2, 33.4/18.4, 37.7/22.6 — and the pre-dish set before it — 27.6/13.5, 28.0/13.5, 30.9/16.0, 36.8/21.7 — are kept as what those hoists measured.) `One.json` at 21:9 (aspect 2.31, resized live) gave **the same out-distance as its 16:9 fit of the day** (31.2, pre-dome), which is the vertical axis binding at every targeted aspect exactly as "Resolution and aspect" in `docs/rendering.md` says it should — the extra width goes to scenery.
    - **The lens follows a retreat and not an advance** (`GameCameraFit.CANNON_ADVANCE_FOLLOW`, #322). The gun backs off *towards* the lens, so before this the walk's far end left it 8.3 to 9.3 units away — nearer than the 11 the stroke cap was sized for, because that figure only ever held where the stand-off bound had placed the gun, which on this set is nowhere. Measured at the far end of the walk: `Ten` 27.8 → 30.5 out (the gun 12.3 → 11.0 ahead of the lens, where before it stood 8.3), `Colossus` 28.8 → 30.8 (13.3 → 11.3, before 9.3). Half the retreat on a wide level, more where the 11-unit floor takes over; an advance moves the lens not at all, and neither does anything in height.

## Scoring (Game)

The rules for what a shot is worth live in **`Prazsky.BS3D/Scoring/ScoreKeeper.cs`** — no MonoGame, no Bepu, no map, no frame. It is fed three events and answers what a HUD and a result screen need. That placement is the point: the rules are the part of this game most likely to be argued about and retuned, and they have no business being another few hundred lines inside a `Game` subclass.

- **Two kinds of released ball, and the difference is the skill.** `BallsConstraintsBuilder.ReleaseSameTypeCluster` used to return `cluster.Count + disconnected.Count`, one number; it now returns a **`BallsReleased`** pair. *Matched* is the group the shot completed (10 points); *orphaned* is everything that fell afterwards because that group was its last anchor (**20** — double, because cutting a support and collapsing half the cluster is the shot worth learning). Orphans cannot happen without a match: the disconnected walk only runs once a group has actually been taken out.
- **The streak.** A scoring shot is awarded at the multiplier standing when it lands and *then* raises it — by **two** since #173, so the ramp is ×1 → ×3 → ×5 rather than four landings long (see "A cleared level is rated" below for why the length of that ramp decided the star rating) — capped at ×5, so the shot that starts a streak still scores at ×1 and it is continuing that pays. `Multiplier` therefore reads **forward** (what the next scoring shot will get), which is why `Landed` returns a `ScoreAward` carrying the multiplier actually applied rather than leaving a popup to read the property after the fact. Any shot that drops nothing resets it to ×1.
- **The three events do not arrive together, and wiring any two to one place would be wrong.** A ball is spent the instant it leaves the barrel (`Shot()`, in `Shoot`); what it *did* takes a physics step or more to resolve (`Landed`); a shot that misses everything resolves later still (`Missed`).
- **Every shot resolves exactly once, and `ContactEvents.IsListener` is that guarantee for free.** A shot ball is a listener from `Shoot` until its shot is over — on attaching, on touching something it cannot attach to, or on being culled. So `BallContactEventHandler` reads *whether it was still listening* before the unregister that its own static-contact branch performs, and the kill-plane cull in `RemoveFallenBalls` reports a miss only for a ball that is still a listener. No per-ball state anywhere. **A miss is resolved at the moment the ball strikes the island, not when it is finally culled** — the player knows they missed instantly, so the streak has to break then; the roll down the stone dish and out through the drain that follows is scenery, not scoring. It also closes the rare case of a shot that comes to rest on the island anyway (a pile propping itself up near the mouth) and is therefore never culled at all.
- **The handler reports; it does not score.** `BallLanded` and `ShotSpent` are events on it, and the game routes them through methods that read the `_score` field rather than binding the instance — the scorer is replaced per level, and a handler holding a stale one would score into a keeper nothing reads.
- The budget comes from the level's set entry (`shots`, null = unlimited). `Shot()` clamps at zero and **refuses nothing**: ending a level when the budget is spent is the lose condition's job (#58), not the scorer's.
- `BaseScore` is what the score would have been at ×1 throughout, so `StreakBonus` is exactly the difference — the line a result screen wants. `CompletionBonus`/`AwardCompletionBonus` (unused shots × 50) exist for #56/#59 and are called by nothing yet; a failed level must never get them.
- Nothing displays any of this. A temporary `[score]` line logs each scoring shot until the HUD (#60) exists — a score that is counted but never seen is indistinguishable from one that is not counted.

**A cleared level is rated 1–4 stars, and the stars are what the campaign counts** (#111, `Scoring/StarRating.cs` beside the keeper, because it is the same kind of thing: a rule that will be argued about and retuned). The rating's yardstick is the level's own **floor**: a cleared field has by construction released every one of the balls it started with, and each released ball scores at least `MatchedBallPoints` at ×1 — so no clear of an N-ball level can score under `10 × N`, one star is "cleared" by construction, and the 2/3/4-star thresholds are the score as a *multiple* of that floor (1.8 / 3.0 / 4.5). That self-calibrates across levels of any size with nothing per-level to author.

**Those thresholds are 1.8 / 4.0 / 6.0 since #173, and they were MEASURED — the first pass was reasoned from the rules and was ordered backwards.** Driving the real `ScoreKeeper` over all thirteen shipped levels showed the rating **rising with the number of shots taken, on every one of them**: clearing One in four shots scored 3.08× and grinding it over twenty-nine scored 4.71×, so the play style four of these levels are *designed* around was the one the rating punished. Thresholds could not fix that — they sit downstream of a score that already rewarded the wrong thing, and any values letting a fast clear reach four stars handed four stars to a grind as well. Two causes, both now fixed in `ScoreKeeper`:

- **The streak's ramp was amortised over the shot count.** A level's total base points barely change with how it is cleared — every ball drops either way — so the score is really `base × the average multiplier`, and starting at ×1 costs a four-shot clear a quarter of its shots and a thirty-shot clear a thirtieth. `MultiplierStep` is **2**, so the ramp is two landings rather than four and most of that dilution goes with it.
- **The completion bonus was a flat 50 a shot**, which is a fifth of an average shot on One and a fiftieth of one on Onion — a different statement on every level and too small on all of them. `UnusedShotValue` is `UnusedShotWorthInShots` (**4**) × the balls an average shot of *that* level would have cleared, so "not needing a shot is worth several shots" means the same thing everywhere. It needs the level's ball count, which is why the keeper now takes one.

Re-measured after both, the band the thresholds are set against is: **~1.9** for the whole budget spent with one shot in three missing, **~5.0** for the whole budget spent cleanly (patient rather than skilful), and **6.5–7.9** cleared in a handful of shots without missing. So two stars is unchanged, three moves 3.0 → 4.0 so a clean grind lands there, and four moves 4.5 → **6.0**, which now takes efficiency rather than patience. On every level and both orphan assumptions the full-budget clear is now the **lowest** of the play styles rather than the highest. One residue is left and is small: an eight-shot clear still edges a four-shot one by ~3 %, because even a two-landing ramp is a larger fraction of four shots than of eight. **Retune these against the harness, not against intuition** — that is the mistake being corrected. The count the floor is sized from is `_initialBallCount`, captured at install because by the time a rating is wanted the map is empty. A failed level is not rated at all (no stars shown, nothing recorded) — a loss is not a rating of zero. Verified end to end on a one-shot test level: 51 matched at ×1 plus 4×50 unused = 710 against a floor of 500, rated 1 star, recorded and written.

**The bests persist, and the star total is the unlock currency** (#92, `Levels/PlayerProgress.cs`): `%LOCALAPPDATA%\BS3D\Progress.json` — under the player's own profile since #353, because it used to sit in the build output where a `dotnet clean` took it — holds each entry's best score and best stars, keyed by the entry's *file* (the identifier that survives a renamed display name and a regenerated set). The write is atomic and keeps one generation back as `.bak`, and the load says whether an empty result is a first run or a save it had to throw away; see "`PlayerProgress`" in `docs/formats-and-tools.md` for both. Its load is **lenient** where the set's throws — a missing save is a first run and a corrupt one must cost stars, never the game — and it is written the moment a clear improves a best (`BS3DGame.RecordLevelResult`, inside `ShowResultScreen`, the one funnel both endings pass through), *before* the next entry's gate is read, so a clear that pushes the total over the gate unlocks Next Level on the very screen announcing it. The picker locks entries whose `minStars` the total has not reached (disabled button, "Unlocks at N ★"), shows each cleared entry's best stars, and a settings row ("Reset progress", two clicks — the second confirms) empties the save.

**⚠ Since #347 the star total is not the whole gate: the campaign also opens one level at a time** (`BS3DGame.IsLevelUnlocked`, the owner's ruling). An entry is playable when the total reaches its `minStars` **and** it is no further than the *first uncleared* entry — `FirstUnclearedLevel`, the campaign's frontier. The star gate alone is a running sum, so a player banking four stars a level ran ahead of themselves: levels 40 and 41 stood open while 37 had never been played, and the campaign could be finished with holes in it, which is precisely what let CAMPAIGN COMPLETE fire on an incomplete campaign. The star gate is kept underneath rather than replaced — it is what sends a struggling player back to rate a level better before the next opens, and it is the economy the set's `minStars` are authored against. **A level already cleared stays open whatever the sequence says**, and that clause is load-bearing rather than tidy: the rule landed on a campaign played under the old one, and the author's save held 65 cleared levels with its frontier at 38, so without it twenty-eight beaten levels would have shut and read as lost progress. The picker says which lock it is — `Locked · #38 first` on a tile, `the campaign opens one level at a time; level 38 'Fault' is next` in the detail line — because a tile quoting a star price the player already holds reads as a bug rather than as a rule.

**And the sequence rule needed a relief valve, which is #347's other half: the bounded skip.** A level the player cannot finish used to be something the star economy let them route around; under the sequence rule it is a wall across the whole campaign. So a failed level offers **Skip** beside Retry, `BS3DGame.SKIPS_PER_BLOCK` is **one per chapter** (per campaign on a set with no chapters, where a per-block budget would be one skip per level and so no budget at all), and `PlayerProgress.Skipped` remembers it — a separate list from the bests, because a skip has no best to record; null until the first skip, so a save without one round-trips exactly as before. **A skip is not a clear**: no score, no stars, nothing towards a gate. All it does is let the frontier past, and the level stays playable for ever — the picker's tile says `Skipped`, which is the only place in the game that level can be found again. Two rules keep it honest. It **may not smuggle the player past the star gate** (`CanSkipLevel` refuses when the next entry's `minStars` is unmet), or the button would drop them into a level the picker calls locked. And the campaign's ending still belongs to a clear: `WouldCompleteCampaign` counts a skip as finished — the campaign is complete when every level is cleared *or* skipped — but it is only ever asked from `CheckLevelCleared`, so the confetti never falls on a skip. A player whose *last* unfinished level is one they skip therefore ends the campaign without the moment, which is deliberate.

**The magazine may only load a colour that is still hanging.** `RecountBallTypes` refills `_ballsOfType` off the map every time a shot lands, and `RandomBallType` draws evenly among the types whose count is above zero. That is the **next-colour policy handed to the shared `Magazine` at construction**, and it is the whole of the difference between this magazine and the Testbed's, which draws uniformly over all thirteen because its cluster is whatever map was dropped on it: answering "what is still alive?" needs a map, a level and its rules, so the shared type takes the answer as a delegate rather than learning any of the three. Without it the queue goes on offering a colour the player has eliminated, and a ball of a colour that exists nowhere can never match anything — it can only be parked, which grows the cluster the player is shrinking, wastes a budgeted shot and in the limit makes a level **unwinnable**. Recounted rather than maintained incrementally on purpose: the walk is the field's cell count (1500 for `One.json`) and runs per landing, not per frame, while a running total would be three places to get wrong (release, attach, second-ring fallback) for microseconds nobody can measure. A colour with fewer than `MINIMUM_CLUSTER_SIZE` left is arguably dead weight already and is deliberately *not* dropped — that changes which levels are solvable, so it is a difficulty decision rather than this fix. **A ball only counts if its kind lets it be matched** (`BallKinds.Matchable`, #323): a rock wears a colour because every cell does, and counting it would keep that colour alive in the queue with nothing in the cluster able to complete it — the exact waste this census exists to prevent, arriving through the one ball that cannot be shot down.

**Special ball kinds: a ball is a colour *and* a kind (#323).** `BallType` is the thirteen colours and stays that; `BallKind` is what a ball *is* beside its colour, and a map carries it as one optional key per cell (see "Levels and serialization" in `docs/formats-and-tools.md`). Adding a fourteenth `BallType` instead would have broken six things at once and none of them loudly — `BallTypes.Count` sizes the render buckets, `BasicEffectParamsProvider` owes every member a tint, the census above would count it, `RandomBallType` would *load* it, `Transmute` would re-colour balls to it, and the generator reasons in thirteen inks. The match rule reads the kind in exactly one place: `BallsMap.GetConnectedSameTypeCells` skips a ball `BallKinds.Matchable` says no to, so an unmatchable ball stops the flood fill exactly as an empty cell does and is never counted into a group nor released by one. **Since #326 there is a second removal path beside it** — a blast, which chooses its victims by geometry rather than by colour and then runs the same disconnection pass; see the Bomb below.

- **`BallKind.Rock` (#324)** is the first of them: it matches with nothing, no colour removes it, and it leaves the cluster only as collateral when a match elsewhere cuts its last anchor — which needed no code at all, because `GetCellsDisconnectedFromCeiling` has never cared what kind a ball is. That is the whole of its removal rule; everything else about it is the two conditions above reading the right count. A rock is a **wall**: it changes the shape of the problem rather than the colour arithmetic. `Testbed\Maps\Rocks.json` is the test field — 196 balls, 13 of them rock, a block inside the yellow plane and four corners under it.

- **`BallKind.Transparent` (#325)** hangs with no colour and takes one the moment a shot lands beside it — the shot's own — and from that instant it is an ordinary ball of that colour: it matches, it counts, it scores. It is the kind that *stops being one*, and three rules carry it, each stated where it lives. **It matches on the very shot that coloured it**: `BallContactEventHandler.ProcessContact` colours between the attach and the group check, so a shot that turns two glass balls into its own colour and takes all three away is one landing and not two — which is the shot the kind exists to make worth aiming, and it is measured (`[shot] coloured 4 transparent ball(s) Type5; released 5 matched`). **It takes the shot ball's colour, and the landing decides where** (`BallsMap.ColourTransparentGroup`), because a landing is a *place* and that is the one rule the player can watch happen; the colour of the group that fell, or of the nearest coloured ball, would each fire somewhere nobody was looking. **Every transparent neighbour takes it**, not a chosen one — choosing would be invisible dice, two identical-looking landings doing different things. ⚠ **And since #344 it does not stop at the neighbours: the colour runs through the whole connected body of glass**, which *retracts* #325's own ruling that scoped it to the one pane touched. The owner's verdict was that a single recoloured ball buried among still-colourless neighbours is a ball the player can do nothing with, so a pane of glass becomes a same-coloured group in one landing, and by the ordinary match rule usually takes what it was holding up with it. That makes one shot much larger — the Mirage's Solitaire has a landing that colours 116 balls — and it did not move *where* the step sits: the colouring still finishes before anything is counted, so the census and the group check never see a half-coloured pane. ⚠ It is also the kind that **split `BallKinds.Matchable` in two**: a glass ball is *not* matchable (it has no colour the player can aim at, so it is out of the flood fill and out of the magazine census) and *is* **removable** — one shot beside it makes it ordinary — so it holds the level open exactly as an ordinary ball does. `BallKinds.Removable` is that second question and the two end-of-level conditions ask it; merging them again would make a level with one glass ball in it permanently unfinished, which is the rock's own bug arriving through the opposite door. `Testbed\Maps\Glass.json` is the test field — a white plate over a white ring holding a 3×3 of glass, plus one glass ball in the anchor plate no shot is meant to reach. **⚠ The score does not reward the colouring itself, and that is a ruling rather than an omission** (the owner's, 2026-09-01): a colouring pays exactly when it *releases* something — the balls it coloured are ordinary by the time the group check runs, so a group they complete arrives in `Released.Matched` and scores like any other, and larger for having them in it — while a colouring that completes nothing is a shot that stuck, scoring nothing and taking the streak multiplier with it through `ScoreKeeper.Landed`'s `matched <= 0` door. The whole rule is carried by *where* the colouring sits (before the group check) rather than by any term of its own; a term would pay the player twice for one shot.

- **`BallKind.Bomb` (#326)** detonates when a shot lands in a cell **beside** it and takes out every ball within `BallsConstraintsBuilder.BLAST_RADIUS` (2 world units — two rings sideways, nearly three levels up and down) of itself, of every colour and every kind, itself included. It is the first kind that removes a ball the match rule never touched, and it is the foundation #327 (Zap) and #328 (Acid) inherit from. Its two predicate answers are the transparent ball's arriving for a different reason: **not matchable** (it is not a colour, so it is out of the flood fill and out of the magazine census, and like the rock it carries a `BallType` nothing may read) and **removable** (the player always has a path — land beside it), so a bomb left standing does not hold the level open.
- **`BallKind.Zap` (#327)** shares the bomb's trigger exactly — a shot landing in a cell **beside** it — and takes **every ordinary ball of one colour off the whole field**, wherever it is. It is the bomb's destruction path at the largest scale the game has: what changes is that the set of cells is chosen by *colour* rather than by geometry, and after that the two are the same three steps (remove, throw so they fall rather than vanish, run the disconnection pass). Same two predicate answers as the bomb, and it reached `Removable` without that file being edited — which is the "everything but the rock" default doing the job it was written for.
  - **The colour is the SHOT's**, and that is this kind's one real decision. It is the only colour the player *chose*: the commonest colour on the field, a colour written on the ball, or a neighbour's would each be a ball the player gambles with or reads an answer off rather than one they aim. The magazine shows what is loaded three balls ahead, so "which colour will this take" is answerable before firing — which is the whole difference between a special and a lottery.
  - **⚠ It takes only `Matchable` balls**, so rocks, glass, bombs and other zaps are untouched whatever `BallType` they carry. Not a special case: it is this codebase's rule stated a third time — a kind that is not matchable carries a colour *nothing may read*. It also keeps the two specials from eating each other, which is what lets the landing order below be a ruling rather than a race.
  - **The zap fires BEFORE the blast** when one landing arms both (`BallContactEventHandler.ProcessContact`, and `SagProbe` repeats the order). A blast that ate the zap ball first would silently swallow one of the two effects the player armed with one shot; a zap can never take a bomb, so wide-then-local is the order in which both always happen.
  - **What it costs the rest of the game is the size of one removal.** A level drawn in three inks can lose one of them in a frame and most of its picture with it — the largest mass change the sag probe exists to measure (#301, #302), and the probe now plays it. On the magazine side `_ballsOfType` for that colour goes to zero the same frame, so `Transmute` re-colours every loaded slot wearing it at once; that is safe by construction rather than by luck, because `_magazineTransmute`/`_magazineFrom` are **per slot**, so five slots crossing over at once are five independent timers. And zapping the last live colour is `AnyBallTypeAlive`'s existing guard: the transmute is skipped and the clear takes over, which is the path #176 already had.
  - **No shipped level carries one**, deliberately and on #326's precedent: this issue built the mechanic, and a chapter that uses it is its own work. `Testbed\Maps\Zaps.json` is the test field — an anchor check of two colours, a middle course of a third carrying three zaps and one bomb, and a skirt that hangs off the middle course alone, so a zap on that colour orphans it and a zap on another does not.
  - **What is hit is a PLACE, not a ball**, which is the transparent ball's rule with a different effect on the end of it. The player aims at the gap beside a ball and never at a ball itself, so "when it is hit" can only mean *when a shot lands next to it* — and every bomb beside the cell goes, not a chosen one, for `ColourTransparentGroup`'s own reason: choosing among several would be invisible dice. (The bomb's reach stops at the cell's neighbours; it is the *colour* that runs through connected glass, not the blast through connected bombs — a chain of those is a chain of detonations, each with its own radius.)
  - **The order inside a landing is: colour the glass → complete the group → detonate**, and both halves of that are decisions. The armed bombs are collected **before** the release, so a bomb the release *orphans* has already fallen and is skipped rather than going off in mid-air; and the shot's own match runs **first**, because a player who lands beside a bomb while completing a group has earned that group and a blast that ate it before it was counted would read as the game refusing a good shot. The two compose, in the order the player did them.
  - **Destruction is not release, and that is the substance.** Everything the game took away before this left through `ReleaseSameTypeCluster` — a group of one colour plus whatever the disconnection walk reported. A blast's victims were never a group, so the path is **choose a set of cells by geometry, remove them, and run the disconnection pass over what is left** (`BallsConstraintsBuilder.DetonateBombs`). The radius is a lattice walk and not an index range: odd levels are shifted half a cell and the levels sit 1/√2 apart, so the index range only *bounds* the walk and every candidate is measured with `BallsMap.GetRealPosition`. **Blasts chain**, through a worklist rather than recursion — a bomb inside another blast is queued and deliberately *not* destroyed as a victim, so it still gets to go off, and it destroys itself when popped.
  - **The victims fall, thrown outward** rather than vanishing. A ball that pops out of existence throws away the best feedback this game has, and the falling-ball machinery, the drain, the sound and the drop cinematic all already exist for it. The bomb itself is at distance zero and has no outward direction, so it goes **down**.
  - **A destroyed ball is a third scoring category** (`BallsReleased.Destroyed`, `ScoreKeeper.DestroyedBallPoints`), worth **the same as a matched one and deliberately not the orphan's double**: the player aimed at these exactly as they aim at a group, so what a bomb pays is in the *count*, and the orphan rate exists to reward the one shot that has to be read for rather than aimed at — which a blast is the opposite of. ⚠ `ScoreKeeper.Landed`'s spent-shot door now reads **matched OR destroyed**; reading `matched` alone would have called every bomb-only shot a miss, broken the streak on it and awarded nothing for a third of the cluster. #173 is the standing warning that a scoring rate which reads reasonably can order the star rating backwards, so this rate is **reasoned and not yet measured** — `Tools/ScoreSim` can only answer once a level with bombs in it ships, and until then the shipped set is untouched because the third argument defaults to zero.
  - `Testbed\Maps\Bombs.json` is the test field — 197 balls, 5 bombs: one hanging in the open under the point (the aimable one), a pair one cell apart (the chain), one in the anchor plate, and one walled inside the body with no empty neighbour at all, which is the case the generator's gate refuses in an authored level and which is kept here on purpose the way `Glass.json` keeps an unreachable glass ball.

- **The campaign hangs the first two of them since #325**, and only in one place: **the Mirage**, the eleventh block, ten levels in the dream. Five hang the glass and five the rock — *one new kind a level, never both*, the owner's instruction, so a player meets one rule at a time — and the two halves are mirrors. The glass half's law is **glass goes on the skin**, never buried and never on the anchor course, because a transparent ball with no empty cell beside it can never be coloured and one on the field's top level is a ceiling anchor that dissolves without warning. The rock half's second law still stands — **what is left standing at the end is the stone**, which is why `CheckLevelCleared` cutting the rocks loose for no score is a *finish* and not a stall. Its first law was **stone reaches the ceiling on its own**, and **#343 retracted it on the owner's ruling**: the field's topmost level is the only one bonded to the ceiling plate, that socket is never cut by anything the player can do, and a rock there is therefore a ball that stays on the field however well the level is played. Stone now stops one course short of the glass — `Keystone`'s own rule, which its piers already followed — and it cost the three levels that broke it 26, 44 and 58 rocks (`Seam`, `Cairn`, `Obsidian`) and cost their shapes nothing: those cells still hang and still draw, carrying a colour instead of granite. Both laws are gated in `Tools/LevelGen` rather than merely written down; see "The two ball kinds in a design" in `docs/formats-and-tools.md` for what each gate refuses and for the two faults it caught while the block was being drawn.

**Neither may the barrel show more loaded rounds than there are shots left (#262).** `Magazine`'s own invariant is that the queue never empties — `Advance` always deals a real colour into the tail, so `Peek(i)` answers something for every one of its five slots regardless of the budget. That is correct for the *simulation*, and wrong to draw literally: at a two-shot budget the bore still held five physical balls, which is a lie the player is staring straight down. `CollectMagazineBalls` (`GameplayScreen.Draw.cs`) clamps how many of those five slots it actually draws to `ScoreKeeper.ShotsRemaining`, the same `Math.Min` the HUD's magazine strip already applied (#236, "The magazine strip, bottom right" in `docs/game-feedback.md`) — so the two readouts agree, and the barrel visibly empties over its last few shots down to a bare tube at zero. The clamp is draw-only: `Magazine.Advance` still deals into every slot exactly as before, `_magazineTransmute`/`_magazineFrom` still ride the full five along, and a slot that stops being drawn simply stops being drawn — nothing about the queue's own state changes, so a level that hands out extra shots (there is none, but nothing would need to change here if one did) picks the count back up for free. The Testbed carries none of this: it has no shot budget, `ScoreKeeper.ShotsRemaining` is a Game-only question, and its own magazine loop is untouched.

**A ball already in the barrel whose colour has just died is re-coloured where it sits** (`Transmute`, over the shared `Magazine.Recolour`), not left to be fired at nothing. Letting a stale queue play out costs the player up to `Magazine.SIZE` shots on colours that cannot match, through no fault of their own; this is a game and not a simulation, so re-colouring a loaded ball is allowed. The colour changes **immediately** and the animation is cosmetic — firing mid-transition gives the new colour, never the dead one. The replacement is drawn at random from what survives; picking whichever colour would help most would quietly make the game easier, which is a difficulty decision and not a fix. **It stops once the level is decided, and — the case that actually bites — once nothing is left to transmute *to*** (#176). The census exists to answer what may be loaded next and nothing loads any more, so all that would still run is a deliberately visible dissolve re-colouring the queue in front of a player who has already won. The two conditions are not one: the *winning* landing runs this before `CheckLevelCleared` arms the countdown, so the level is not decided yet while the map is already empty — measured on a one-colour test level, every clear re-coloured three to five of the five loaded slots to a default the instant the field went, which is #176 on **every** clear rather than only on the late landing the report describes. A landing can also still reach the path with the gun shut, which is why the gate is here and not left to `Shoot`'s refusal: a shot fired before the field emptied may be in the air when it does, and an empty field's own ceiling will take it.

- **The transition is a dither cross-fade, and that is a technical choice before it is a stylistic one.** A ball's colour is a **per-draw uniform** (`GetDiffuseTintByType` selects the bucket), so there is nothing per-instance to lerp: blending two colours means drawing the ball **twice**, once in each bucket. Two coincident *translucent* spheres would need depth sorting and would come out muddy — a dither cut needs neither, and both draws stay in the opaque path.
- `ModelInstance` therefore grew a **`Dissolve`** float (TEXCOORD6, a `Single` rather than another `Vector4` — one scalar is all it is, and this rides on every instance in the scene, the city alone being over a thousand). **Zero draws the whole thing**, so nothing has to know it exists to opt out. Positive *goes* (keeps pixels whose noise is above it), negative *arrives* (keeps those below its magnitude); the two are exact complements, so `+t` and `-t` together cover every pixel exactly once. Only the ball pattern technique reads it; an unconsumed element in the vertex layout costs the other techniques nothing.
- **The noise is screen-space, and the trap worth remembering is which screen.** This paragraph used to say object-space, and that was right about the trap and wrong about the fix: a dither laid down in *target* pixels is averaged straight back into a smooth cross-fade by the box filter that resolves a supersampled frame, so the pixelation the effect exists for vanishes at exactly the settings that make everything else look better. The answer is not the ball's own space — cells there are cubes in the world, so they turn with the ball and take its perspective, and read as lumpy three-dimensional mottling rather than as pixelation of the picture. It is a cell sized in **display** pixels (`DISSOLVE_DISPLAY_PIXELS`, multiplied by the supersample factor on the way to the shader), which survives the resolve because every target sample inside one display pixel decides alike. See "The ball dissolve dithers in display pixels" in `docs/rendering.md`, which is the authority. The hash has no `sin` in it, for the same reason the cloud field's has none.
- The cut is branchless (a select, not an `if`: `Dissolve` varies per instance and a branch would diverge inside one draw call) and sits at the top of `PatternPS`. At the settled zero it reduces to `clip(noise)`, and the hash is never negative.
- `_magazineTransmute` counts **down** from 1 to 0, so the dissolve's progress is its *complement*. Feeding the countdown straight in runs the effect backwards — the new colour arrives complete on the frame of the swap and the old one is never seen. That is exactly what the first build did, and only a zoomed screenshot showed it.
- **The shift is the shared `Magazine.Advance`'s, and the cross-fade state rides along on a hook.** `_magazineFrom` and `_magazineTransmute` are drawn in step with the colours, and shifting only the colours would leave a slot dissolving out of the ball behind it — so `Advance` owns the queue's shift and reports every `(destination, source)` pair to the `slotCarried` delegate wired once in the constructor, and this file carries its two arrays along the same path in the same order. The colour state stays here because *which* colours have died is a question about this cluster; only the knowledge that a queue moved is shared, and there is now exactly one place holding it. `Refill` fires the companion `slotLoaded` hook for every slot, which is what stops a level loaded over a mid-transmute session inheriting its half-finished dissolves; `Recolour` deliberately fires neither, because the old colour is the one thing this cross-fade must keep.

## Physics in the game (Game)

The Testbed's simulation, ported into `Game` (its own decision, taken after the founding slice shipped without it). What made the port cheap is that `Prazsky.BS3D.Physics` was already complete: `BallsConstraintsBuilder` builds the constrained structure, attaches a newly landed ball, and — in `ReleaseSameTypeCluster` — **already implements the match rule** (`MINIMUM_CLUSTER_SIZE` 3) including the follow-up release of everything left unanchored, via `BallsMap.GetCellsDisconnectedFromCeiling`. Wiring Bepu therefore delivered "balls fall on a match" for free; it was never a separate job.

- **Three files moved out of the Testbed into `Prazsky.BS3D.Physics` rather than being copied**: `Callbacks.cs` (the narrow-phase and pose-integrator callback structs, in class `Simu`), `ContactEvents.cs` (adapted from Bepu's demo) and `KinematicBody.cs`, plus `IContactEventHandler`, which was a loose interface at the bottom of `Testbed.cs`. All four were pure Bepu infrastructure with **zero** coupling to Testbed types — they only happened to live in its namespace. Two divergent copies of the contact material (`FrictionCoefficient` 1, `MaximumRecoveryVelocity` 2, contact `SpringSettings(30, 1)`) would have been exactly the kind of silent drift this repository keeps one copy of shaders and scenes to avoid. `Prazsky.BS3D.Physics` needed `AllowUnsafeBlocks` for them.
- **The one place the game deliberately does not do what the Testbed does is the timestep.** The Testbed takes one `Timestep(min(frameTime, 1/60))` per rendered frame, which runs the simulation in **slow motion below 60 FPS** (at 30 FPS everything moves at half speed) and with a dt that varies with the display and the load above it — and Bepu's own guidance is to keep the step constant. The game accumulates the frame time and spends it in whole steps of `PHYSICS_TIMESTEP` (1/120 s), capped at `PHYSICS_MAX_STEPS_PER_FRAME` so a frame that hitched drops the remainder instead of spiralling. It runs `IsFixedTimeStep = false` and offers `nocap`, i.e. precisely the configuration the Testbed's approach breaks under — and since #124 the cap is also a Settings row ("FPS limit"), switchable at runtime rather than only at launch. **The cap is no longer vsync**: since #270 the game presents immediately in every mode and holds the rate in `Platform/FrameLimiter.cs`, vsync having been measured presenting a level at exactly half refresh while the frame cost under 5 ms (see `docs/scenes.md`). The fixed timestep is what makes that switch safe to make — the simulation is spent in whole 1/120 s steps whatever the frame rate turns out to be, so changing how frames are paced cannot reach the physics. **And since #293 the fixed step's other half is in place: the drawn frame interpolates every ball between the pose before the last step and the live one** by the accumulator's fraction (`_renderAlpha`, snapshots per step in `StepPhysics`), so a frame that falls between steps — routine under the drop cinematic's slow motion, where whole rendered frames pass with no step at all — still moves everything smoothly. The drawn world runs up to one step (8.3 ms) behind the simulation; nothing reads drawn positions for decisions — rules, aim and the landing preview all ask the simulation — and a placed body (the landing's #265 placement) resets its history so no blend straddles a teleport. See "The drop cinematic" in `docs/game-feedback.md` for the measured staircase this removed.
- **The simulation does not stop when the level does (#241).** The owner played a level to its end and watched the arena stop dead behind the numbers: the cluster hanging perfectly still, the last of a collapse halted half way down the drain, shots frozen in mid-air — while the fireworks climbed and the result page's own camera swung out around all of it. The cause was the stack doing exactly what it was built to do: every page over a session sets `UpdatesUnderlying` false, and the result page was one of them. `ResultPage` now answers **true** and is the only page over a session that does, because a pause is a game *put down mid-move* and an ending is the arena *carrying on without a player* — a distinction one flag can express and "is a page over it" cannot.
    - **The session decides what that permission buys, and the split is the world against the game.** `GameplayScreen.UpdateUnderResult` runs the simulation and the gun's own answer to the last shot — the recoil stroke, the magazine's glide, the transmute fade — and nothing else. No input or aim (the page owns both), no landing preview, no ceiling descent, no ending, no quality verdict, and **no camera**: the page is easing the lens off the gun and two writers of one pose is one of them losing. The HUD is not stepped either, for the plainest reason available — it is not drawn once the level is over — and the crosshair goes with it (#259): its opacity is `PreciseAim.Blend`, which freezes at whatever the loss found it at for exactly the reason above (nothing under the page steps the lean), so without the gate a loss taken mid-hold left the reticle parked over the result screen for as long as the page stood.
    - **Leaving the rules out of that method is not what holds them.** Contacts are processed from *inside* the step, so a shot still in the air lands straight back into `OnBallLanded` whatever the covered frame chose to call. `LevelOver` is what holds them at each door: the landing returns at once (the ball still sticks, silently — the handler attached it before saying so), `OnShotSpent` scores nothing, and `RemoveFallenBalls` is handed `scoreMisses: !LevelOver`, so a shot culled under the page does not move a keeper that `LevelResult` was already taken off.
    - **Measured on a real ending** (level One played to `OutOfBalls`, twice): under the page `shots=2 → 1 → 0` as the two balls still in the air fell and were culled — on the old build they hung there for as long as the page was up — while the score stood at 4,320 across all thirty probe samples and the 33-ball cluster's summed speed stayed between 0.66 and 2.16, never zero. A hanging cluster never quite settles, which is the whole point of leaving it running.
- **The frame question is the one that would have silently wrecked the port.** `Game` has a lattice frame and a world frame, offset in Y by the cluster offset `FitFieldToMap` solves per level (−4.9497 for the fallback field — the offset hangs the field's top at the frame's height, raised for a deep field so its bottom clears the death line); the Testbed has no such offset. The simulation lives in **world**, because everything else it touches does — the funnel floor, the ceiling, the muzzle a shot leaves from, the kill plane — and only *one* thing is lattice-framed: the positions `BuildBallsStructure` reads out of the `BallsMap`. So `BuildBallsStructure` took an optional `worldOffset` (the Testbed passes nothing and is unchanged), and it is applied to the body positions **and to nothing else**: `ConnectBalls`'s anchors are *differences* of two grid positions, so a uniform translation cancels out of them, and `ConnectBallToCeiling` reads only X and Z from the grid (its Y is the ceiling body's own). Applying it twice tears the structure apart on the first step; forgetting it on the ceiling body yanks all 285 balls five units up into the glass. `MapToWorld`/`WorldToMap` were **deleted** rather than kept: the crossings are now countable — the builder's offset, and the offset the contact handler carries to take a world contact down into the grid frame.
- **The cluster does not settle on its lattice, and the gap is not even a constant.** The ceiling `BallSocket` anchors a ball's top (local +0.5) to the plate's bottom face (local −0.5), so the top level comes to rest one unit below the plate's *centre* — a unit above its own lattice row — and the structure hangs stretched under it, the offset decaying to nothing further down. That much is the Testbed's own behaviour and is kept for parity; `CeilingPlate.CLEARANCE` is the figure to change if the cluster should ever hang exactly on its grid, and `BallsConstraintsBuilder.CeilingRestY` is where the resting height itself is derived, next to the anchor pair that decides it. **What is this game's own is that the plate then walks down over a level**, dragging the whole cluster with it while the lattice stays exactly where `FitFieldToMap` hung it — so the lattice-to-cluster gap starts at about +1 near the glass and passes through zero and out the other side as the descents accumulate. Two consequences, and neither is optional. Nothing may derive a world position from `cell + _clusterWorldOffset`: everything that has to goes through `ShotPlacement.CellWorldPosition` with the drift the solve measured (see "The landing preview" above for the three things that were getting it wrong and by how much). And anything asking *where the balls are* has to read the bodies — which `ClusterCollector`, `CheckLevelLost`'s death-line walk and the HUD's cluster profile all already do.
- **`BallContactEventHandler` is `Prazsky.BS3D.Physics`' since #68**, one copy for both executables, and the split inside it is the whole point: Bepu runs contact callbacks on worker threads from inside `Timestep`, so `OnTouching` only enqueues, and everything that touches the simulation, the map or the listener set happens in `ProcessQueuedContacts` on the main thread. The order `Timestep` → `Flush()` → `ProcessQueuedContacts()` is mandatory — unregistering a listener is only safe once the per-worker adds collected during the step have been applied — and it runs **per step**, not per frame; since #76 it is `PhysicsWorld.Step`'s own order, with the per-step work handed in as a parameter, so a caller cannot take a step without it.
    - **The copy that survived is this one, so it is the Testbed's contact path that changed.** The two were never the same rule, which is why #68 was a gameplay change rather than a file move and why it waited for #73 to put the Testbed's copy in a file of its own first. Three differences, each of which the game had right: it listens on `OnTouching` rather than `OnContactAdded`, so it never attaches off a *speculative* contact (the measurement is in that method); `ShotPlacement` decides the cell and the handler places it, in two steps, which is what gains the Testbed the second-ring fallback and the drift-aware rest position; and a shot resolves exactly once, through `BallLanded`/`ShotSpent`. The Testbed subscribes to neither event — nothing there keeps score — so it takes them as two null checks per landing. What it also gains is the `[shot] bounced…` lines, which are unguarded: they are rare in play, but the Testbed's `autoshoot` sprays balls at a full field from the free camera and can produce them in bursts.
    - **Both callers build one per field and let the previous one go** — the game per level, the Testbed per map load — so every field on it is `readonly`. That is not tidiness: #73 had to give the Testbed's copy a settable ceiling, because `FitCeilingToMap` replaces the whole `KinematicBody` wrapper on every load and the handler went on holding the old one (harmless only because the body and handle it reads outlive the wrapper). Rebuilding removes the class of staleness instead of pushing each field as it changes, and it is safe because a map load retires every ball in flight first, so no listener outlives the handler it was registered with.
    - **What #68 deliberately did *not* do** is give the `BallsMap`-plus-`PhysicsBall[,,]` pairing an owner, which its own text proposed. That would mean reshaping `BallsConstraintsBuilder`'s whole static API and every walk over the structure in both programs — the ripple, the loss check, the cluster profile, the aim preview, the teardown — and none of that is where the duplication was. The defect #68 names ("a fix in one silently misses the other") is answered by there being one contact rule; the threading of two parameters is a wider change with nothing broken under it.
- **A manifold offset is relative to the pair's *first* collidable**, not to either body's own, so the world contact is `pair.A`'s position plus the offset whichever of the two the shot ball happens to be. Both velocities are zeroed on attach, angular included: residual spin would drag the constraint anchors that are about to be created around with it.
- **A landed ball's body is PUT in its cell, and letting the constraints drag it there instead was #265.** The handler used to leave the body where the shot touched and rely on the new sockets to pull it across "up to several diameters within a frame or two". That looks equivalent and is not, because of what the ceiling socket anchors: **the ball's own north pole** (local `+BALL_RADIUS`) against a fixed point under the plate. That pair has **two solutions** — the ball hanging under the anchor, and the ball sitting inverted on top of it — and a ball dragged in from above and to one side settles into the wrong one whenever it has too few neighbours to break the tie. It is a valid solution, so it is stable and the body **goes to sleep there**, which is why the symptom persists rather than passing: the ball comes to rest a measured **1.16 units above its cell**, putting a top-level ball's centre above the plate's own centre. That is the report's "attaches visually outside the ceiling plate", and it never settles back.
    - **The same drag-in produced the other half of the report.** A socket anchored on the ball's *surface* turns the correcting impulse into a **torque**, and nothing in this simulation damps angular velocity — `PoseIntegratorCallbacks.IntegrateVelocity` adds gravity and nothing else — so a ball spun up on the way in keeps spinning. Measured at **1–3 rad/s still, fifteen seconds after landing, the body never sleeping**, for every snap of a diameter or more onto a cell with one neighbour or none.
    - **Measured on a headless rig** that drives the real `PhysicsWorld`, `BallsConstraintsBuilder` and `BallsMap` through the handler's own sequence (the physics library is plain `net10.0` and opens no window, so a landing can be played without a game). Twenty-seven cases — three neighbourhoods (a solid top level, one lateral neighbour, nothing at all) × three snap directions × 1/2/3 units — and the control the rig had to pass first: a ball placed *at* its cell, which is what the build pass does, settles to 0.002 u and sleeps. **Before:** the sparse neighbourhoods spin for ever, and the from-above snaps end 1.13–1.16 u up, asleep and inverted. **After:** all twenty-seven at 0.000 u of rise, ≤0.002 u off the cell, asleep. Eight repeats of one failing case were **bit-identical**, so "occasional" in play is the geometry of a particular shot and not a threading race.
    - **Placing the body first makes the constraint error zero at the moment it is created**, so there is no impulse, no torque and no second solution to fall into. The orientation is deliberately left alone: a shot carries no torque in flight, so it arrives near identity and the measured fix needs no reset — while resetting it would snap the drawn ball's procedural pattern in place. And it **deleted a workaround rather than adding one**: `PhysicsBall.RenderOffsetArmed` existed to skip the arrival glide for exactly one frame, because the body was still at the impact point then and `body + offset` drew the ball as far *past* the impact as the cell was short of it. With the body in its cell that same expression is the impact point itself, which is where the glide is meant to start, so the flag, its branch and its doc are gone.
- **The shot's collidable is swept**, and it took a level nobody could play to establish that it has to be. It used to be built from the bare `TypedIndex`, with a comment calling that load-bearing — but a bare index is `ContinuousDetection.Passive`, an *unbounded speculative margin* with the bounding box expanded by velocity, which generates contacts at any distance rather than at a solved time of impact. That stops a shot passing clean through the cluster, and it was doing so. It does **not** make the shot land: the early contact carries a normal taken where the ball has not arrived, and against a cluster face the shot meets at a glance the solver turns the ball away with the manifold never reaching `depth >= 0` — the gate `ContactEvents` raises `OnTouching` on. The attach is never attempted and the ball flies on to stick alone at the edge of the glass, which is how it was reported from play. **Measured on Static** (370 balls, 1773 constraints, 20 shots a run along a fixed aim into the standing cluster, four runs each): with the bare index, **0 contacts of any kind and 0 attaches in all four runs** — the level is unplayable, not merely hard; with the swept collidable (a speculative margin bounded to `BallsConstraintsBuilder.SPECULATIVE_MARGIN` plus `ContinuousDetection.Continuous`), **13–14 attaches per run and no shot reaching the glass**. The failure is geometry-specific — the same measurement on One attaches under both — which is why it showed up against one level and why a rig that only plays the opening level sees nothing. The distinction still matters for the two rules it always did: a shot must be attached on `OnTouching` and never on `OnContactAdded`, and the contact has to be anchored at the ball it was made against. Both are in `BallContactEventHandler`, with the measurements that forced them.
- **What is drawn is the simulation.** `ClusterCollector.Collect` — the shared walk since #76, one copy for both playing executables — goes over the `PhysicsBall[,,]` plus the shot and falling lists and takes each ball's world matrix from its body pose, **orientation included**, which is what makes the beach-ball pattern read as rotation. Two smoothings ride along: the occlusion is *eased* towards its target (a ball joins or leaves the lattice in one step while it has not moved, so taking it straight pops the shading of everything around a hole a released group left), and a landed ball is drawn *gliding* in from where it actually hit, since it is snapped to the nearest free cell and its body crosses several diameters to get there. Each ball must be visited exactly once a frame — both advance state on the ball itself, as does the ripple — and that is now enforced rather than remembered: `BallRenderSet.BeginFrame` is the only way to open a collection and refuses to open a second one before the first has been drawn.
- **Falling below `KILL_PLANE_Y` is the only thing that removes a ball, and that is a deliberate parting from the Testbed.** The Testbed also culls any ball whose body has gone to sleep — cheap, and it keeps the scene tidy, but it means a ball that settles on the island's stone winks out in front of the player, which reads as a bug whatever it saves. Here a ball that does come to rest stays there and stays visible — a rare case now rather than the routine one, since the stone ring is a dish (`ArenaIsland.DISH_DEPTH`) that rolls a landed ball into the drain and out through the hole; what can still rest is a pile propping itself up near the mouth, and those fall asleep, leave Bepu's active set and are drawn but barely simulated. `RemoveFallenBalls` is only ever handed the shot and falling lists; handing it the structure array would delete the cluster. **The consequence is a trap, and it cost a level that could not be lost (#66): `_shotBalls` holds shot *bodies*, not shots in flight.** A ball leaves that list only by attaching or by being culled, so one that settles on the stone stays in it for the rest of the session — and the out-of-balls loss, gated on `_shotBalls.Count == 0`, could then never fire again: 0 balls left on the HUD and no result screen, ever. Anything asking "has every shot resolved?" must ask the resolution state and not the list's length — `AnyShotUndecided` reads the **contact-listener flag** (a ball stops listening at exactly the moment its shot resolves, and `Shoot` is the only place anything is registered, so every listener is a shot in the air) plus `Awake`, which covers the one ball the flag alone misses: one resting on another *loose* shot ball has touched nothing static, so nothing ever unregistered it. It bit on the authored 30-shot level and not on a 3-shot test set, which is the shape of every bug of this kind — the longer the budget, the likelier a shot has come to rest by the time it runs out.
- Measured: 285 balls and 1377 constraints at startup, and the frame rate is unchanged from the pre-physics build (vsync-capped) — this cluster is two orders of magnitude smaller than the Testbed's 3000-ball stress map.

# The game: shell

The shipping product's frame — its menus, the screen stack they live on, and the quality tier the frame is measured against.

Part of the BS3D documentation. CLAUDE.md holds the project overview, the build commands, the ball grid and the repo-wide conventions, and says which of these documents covers what.

## Adaptive quality (Game)

The default of 2× supersampling costs four times the shaded pixels, and on a weak GPU that is the single biggest thing in the frame. Measured on an integrated Vega 10, the **main menu** ran at 13 FPS — so the one setting that would nearly triple it sat behind a menu the player had to sit through at 13 FPS to reach. `TuneQualityToFrameRate` notices for them (#62).

- **It measures, it does not recognise the adapter.** A vendor/name list is wrong on the first machine nobody tested — plenty of AMD parts are discrete and plenty of Intel ones now are — and it cannot see the other reasons a frame is slow: a 4K display, a laptop on battery, something else on the GPU. This is the same "be universal, not hardcoded" rule the rest of the renderer follows.
- **The front end is its own probe, and since #121 so is the level.** The menu already draws the same city, clouds, glare and tonemap the game does, at the same factor — so no separate probe pass, no synthetic workload, and the player is sitting there anyway. The verdict lands once the frame rate has settled — a few seconds in, and later on a machine whose GPU is slow to reach its clocks (see the ramp-gate bullet below). What it could not see was a **cluster** — the assumption that it did not need to ("on this class of hardware it is the fixed scene cost rather than the ball count that dominates", #64) held only for the sizes that existed when it was written — and since #249 the front end shades one anyway, the preview map the backdrop hangs (see the front end's own bullet below), so its verdict now includes whatever the rolled map costs. Not the level about to be played, though, which is one reason the level's own probe stays. The shipped set runs from 225 balls to **959**, and a cluster that fills the frame is shaded pixels like any other: on a 75 Hz panel Onion cleared the menu at `High` and then played the entire level at **37.5 FPS — exactly half refresh** — with the probe already latched and nothing left watching. `GameplayScreen.Update` calls the probe too now, on real (unscaled) elapsed and never while a drop cinematic is engaged — the heaviest and least typical second and a half of a level, and a verdict taken on a collapse would spend image quality for the rest of the session on it.
- **A fullscreen switch re-opens it too, at every tier since #123.** It always re-opened, but under a guard of `_quality != QualityLevel.High` — which excluded the one tier that most needs re-measuring when the back buffer grows several-fold, since `High` is where the frame is dearest and where 2× supersampling sits on top of it. Nothing in the reasoning was ever specific to `Medium` or `Low`; a `High` verdict is just as much "this machine's answer for *this* back-buffer size". It routes through `ReopenQualityProbe` now, so the two re-open sites cannot disagree. **Demonstrated** on the desktop, 1600×900 → 3840×1600: the dream settles at `High` windowed and, on the switch, logs `[quality] 25 FPS in the menu at High (floor 68) — lowering to Medium` — a step the old guard made impossible, leaving that scene at 25 FPS for the rest of the session. The cavern shows the whole chain (`High` → `Medium` windowed, then → `Low` after the switch); the neon city needs no step at all here and takes none.
- **A level build re-opens the latch** (`BS3DGame.ReopenQualityProbe`), per level rather than per session, because the answer is only ever "this level, this machine, this back buffer" — the light levels may hold a tier the heavy ones cannot. It is unconditional on the current tier, unlike the fullscreen switch's own re-open, which takes it only when the tier is already below `High`; `High` is exactly the tier this case exists to catch. A tier the **player** pinned is never re-opened, which is what `_qualityPinnedByPlayer` is for. Measured after the change, same machine and panel: Onion steps to `Medium` once its frame rate settles (`[quality] 37 FPS in the level at High (floor 68) — lowering to Medium`) and holds **75**; One is never stepped and keeps `High`'s 2× at 75.
- **Warm-up is counted in seconds, not frames.** The opening frames are shader compiles, the first touch of each render target and the window settling. A fixed frame count is itself a function of the frame rate, so on the slow hardware this exists for it would wait the longest — exactly backwards.
- **A downward step waits for the frame rate to plateau, because a GPU still spinning its clocks up reads slow.** The warm-up is a fixed length and a card's clock ramp is not: measured on an average desktop with an older discrete card, the Forest front end climbs **46 → 50 → 57 → 60 FPS over the first four seconds** and then holds the 60 Hz vsync cap, yet the old probe — 1.5 s warm-up, one 1.5 s window — took its verdict at ~3 s on a 52 FPS ramp window and dropped a tier the machine did not need (`[quality] 52 FPS in the menu at High (floor 54) — lowering to Medium`). So the tier is now stepped down only once a verdict window is no more than `QUALITY_RAMP_FRACTION` (3 %) faster than the one before it — a window still climbing faster than that is the ramp, not the machine, and only rolls the measurement forward. A window that clears the floor still settles at once (a machine already above it is not made to wait for a plateau it is past), and the first window has no predecessor, so a drop takes **at least two windows and never one**. Re-measured after the change on that desktop, every scene warms up past the floor and is **left at `High` holding 60** — the Forest and the neon city windowed, and the neon city, the dream and a 364-ball level fullscreen at 1080p — where the Forest's own front end was knocked to Medium on its ramp before. The downward path itself is unchanged and still fires on the hardware it was built for (the integrated-Vega/75 Hz laptop below): once that machine's frame rate has plateaued below the floor the same step runs, only no longer on a ramp-up window. **The gate cannot keep a slow machine at a tier it cannot hold** — a plateau below the floor is exactly what trips it — it only stops a drop taken while the frame rate is still rising.
- **One step per verdict, never upwards, and it latches.** `_qualitySettled` is one-way: set when the player named `quality=` or `ssaa=` on the command line (`Program` passes nullables — null means "nobody said"), when they touch Quality in Settings, when the machine proves fast enough, or when there is nothing left to lower. A quality dial that keeps moving under the player is worse than one merely set too low once, and raising it again on a machine that recovered would put them back where they started.
- **It steps the tier, not supersampling alone** (#63, below). Stepping the factor by itself left the neon city at 30 FPS with 40 % of the frame still on the table. A tier step resizes the scene target (and would rebuild the city if the tiers still differed in radius — since the sort none does), so it **re-arms `_qualityWarmupLeft`** — left unarmed, the very next window measures that hitch and steps again on the strength of it.
- `QUALITY_MIN_FPS` (45) sits comfortably under any display's refresh, so a vsync-capped machine — the normal case — never trips it. A drop is explained on the main menu on its own dark plate, because that screen deliberately has no scrim and a line of small text over open water is otherwise unreadable.
- **Not persisted.** No *setting* in this game is — there is still no settings file, so every run re-measures. Cheap, and it means a machine that was busy once is not penalised for ever. (The one thing that does persist since #92 is the player's campaign progress, `Progress.json` beside the level set — that is data the player earned, not configuration; see "Scoring" in `docs/game-session.md`.)

## The quality tier, and what the frame actually costs (Game)

One bundled **`Quality: Low / Medium / High`** setting (#63) replaced the lone Antialiasing row, because supersampling was never a performance dial — it is tied to a look decision (it is what keeps the balls' procedural relief sharp) and it was the only setting that reached the rest of the frame at all. `QualityLevel`/`QualityPreset` (`Game/QualityLevel.cs`) hold the tiers; `ApplyQuality` is the **one** writer, on `SetScene`'s rule, so the adaptive probe, the settings row and the command line cannot disagree about what a tier means.

What each tier turns down was **measured, not guessed** (#64), because until it was, both issues were naming candidates by intuition — and intuition got the ranking wrong. `logfps` writes one frame-rate line a second to stdout with every setting that changes what it means, and `scene=`/`sky=` pin the backdrop, without which nothing is comparable: **the scene is picked at random per launch**. `.claude/skills/benchmark` drives the lot.

- **The order of magnitude is the scene, not the setting.** Front end, windowed 1600×900, vsync off, integrated Radeon (Ryzen 7 5700U), at the default settings **when the tiers were chosen**: sea 14.6 ms, meadow 15.5, savanna 16.6, desert 16.9, mountain 19.1 — and then **city 76 ms and neon city 103 ms**. A spread of 6.8× on identical settings. (The front-to-back sort has since collapsed the city figures to 23.0 and 27.5 ms — a spread of 1.9× — see "Drawing the city near to far" in `docs/rendering.md`; the terrain figures stand, there being no city in them. The dream and the cavern, added later and then rebuilt on the fractal noise library, measure 61.7/18.8 and 56.5/17.5 at `High`/`Medium` — their `High` is a desktop setting by design, and the probe steps this class of hardware to `Medium`, where both hold 53–57 FPS.) **The cavern's pair there is a flat-plane-era figure and was left behind twice**, once when the river was marched and again by #155: `docs/scenes.md` records 73.5/21.4 for the same thing and the two have contradicted each other since. #250 settled it by measuring rather than by choosing — the cavern on the same machine class now runs **26.5 ms at `High` and 24.3 at `Medium`** at a level (`level=Chest`, 1600×900, `nocap`), so both stored pairs are history and the live figures live in `docs/scenes.md`. The dream's 61.7/18.8 has **not** been re-measured and is under the same suspicion (#167 is open on exactly that). The five terrain scenes never needed a tier; the two city scenes were unplayable without one, and one launch in thirteen lands in each.
  - **Space measures 14.6 ms on the same machine and settings — the sea's own figure, i.e. the cheapest scene in the game** (68.5 FPS against the sea's 69.2; the same run put the city at 77.5 ms, matching its published 76). A full-screen procedural sky sounds expensive and is not, because of what it *replaces*: there is no terrain grid, no sky dome, no cloud deck and no per-pixel cloud shadow, and the cloud cost alone is 1.1–10.6 ms depending on overdraw. The heavy parts (each nebula's warped fBm, the Milky Way's structure, the planet's bands) sit behind `[branch]`es on screen-coherent masks, so most of the frame pays for the void, three hashes of stars and one galaxy cell. It needs nothing in a tier.
  - **`ssaa=` used not to override the tier's factor, and every figure above predates the fix (#122).** `LoadContent` calls `ApplyQuality(_quality)` unconditionally and its last line was `SetSupersampleFactor(preset.SupersampleFactor)`, which overwrote whatever the command line had asked for — so an `ssaa` 1-vs-2 sweep silently measured 2× twice (caught by the two columns coming out identical to a tenth of a frame for all three scenes, and again later when an `ssaa=1` control run appeared to rule supersampling out of #121 and had not). The factor is remembered as `_supersampleOverride` now and the tier writes it only when nobody pinned one. The `[fps]` line prints the factor actually in force; read it before believing an A/B that varies it — that line is what would have caught this both times.
- **Splitting the frame by `ssaa` 1 vs 2 separates per-pixel work from the rest** (the difference is 3× the 1× pixel cost). The terrain scenes carry only ~2 ms of pixel work at 1×; the city carries 17 and the neon city 22. So the city's window-grid shading *is* the fixed cost this hardware pays for, and everything else is inside a 15–19 ms frame.
- **That killed three of the four candidates outright.** Glare is ~1 ms at this resolution — the two passes are sized off the *back buffer*, so they are independent of `ssaa` and cannot move this frame. The per-pixel terrain relief cannot be more than the ~2 ms of pixel work the whole terrain scene has. And the particle counts are not measurable here: the mountain carries 2800 snowflakes and the sea 2000 spray billboards, and they are the 5th and 1st *cheapest* scenes. (The snowfall was 1400 when the tiers were chosen; #85's retune doubled it, and the A/B says the doubling is free — 23.9 ms against 24.2 on the same machine and pins, inside a run whose own spread is wider than that. The mountain reads ~24 ms there now rather than the 19.1 above: that figure is from when the tiers were chosen and the scene has been worked on since, so use it for the *ordering*, which holds, and not as today's absolute.) None of the three is in a tier, and saying so with a number is worth more than a settings row that buys nothing.
- **Measured, on the pre-sort neon city at 103 ms:** the facade grain (three noise octaves per city pixel) is 11.3 ms; the window-frame moulding is 9.1; the two together 20.8, i.e. they add. The clouds — the drawn deck *and* the per-pixel shadow every lit surface pays — are 10.6 ms here, 2.3 on the mountain and 1.1 on the sea: the cloud cost is **per shaded pixel**, so the city's overdraw multiplied it. Since the sort removed that overdraw, the grain and the moulding together measure **0.9 ms at 1×** (`Low` against `Medium` at the same skyline) — the entries stay in the tier for machines weaker than this one.
- **The surprise, and the reason to measure: the city's biggest cost was overdraw, not per-pixel complexity.** Cutting the block radius 14 → 12 saved almost nothing (1.7 ms — the towers still fill the frame), 12 → 10 saved 19.4 ms, and 10 → 8 another 16. The knee was at 10, which is where `Low` used to stop. **A depth prepass or a front-to-back sort would buy this for nothing visually** — the city was one instanced opaque draw in generator order, so it got no early-Z benefit. **That lead has since been taken** (see "Drawing the city near to far" in `docs/rendering.md`), and the re-measured sweep is flat — 14/12/10/8 blocks read 27.5/28.6/28.3/26.4 ms at `High` — so the radius dial is dead, `Low`'s skyline went back to the authored 14, and the knee paragraph above is the record of why it was ever reduced.
- **The tiers, and what they measure at.** `High` is the authored look untouched (ssaa 2, full city). `Medium` gives up supersampling only, which is the one change that reaches every scene. `Low` adds the city's two per-pixel luxuries (the skyline it once pulled in is back at the authored radius since the sort). Since the sort: neon city **36.4 → 95.6 → 103.7 FPS**, ordinary city 43.4 → 108.0 → 119.2 (they read 9.9 → 30.0 → 43.4 and 13.1 → 38.4 → 55.4 when the tiers were chosen); the sea 68.5 → 125 → 125 (identical, because the city dials are dead in five of the seven scenes — `Low` is there for the cities and for machines weaker than this one). `Low` clears #63's "30+ FPS on this class of hardware" on the *worst* scene many times over, and `High` now sits under the probe's floor only in the two cities.
- **`ssaa=` overrides the tier's factor** — the expert override the benchmark and the screenshot harness both use — and both it and `quality=` latch `_qualitySettled`. It is held as `_supersampleOverride` rather than merely applied once, because `ApplyQuality` runs again in `LoadContent` and on every adaptive step, and each of those wrote the tier's factor back over it until #122. The two are not overlapping writers in the UI, because there is only one row now.
- **A trap the startup order sets.** `ApplyQuality` runs from `LoadContent`, before the menu pages exist, so `SetSupersampleFactor`'s `_settingsPage.Refresh()` had to become null-conditional — a command-line `quality=` reaches it long before there is a row to write the value onto. It crashed on the first run for exactly this.
- **Ways to measure nothing at all** — all four have happened, and the skill lists them. MonoGame's `InactiveSleepTime` is left at its default, so an **unfocused** window is capped near 50 FPS and that cap silently becomes the measurement (the benchmark clicks the title bar for this reason alone). Every A/B must pass `quality=` or `ssaa=` explicitly, or the adaptive probe steps the tier down three seconds in and it reads as a win for whatever was being tested. **The front end's camera orbits**, and the cost swings with the view — one neon run read 43 FPS and climbed to 74 over fifteen seconds as the lens reached an angle with fewer towers across it, so a fixed window samples the *expensive arc* rather than an average, and runs of different lengths are not comparable. And a **stray process** from a harness that died between launch and cleanup halves everything measured afterwards with no sign of why — worse than halves, in fact: two instances competing for the GPU took the front end to **6 FPS**, and because Myra reads its input inside `Draw`, a scripted 100 ms press-and-release then falls entirely between two frames and is **never seen at all**. The symptom is not a slow run, it is a script whose clicks silently do nothing and a game still sitting on the main menu; two runs were lost to it before the frame counter in the corner of the capture explained why. Kill any existing instance before launching, and read the FPS off the screenshot before believing anything else in it.
- **Scripted input is not a repeatable input, so nothing counted downstream of it is a measurement.** The automation used to drive the game from outside sends real `mouse_event`/`keybd_event` into a 60 Hz poll, so whether two clicks land on one frame or two is up to the scheduler. Three runs of a single unchanged build, same script, gave **25, 28 and 26** balls left — the run-to-run spread is larger than most changes worth testing. So a before/after in balls, score, or anything else the shots decide reads as signal when it is noise (it was quoted once in #79 and withdrawn). What *does* survive is a **categorical** outcome — which page ends up on top, whether a body is asleep, whether a thing was drawn at all — because it does not care how many clicks got through. Prefer one of those, or read the code path; do not build a numeric A/B on this harness without first running the same build twice.

## The front end (Game)

The game's menus, built on **Myra** — the project's one GUI library, already carrying the map editor's scene-config panel, so the game's menus and the editor's tools are one stack. Each page is its own class under `Game/Screens/`; `BS3DGame` keeps only the frame's share of the menu (the one `Desktop`, the fonts, the layout scale, the palette).

**The seam the whole thing is built around is the screen stack** (#65), and it is what keeps the menu from having to fight the game for anything. It was a `GameState { Menu, Playing, Paused }` enum with a switch and it is now the stack itself: a menu page is *over* the gameplay screen, so no gameplay path runs — no aiming, no shooting, no physics, no game camera — because the manager stops updating what is covered, and the frozen game goes on being *drawn* underneath because the page draws what is under it. A pause therefore shows the game exactly as the player left it, camera shake and all, and the *setting* (sky, backdrop, island, drain) is on screen whether or not there is a session, because whichever screen is at the bottom draws it — and since #249 that bottom screen hangs a random level's map over the island too, so the front end is never a stage with nothing on it (its own bullet below). See "The screen stack" for the split and for how the frame is sequenced around the one HDR resolve.

- **The world is built on the first "Play", not at load** (`GameplayScreen.BuildLevel`/`TearDown`). The simulation, the ceiling body, the drain's collision mesh and the cluster are the expensive part of starting up and belong to a session; the menu comes up without paying for them — the preview map it hangs since #249 is none of that, being parsed data and one instanced draw (next bullet). Two consequences worth knowing: the ceiling's **mesh and renderer** are the host's rather than the cluster's (its kinematic *body* is still the simulation's), because `SkyLitRenderers` must be complete before the first `ApplySkyLighting`; and a rebuild disposes the whole `Simulation`/`ContactEvents`/`ThreadDispatcher`/`BufferPool` set rather than emptying the cluster ball by ball — same order as `UnloadContent`, for the same reason.
- **The front end hangs the promise over the island: a random level's map, no cannon and no physics (#249).** `BackdropScreen.RollPreviewMap` draws an entry of the level set at random — any entry, played or not, because the point is *what awaits*, not what is next — and hangs it through the very offset a session derives for the same map (`GameplayScreen.FitClusterWorldOffset`, extracted from `FitFieldToMap` for exactly this), so the preview sits exactly where the map will hang when it is played — with **one correction #254 had to add**, because that offset is the *lattice's* and a played cluster does not rest on its lattice: the ceiling `BallSocket` ties a top ball's crown to a point one radius under the plate's **centre**, so a real cluster settles a whole diameter under it and its top surface meets the glass exactly (`BallsConstraintsBuilder.CeilingRestY`; `CeilingPlate.CLEARANCE` says the same from the other end). A preview has no bodies and no solver, so it hung one whole unit of daylight short of the glass — a gap no level ever shows in play, on the screen whose job is to promise what play looks like. It is raised by that unit as it is hung, and the camera is framed off the raised figure. The cluster is data and one instanced draw (`BallDrawFrame.AddMap`'s new offset form — the map editor's own physics-free path, "a caller with no physics is a first-class caller"): no simulation, no bodies, no gun, breathing on the wall clock like any cluster. Over it hangs the menu's **own glass** — a second `CeilingPlate` on the host rather than a refit of the session's, because a kept session (Continue) never reinstalls its level and would otherwise draw glass cut to another field's footprint — at rest above the preview field's top level, from a plain translation rather than a body: nothing ever steps this ceiling down. It fits at a **stronger opacity** than the played field's (`MENU_CEILING_ALPHA` 0.7 against `GLASS_ALPHA`'s 0.48): the menu camera looks at the field almost level and from well outside the island, where the played whisper disappears against a bright sky, and the owner's ask was that this ceiling be *seen*. The map is **rolled again on every return to the front end** (`ReturnToMainMenu`), so the menu never hangs the map just played back over the player as if it were the next one; the scene and its dome are deliberately *not* the map's — the note the feature answers is explicit that they do not matter — so `SetScene` keeps its one-callers-only rule and the scene page keeps its meaning. An unreadable entry skips to the next, and a whole unreadable set falls back to the bare setting; one console line (`[menu] preview map …`, in the manner of `[field]`) names what was rolled. For the probe this means the front-end verdict now shades balls (see "Adaptive quality" above).
- **The game's name is a 3D object standing in the scene, not a label over it (#248).** `Effects/TitleWordmark` sets the title in round tube letters — one word to a line, stacked into the top-right corner with the last word blown up into a badge, each letter its own colour off a rainbow that flows through the word, each ringed by a dark keyline, the whole block drifting and breathing. The owner's complaint was that the Myra label it replaces *whispered*: "the title should be prominent, distinct, unmissable, the main thing". The issue offered two roads — an outlined/rainbow/pulsing **2D** treatment, or real 3D lettering in the scene — and the owner chose the second (*"it will render as colourful/rainbow 3D objects"*), so the 2D road is not built at all and the label is gone. The corner and its inset are unchanged, so `MainMenuPage`'s composition is still the one its class doc describes.
  - **The letters are a hand-authored mono-line alphabet swept as tubes, not the real Anton outlines extruded** (`Prazsky.Core/Render/LetterMesh`, with `LetterShapes` holding eleven capitals and a space — what the name needs). Two things decided it, and the second is the one that matters. Cost first: a closed glyph outline with counters needs a polygon triangulator with hole merging, which would have been the only geometry maths in this repository with no precedent, and a triangulator's failures are invisible in the code and only show as a wrong picture. Then the look: **an extruded letter's front face is one flat plane with one constant normal**, so it takes one flat shade and its three-dimensionality lives entirely on the bevel, where a round cross-section has every normal in the half-circle facing the lens and so carries a light-to-dark gradient, a specular streak down its length and a Fresnel rim on its silhouette — the same three things that make this game's balls read as spheres rather than as discs. A wordmark whose whole job is to say "this game is 3D" wants the shape that says it loudest. The tube sweep itself is `TrophyMesh.BuildHandle`'s, fixed frame and all, for the same reason: the paths are planar, so there is no twist to accumulate and no Frenet flip where the curvature reverses. Domes close every terminal and simply **overlap** where two strokes meet, which is what a letter drawn with a round nib looks like.
  - **S and 3 are constructed, not sketched, and a sweep written the wrong way round makes a spiral rather than a wrong-looking letter.** Both are two arcs meeting at the waist with a common *horizontal* tangent — each bowl's centre one bowl-radius above or below the waist, so the waist is the bottom of the upper circle and the top of the lower one and the two curves flow through it instead of meeting at a corner. What then separates an S from a 3 is only which way each bowl is travelled: the S opens its upper bowl left and its lower right, the 3 opens both left. Both first passes were spirals.
  - **It is drawn in the HDR scene pass from `BackdropScreen.Draw`, between the balls and the drain's glass** — not in the sharp foreground layer the cup and the confetti use. The front end never defocuses and never dims (`MainMenuPage.DimsFrame` is false and no front-end page overrides `FrameBlur`, so `Find<PausePage>()` comes back null and the whole defocus chain is skipped), so there is nothing here for a sharp layer to be sharp *against* — and that layer costs a permanently allocated supersampled target, a bright pass over it and a full-screen composite on every frame of the one screen the adaptive-quality probe is measuring. In the scene pass the letters get the frame's real depth buffer, the same exposure, ACES curve and film grain as everything else, and their bright pass feeds the bloom pyramid for free. States are stated and put back, the in-and-out contract `ArenaIsland`'s slices keep.
  - **The gate is one test against the active page, in the screen that owns the front-end frame, and that is correctness rather than taste.** `Screen.Enter`/`Leave` are raised on a **push** and a **pop** only, and Settings, Scene, About and the level picker are all pushed *over* the main menu without popping it — so a `Present`/`Hide` pair on `MainMenuPage` would have left the title standing behind all four of them. It is also in `BackdropScreen` rather than in the host's `BeginSceneDraw`, where the fireworks, the confetti and the cup are drawn, because **nothing the host draws is front-end-only** — which is exactly why `celebrate` and `confetti` work as front-end test levers at all; this screen, by contrast, is not reached once a session is on the stack, `GameplayScreen` not drawing what is under it. Verified both ways by capture: no wordmark on the settings page, none on a `play` boot, and it comes back on the way out.
  - **A rainbow here is the SECOND deliberate exception to the greyscale rule, and it has to argue for itself** — see that bullet's own warning about the next accent being waved through on the first one's example. Three things make it admissible where #180's flat rainbow badge failed (that one was photographed over the meadow and its cyan stop went "very nearly invisible against the sky", so the badge blinked out for a third of every sweep). One: it is **not chrome** — it is the game's own name, the one element on the screen whose job is to be looked at rather than read past, and the owner asked for it in as many words. Two: it is a **lit solid**, so it reads by shading and by silhouette even where its hue matches what is behind it, which is precisely what a flat sprite cannot do. Three: every letter carries a **dark keyline of its own**, so most of the contrast the word is read by comes from something other than its colour. That third leg is **weaker than it first was, and knowingly**: the keyline was a constant near-black, which was contrast no hue could take away, and the owner ruled it out on seeing it run — it read as ink from a different game and did not suit the rest of the game's style. It now plays the rainbow itself. What survives of the guarantee is that the rim is *dark* whatever it is playing, which holds against a bright scene; what is given up is the dark-scene case, where a dark rim has nothing to be dark against and the glow's floor carries the word instead. Photographed on the Moon, that is exactly what happens and the word is still perfectly readable — but it is the glow doing it, not the rim.
  - **The keyline is a second, fatter tube off the same skeleton, drawn with FRONT faces culled**, and the whole trick turns on that. Both tubes share an axis and the keyline's is the fatter, so its near surface is *nearer the lens than the letter's* and drawing it normally would hide the letter inside it; culled the other way round, what is drawn is its **far** surface, which lies behind the letter, so the letter paints over it and the ring survives only where the letter does not cover it. Its `SpecularAmbientStrength` must then be **zero**, because every pixel of a back face has a normal pointing away from the lens and the shader's Fresnel term reads that as a grazing angle and returns full reflectance — at any strength above zero the ring that is meant to be dark mirrors the whole sky and becomes the brightest thing in the frame.
  - **It is thinner than it was and it plays the rainbow itself**, both on the owner's ruling after seeing the first version run. Each rim takes its letter's hue advanced **a third of a turn** round the same wheel and held dark — a third rather than a half because the complementary of a colour at a low value is the muddiest thing on the wheel, and rather than a small shift because that reads as a shaded edge of the same letter instead of a line playing its own colours.
  - **The rim's colour arrives as EMISSIVE, not as diffuse, and that is the only way to get a stable one.** A back face's normal points away from the *lens*, which says nothing about where the three directional lights are — so a lit rim would brighten and darken as the menu's ninety-second orbit carried the lights round behind it, and a line meant to be one colour would breathe on its own. `EmissiveTint` is added flat, per pixel, ungoverned by any normal, so the rim is exactly the colour it was asked for from every bearing and under every one of the eighteen domes. Its diffuse is held at black so nothing else can reach it.
  - **It cost the batching, and that is the whole price.** Every keyline being one flat tone had meant all the letters sharing a mesh went through **one** instanced draw — eleven draws for fifteen letters. A colour is a per-draw uniform here, so the moment every rim differs there is nothing left to batch: fifteen draws, on a pass whose entire cost measured under this machine's noise.
  - **Two colour spaces, one object, and getting either backwards is silent.** The per-letter hue goes in through `Draw`'s `diffuseTint`, which the shader **decodes** (`SrgbToLinear(DiffuseColor.rgb)`), so it is handed over in sRGB; the glow goes in through `EmissiveTint`, which is the one term on this material the shader neither decodes nor premultiplies, so that one is handed over in **linear radiance**. The glow is then scaled so its Rec. 709 *luminance* is the same for every letter rather than its radiance being the same — without that, Rec. 709's weighting (green 0.72 against blue 0.07) would carry the word over the glare threshold in six places at six different times, and it would read as flickering rather than as breathing.
  - **The pulse's trough is a floor, and the figure was found over the Moon.** It swings the glow's luminance between 0.22 and 0.60, across the glare pass's 0.55, so the word breathes in and out of its own halo. The trough was **0.08** for one pass and that failed outright: a black sky gives the light rig almost nothing, so over the Moon and in space the glow is very nearly the only thing lighting the letters, and at 0.08 the word went dark and muddy for half of every 2.2-second beat — #180's blink arriving through brightness instead of through hue. The crest is deliberately no higher than it needs to be, for the opposite reason: the emissive term is **flat**, added per pixel without regard to the normal, so it is brightness the shading gradient does not get to vary, and a glow big enough to swamp that gradient turns the letters back into the flat stickers this whole approach exists to avoid. The beat has a second limb as well — a 3 % size breath — because motion reads over a bright backdrop where a glow barely does, and a glow reads over a dark one where 3 % of a size does not.
  - **The coloured haloes are a shell of their own, not a brighter letter.** The owner saw the soft coloured ghosts the letters wear in the verification captures and asked for more of them. They are the **bloom pyramid** reading the letters' glow, and a halo cannot be turned up on its own — the bright pass thresholds on luminance at 0.55, so the only lever is how far over it something goes. Pushing the **letter** over it was tried first and came back wrong: at a glow of 0.34/1.15 the word went chalky at every crest, because an emissive bright enough to swell a halo dominates the diffuse and the tonemap's shoulder desaturates what it compresses. So there is now a **third tube** per letter, fatter again than the keyline, drawn **additively** behind it — a band of the letter's own colour just outside its rim, which the glare pass blows into the halo. The letters keep the light they were tuned to, and the ghosts get as much as they want.
    - **Its crest is bounded by what it does to the letters, not by itself**, and that is the third figure this lesson moved. The bloom is a full-frame pass, so a halo that swells lays its own blurred light back over the letter it came from, and that climbs the same ACES shoulder. At 1.75 the letters were flooded and their counters filled in; 1.05 kept the counters and still went chalky; 0.85 reads as the word breathing between *solid and vivid* at the trough and *soft and glowing* at the crest, which is a better pulse than brightness alone. It is a ceiling found by photograph, and anything above it is paid for in the letters' colour. The letters' own swing was cut right back with it (0.20 to 0.38, from 0.22 to 0.60) — the aura carries the beat now, and two things breathing at once went pale for a second time.
    - **⚠ Its WIDTH has a hard ceiling that is geometry, not taste, and going over it is what the owner reported next: "visible coloured patches in the bends that look like an error, or a hole, or a gap".** A tube swept along a path curves as the path does, so a tube whose radius reaches the path's radius of **curvature folds through itself** on the inside of that bend — the surface turns inside out over a patch, and what shows there is a flat wrong-facing sheet. The shell was swept at a radius of 0.215 against this alphabet's tightest bend of **0.2016** (`LetterShapes.MIN_BEND_RADIUS`, set by U's bowl), so it folded, and the folds filled the counters of B, O, D and 3 with flat coloured patches. `LetterMesh` now **refuses** a tube that fat rather than building one that folds, in the manner of `MeshBuilder` refusing to wrap its 16-bit indices — a fold does not announce itself in the code, only in a picture. The width is now 85 % of that bound less the letter's own radius, which leaves B's counters a clear 0.16 of a cap height.
    - **The width and the two levels are tied by their PRODUCT.** What the bloom integrates is the light in the band — its width times its radiance — and the halo's *size* was never the band's anyway, it is the pyramid's. So halving the width to clear the fold and doubling the radiance keeps the same halo, and the photographs either side of the fix match.
  - **⚠⚠ THE ORDER OF THE THREE SHELLS IS LOAD-BEARING, AND IT IS: LETTER, GLOW, KEYLINE.** Two separate faults settled it, one visual and one measured.
    - **The letter goes first because it is the only one that writes depth**, so early-Z rejects both unlit shells wherever the letter already covers, and they are paid for only where they show. With the glow drawn *before* the letters instead there is nothing in the depth buffer yet, every pixel of every shell is shaded and then painted over — **measured at 2.6 ms of a 26 ms frame**, the only version of this feature whose cost came out above the machine's noise. Drawn after them it is **1.16 ms**, and the picture is identical: additive blending is order-independent, and the only pixels that differ are the ones the depth test removes in both orders.
    - **The keyline goes LAST, over the glow, and that removed a one-pixel seam.** While the keyline was drawn first and wrote depth, the glow's inner edge was defined *by* the keyline's silhouette — two shells having to meet exactly along a curve, which two rasterisations of two different radii cannot do. Magnifying the badge's edge to single pixels showed a thread of **backdrop** between them: grass at (120, 225, 100) sitting between the keyline's purple and the glow's cyan, belonging to neither, which along the length of a letter reads as the outline being **dashed**. Drawn this way round the glow runs continuously from the letter's own edge outwards and the keyline is painted on top of it, so there is no boundary for anything to fall through. Neither unlit shell writes depth — their bands overlap wherever the word is tight, and one of them writing depth would punch a hole in the other.
    - What the band is paying for is the frame's **full lit material** — cloud shadow, hemisphere ambient, Fresnel, sky radiance — every term of which is multiplied by a black diffuse and thrown away. So the lever if it ever has to come down again is a flat-colour technique or a narrower band, **not** fewer facets, which is not what it is spending.
  - **⚠ ALL THREE SHELLS SHARE ONE FACET COUNT, because their silhouettes have to NEST.** Each is a polygon of *n* sides and not a circle, so its silhouette lies somewhere between `r·cos(pi/n)` and `r`. Give two neighbouring shells different counts and those bands **interleave**: their boundaries cross back and forth, and every crossing leaves a sliver belonging to neither — which shows whatever is behind, and reads along a letter as a **dashed** outline. It bit twice, both times plainest on the badge, the largest thing on screen and so where a tessellation fault shows first: the keyline against the body at ten sides against sixteen, and then the keyline against the glow once the glow had to be narrowed to clear the fold (at the old width their radii were 0.063 apart and no facet error could bring them together; at the new one they are 0.020 apart and a 12-gon inside a 16-gon crossed it easily). At one count the three radii keep their bands clear of one another — 0.1275–0.130, 0.1491–0.152, 0.1687–0.172, which do not touch. Sixteen is what the letter itself wants; the other two would have been happy coarser and cannot be.
  - **The ramp is whitened only as much as its dark side needs.** A fully saturated blue is a *dark* colour, and a near-black letter in a rainbow word reads as a hole in the word rather than as a letter; every hue is therefore lerped a sixth of the way to white. It was a fifth for one pass and photographed over the **sea**, the brightest backdrop in the game, where the whole word came out chalky pastel — so the whitening answers the blue stop and nothing else, and the contrast comes from the keyline, which no bright scene can wash out.
  - **The letters take the dome's light like everything else in the frame**, which is the opposite of `TrophyPodium`'s choice and is why they are enrolled in `SkyLitRenderers`. A cup is presented for a few seconds and wants one controlled finish; a wordmark stands over all thirteen backdrops under all eighteen domes for as long as the game is not being played, and enrolment is what makes it come out right at both ends of that range — over the sea at noon the rig is bright and so are the letters, over space and the Moon the background is black and dim letters read perfectly against it. The glow and the keyline are the two things that do *not* follow the dome, and between them they are what stops a dark dome taking the word away. It is therefore built in `LoadContent` **immediately before the startup `SetScene`**, because that call ends in the first `ApplySkyLighting` and a renderer made after it stands under the library's default rig until the next scene or dome change — the fault the ceiling glass once had.
  - **There are TWO compositions and every frame is somewhere between them (#248's second pass).** The title opens as a **card**: the whole name on one line, centred in the frame at 0.88 of its width, facing straight out. When `SplashPage` hands over, it **moves** — over `MORPH_SECONDS`, smoothstepped so it leaves and arrives at rest — into the menu's composition: one word to a line, right-aligned into the corner, the last word blown up into the badge, turned inwards. So "3D" genuinely travels onto its own line as the block re-flows. Both are solved once at construction; the frame lerps the block's size, the share of the frame it asks for, its anchor, its turn and every letter's place in it.
  - **The anchor is two numbers rather than a mode**, which is what makes the move a plain interpolation: at 0 the block is centred in the frame on that axis, at 1 it is pinned to the far edge with the inset. The rest of the anchor arithmetic is shared, so lerping that pair lerps the whole anchor and there is no second code path for "on its way".
  - **The letters' hue and wave ride on reading order alone**, which is the same in both compositions — so neither jumps while the title is moving, and a letter that changes line does not change colour doing it.
  - **The caller states the target, never the progress.** `BackdropScreen` passes `settled: active is MainMenuPage`, and since #261 one more figure: the title's `presence`, 0–1, which is the flight's closeness eased down to a floor of a third (`WORDMARK_ASIDE_SCALE` — the owner's ruling was that the title stays, small, in its corner, not that it goes) — the caller states *where the flight is*, and the word decides what that means (the block scales about its anchored corner, the reveal's idiom, opaque tubes having no alpha; at a true zero the draw would be skipped, though nothing on the front end asks for one any more). The move belongs to the wordmark, so no page can leave the title stranded half way across the frame. The step comes off the **wall clock** rather than an elapsed value handed in, because this class is only reached from a draw: a frame that was not drawn is a frame in which nothing here should have moved. It is deliberately not clamped — a gap in the drawing (a level played, then Main Menu) arrives as one huge step, which saturates the morph, and that is right, the title belonging settled in its corner by then.
  - **The splash's own label is gone and its tree is empty**, which is the point of the whole second pass: a flat centred Myra label two and a half seconds before a 3D wordmark read as a different game's title card. What is left on that page is the piece of time it owns — how long the card holds, and what skips it — which was always the part that belonged to a screen rather than to a widget. Its fade-up went with the widget; the wordmark's own arrival answers that instead, a swell from `REVEAL_FROM` to full over `REVEAL_SECONDS`. **The arrival's overshoot is in the GLOW and not in the scale**, and that is forced: the fit below solves the block to the frame, so a block that overshot its own size would cross the inset it was just fitted inside. Light has no such budget.
- **The size and the anchor are solved from the camera's own projection, at the sway's EXTREME rather than flat.** The frame's half-extents come out as `d / M11` and `d / M22` (`TrophyPodium`'s trick, and the front end needs it more than the cup does — this lens is 60° where gameplay's is about 43°, and they are the same camera object), and the block is then sized to its share of them. Solving that flat was a visible fault: the sway and the depth bow carry a corner letter over a cap height nearer the lens, a nearer letter projects *bigger*, and a block that fitted at rest put its last letter on the frame's edge a few seconds later (photographed over the meadow — "SHOOTER"'s R and the badge's D both). A perspective divide is linear in the near distance, so both the fit and the anchor have a closed form rather than needing to be iterated. The **bow is deliberately not** in that reach: what the fit has to survive is the letter nearest the frame's edge, and the bow is zero there by construction, being a parabola across the block.
  - **The yaw is biased towards the frame's centre and never crosses back**, which is the owner's ruling on the first version's plain symmetric sway. A symmetric sway spends half its time turned the *other* way, and the wordmark hangs in a **corner**, so half the time it was angled away from everything — "as if it were looking out of the window, and since it is at the edge that does not look good". Turned inwards it reads as a sign angled to face the room rather than the wall behind it, and the sway is smaller than the bias so their sum never reaches zero. The sign is easy to get backwards and is stated in the code: block space has +x to screen right and +z towards the lens, and `CreateRotationY` carries +z to `(sin, 0, cos)`, so a **positive** angle faces the frame's edge and a **negative** one faces its centre. The visible consequence is that the block's outer edge is now permanently the nearer one — "SHOOTER"'s R photographs plainly larger than its S — so the margin the perspective fit reserves is spent all the time rather than only at the extreme, which is why the fit takes the *bias plus the sway* as its reach. Photographed at 16:9 and at both ends of the aspect range a window can be dragged to — 1104×861 and 1744×721, where the height limit and the width limit swap over.
  - **Cost: 1.16 ms of a 24 ms frame, and only on the front end.** Measured on a pinned rig — the orbit frozen and the preview map pinned, since both move and the map is rolled at random — over four paired runs whose groups do not overlap. (Pinning the map is `preview=` since #254; freezing the camera is still a scratch edit.) The letters and the keyline alone came out **under a millisecond and inside this machine's noise** (a difference of means of 0.7 ms with the groups overlapping, the fastest run *with* them faster than the slowest without); the aura is what made the cost measurable, and the shell order is what brought it from 2.6 ms down to this. The first measurement of it was wrong in the instructive way — 37 FPS against 28 FPS, compared across two runs that had rolled **different preview maps** at different orbit bearings, which is the same "both halves have to be the same place in the same state" trap this repository has now walked into four times.
  - **The 240-unit title font went with the label.** `MENU_FONT_GAME_TITLE`, the `SpriteFontBase` it built and the two accessors that handed it out are gone, nothing having read them once the widget was removed — and a size is its own glyph **atlas**, rasterized out of the display face on every menu rebuild, which runs on every window resize past `MENU_REBUILD_QUANTUM`. The **splash's** 2D label went the same way in the second pass — see the two compositions above — so `GAME_TITLE` is not set as a *heading* anywhere in the game any more. It survives as prose in About's paragraph and as the text of the window's own title bar, and `MENU_FONT_TITLE` still sets the result screen's headings.
- **Returning to the main menu keeps the session.** The gameplay screen leaves the stack but *stands*, so the menu offers **Continue** (shown only when `GameplayScreen.IsBuilt`) as well as **New Game**, which is the difference between a mis-click costing a click and costing a game. "Is there a game to go back to" is deliberately `IsBuilt` and **not** whether that screen happens to be on the stack — the two are different questions and only the first one is the player's.
- **`GameplayScreen.CoveredChanged` is the one door into play, and it exists for the input state.** The cursor is captured and recentred every frame while playing and left alone in the menu, so the first frame back would read the distance from wherever the player clicked to the viewport centre as an aim delta and yank the barrel across the field — and the very click that pressed the button would arrive against a stale "released" and fire a shot nobody asked for. `MouseAim.Invalidate()` skips that first frame's aim, and `MouseAim.Initialized` gates its shot test — **explicitly** since #76, where before the test sat inside the same block by nesting rather than by intent; the gamepad is re-polled there so a held trigger or Back button has to be released first. Since #154 the arrival also **captures the cursor at once when the pointer is already in the picture** — the menu press that put the screen on top is the opt-in, and both hazards above are answered by the same invalidate-and-snapshot — where #99 had left every arrival free and asked for one more click in the picture; a pad or keyboard arrival with the mouse parked elsewhere stays free (see "The cursor" in `docs/game-session.md`). It is the manager that raises it, on whichever screen ends up on top, so every way of arriving — a first push, a retry, the pop of a pause or a result screen — comes through the one path rather than each remembering to do this for itself.
- **Escape hands over through the same `_previousKeyboard` the play loop uses.** `UpdateMenuChrome` stores its snapshot into that very field — which stayed on the host precisely so the two sides share one, rather than the pages and the session each keeping their own — and that is what makes them hand over cleanly: the Escape that paused the game is still down on the first menu frame and is correctly not seen as a second press, and the Escape that resumed it is not seen again by the play loop. `UpdateInput` **returns immediately** after pausing — firing or traversing on the way out would be an action asked of a game that has stopped. That alone was **not enough**, and #79 is what it cost: `ScreenManager` applies stack mutations at the top of the *following* frame, so returning out of `UpdateInput` left the rest of `Update` running on a game that was not paused yet — `UpdateAim` on the very next line, then the ceiling slide, `StepPhysics` and `CheckLevelLost`. The pausing frame still aimed. So `UpdateInput` **reports** whether it paused (`private bool`) and `Update` stops on it. Only the pause path returns `true`; the other early exits return `false` because they write `PreviousKeyboard` alone and rely on `UpdateAim` to write `PreviousPad`, while the pause path writes both itself — which is why it was already safe to leave. Note what the evidence for this is: the code path, read. The A/B ball counts first quoted for #79 were withdrawn — the input automation drives real `mouse_event`/`keybd_event` against a 60 Hz poll, and three runs of one unchanged build gave 25, 28 and 26 balls left, so a one-ball difference is inside its noise.
- **`Desktop.Render()` also reads the input, so it only runs where the game's own input has stood down**, and **only while the window is focused** — **and only once the left button has been seen released since the top screen last changed**. `Mouse.GetState()` reports the button and a window-relative position whether the window has focus or not, so a click meant for another application that happened to land over a menu entry would press it — and "Quit" would close a game nobody was looking at. The second gate answers the same hazard from the *inside*: Myra keeps its own previous-mouse-state and is only fed input while a menu is on top, so on the first frame a page is drawn it compares a still-held button against whatever the state was the last time a menu had input — frames or minutes earlier — and reads a press that never happened there. Pausing with the fire button held therefore opened whichever pause entry the cursor sat over (Settings, in both runs that found it). `_menuClickArmed` clears whenever `_screens.Active` changes and re-arms on a released button; it is polled **only while a `MenuPage` is on top**, which is exactly when nothing else polls the mouse — the play loop takes its own snapshot in `UpdateAim`, and two reads of one device in a frame is what `BestPractices.md` #5 forbids. Keyboard and pad navigation are deliberately *not* gated, so a page under a held button is still operable, just not clickable. Both gates share one path: the desktop is laid out and drawn (`UpdateLayout` + `RenderVisual`) but not fed input — a just-arrived page and an unfocused window want the identical treatment, visible and not yet clickable. It is the same rule `MouseAim.Invalidate()` applies on the way *into* play and `_padTriggerReleased` applies to a held trigger, and the evidence for it is categorical rather than measured: which page ends up on top, Settings before the fix and the pause page after.
- **The look is deliberately greyscale, and that is a constraint rather than a taste.** The menu sits over thirteen backdrops whose palettes are nothing alike — a magenta/cyan neon city, an ochre desert, a blue sea, white peaks, green meadow — so any accent colour that reads as the game's own over one of them fights the next. Emphasis is carried by **brightness and opacity**: mid-grey slabs for entries (bar the front end's, below), a step up the grey ramp for hover, white for the active item and grey for the inactive. There are **no drawn frames** either: a border is one more shape competing with every backdrop, and the tone step at a slab's edge already reads as a control. Myra's stylesheet supplies a framed grey tool button by default, so `Border` has to be explicitly cleared. Note each state brush **replaces** the one before it rather than compositing over it, so a state cannot be a wash laid over the one before it.
  - **Every brush here is PREMULTIPLIED, and none of the figures mean what they look like until that is said.** Myra paints through `SpriteBatch`'s default `AlphaBlend`, so a brush composites as `rgb + dst * (1 - a/255)`: the RGB is the light the slab **adds** and the alpha is only how much of the scene it **blocks**. `(55, 55, 55, 191)` is therefore not "grey 55 at 75 %" but grey **73** at 75 %, and a lighter tone typed in unmultiplied comes out milky over a bright sky instead of lighter. #179 measured this from the other side without naming it: alpha 190 let "a quarter" of a blurred sky through a plate, which is exactly `1 - 190/255`.
  - **The controls were near-black and are mid-grey since #216, and the reason it is a step rather than a dial is the SETTINGS page.** A control has to hold near-white type over a *bright* sky (so it must block light), read as a surface over a *dark* one, **and** read as a control on a **plate** — and a plate is itself a mid-dark panel, so a slab anywhere near its tone is invisible on it. That was tried at grey 51 with 41 % of the scene through it: the front end looked right and every settings value vanished into its own card. So a control is either well below `MENU_PLATE` (near-black, which is what #216 was filed about) or well above it, with no modest version in between. It is `grey 73 at 75 %` now, hover `116 at 90 %`, pressed `140 at 94 %` — about 20 of tone clear of the plate and of each other, looked at on the card rather than reasoned about. A quarter of the backdrop coming through is as far as the transparency can go: the label is near-white, and at a quarter the worst case in the game — white type over a blown sky — still measures about 4:1.
  - **The front end rests its entries almost to nothing — the one place the "mid-grey slabs for entries" rule above does not hold.** The tones above are a *control's*, and they earn their keep on a plate and under small print — but the front end has neither. Its entries are the game's name's own Anton at 108, which holds itself over any backdrop exactly as the unplated title two corners away does, so the mid-grey slab was blocking a quarter of the turning scene to back type that did not need it (#217's "six black bars" complaint, taken the rest of the way). `MainMenuPage.EntryRestBrush` rests them at `MENU_FRONT_BUTTON` — black at **alpha 18, ~7 %** since #247, pure transparency so it only darkens and never milks a bright sky — a whisper kept only so the hover has something to lift from. It was alpha 40 (~16 %) for one revision and the owner played it: still a plate. **Note what #247's own body cites and do not follow it** — it names grey 73 at 75 % and `Menu.cs:185`, which is `MENU_BUTTON`, the brush every *other* page uses; editing that would have changed settings, both pickers and the pause, which #216 rules out. The front end got its own rest brush the day before that issue was filed. **At 7 % the thing that reads is the EDGE rather than the tone** — over the moon's bright regolith and over the island's stone the rectangle is a faint step, over grass it is all but gone; that is the ask ("just a hint that a control is there") and it is also where the next complaint would come from, and Myra 1.6.3 has no gradient brush, so a genuinely soft-edged slab would need a generated texture. Hover and pressed stay the shared tones, so the pointer still lifts the same slab every other page shows and the resting entry is all but the label alone. It is a **front-end** rule, not a palette change: every other page's entries keep `MENU_BUTTON`, where the slab sits on a plate or in a picker and is what reads as a control — which is why the rest tone is asked of the **page** (`MenuPage.EntryRestBrush`), because `ApplyNavHighlight` repaints every entry each pass and would otherwise paint the shared grey straight back over the front end's own rest. Verified over the sea, the brightest backdrop: white Anton on the whisper reads at rest, and the hovered entry stands clear.
  - **The star rating is one of the two deliberate exceptions (#139), and what makes it one is worth stating, or the next accent gets waved through on its example.** (The other is the 3D wordmark since #248, which argues its own case in its own bullet above; both had to.) It is a *readout of what the player earned* rather than chrome: the hue **is** the information, and brightness cannot carry it here, because all four tiers have to read as "good" and any ramp that dims towards the best one says the opposite. It is also the one place hue is not competing with a backdrop — the result screen softens the whole frame (#178, the scrim having gone) and the picker's stars sit on a tile's own slab over the page's plate, so bronze, silver, gold and the cold tier above it are read against a controlled grey in both places rather than against a neon city or a desert. That grey is no longer near-black since #216, which costs the tiers some contrast but not their hue; the plate bullet below records what was checked. And it is a vocabulary the player already has, which four neutral glyphs cannot speak at all. `BS3DGame.StarTierColor` is the one writer; `STAR_EMPTY` is the grey the unearned remainder recedes to.
- **Sizes are authored for 2160p and resolved at build time** (`MENU_DESIGN_HEIGHT`, `Scaled()`), exactly as `InfoRenderer`'s overlay is — Myra measures in pixels, so without this the menu keeps its pixel size and shrinks to a postage stamp at 4K. It is done by re-resolving the sizes and rebuilding the tree, **not** by `Desktop.Scale`: scaling the desktop also scales the full-screen scrim, which then stops covering the frame (tried, and it left the scrim as a rectangle in one corner). `EnsureMenuLayout` runs from `Draw` rather than off the resize event, so the one place it can be stale is the frame about to draw it, and the rebuild is quantized (`MENU_REBUILD_QUANTUM`) so a live window drag asks the font system for a couple of dozen sizes rather than hundreds.
- **Two typefaces split by ROLE, not by weight: Anton for everything loud, Inter for the small print** (both SIL OFL 1.1, each shipping with its licence beside it, both `EmbeddedResource`s rather than content — no path to get wrong, nothing to install, nothing through the MGCB pipeline). Myra's own stylesheet carries a small bitmap font, fine for a tool panel and far too coarse for a title. **Anton sets the title, the menu entries, a result's headings and the whole in-play HUD**; **Inter stays for `MENU_FONT_SMALL`** — About's paragraphs, a level's rules on the picker, the adaptive-quality note — because a condensed display face is drawn for headlines and closes up at the size those are read at. The menu was set in Inter throughout and read as a document rather than as a game.
  - **Anton is the shippable form of the Impact look, and that is a licence decision before it is a taste one.** Impact is bundled with Windows and **not redistributable**, which is the same rule that keeps Segoe UI (the `InfoRenderer` spritefont's source) out of this assembly — `Game.csproj` states it where the fonts are declared. Anton is the OFL face closest to it and is the better-drawn of the two anyway: looser sidebearings and open apertures where Impact closes up. Loading Impact off `C:\Windows\Fonts` at runtime would dodge redistribution but trades the "nothing to install" guarantee for a system dependency and a fallback path.
  - **Three `FontSystem`s, not three fonts in one** — FontStashSharp falls back through a system's fonts glyph by glyph, so a second face added beside the first would never be reached, since the first has every glyph. Picking a face means picking a system.
  - **A single-weight display face costs you weight as a signal, and something has to replace it.** Anton has one weight, so a "bold" slot resolves to the very same glyphs — see the HUD's low-balls alarm, which was a weight step and is a size step now (`HUD_LOW_EMPHASIS`). Anywhere else that reaches for emphasis has to reach for size, colour or motion instead. `_menuFontSystemBold` (Inter Bold) is still loaded and is what remains for the small print if it ever needs a heavier cut.
- **The pages** each own their tree and are swapped into the one `_desktop.Root`; everything that must agree with the game's state (the resume entry, each setting's value, the marked scene, the figures on the result screen) is refreshed in `ShowPage`, because a page can only change while nobody is looking at it. **Splash** · **Main menu** (the game's name top-right, Continue/Play, Scene, Settings, About, Quit bottom-left — see the composition bullet below) · **Level select** · **Pause** (Resume, Settings, Scene, Main Menu, Quit) · **Settings** · **Scene** · **About** · **Result**. Settings, Scene and About are reached from both the front end and a pause, and neither has to remember which: they are pushed over whatever was up, so "Back" is a `Pop` and the scrim is a question about the stack — which is where the one asymmetry lives: **only a pause dims the frame.** The front end draws no scrim at all, because the rotating scene is the whole point of that screen and a full-screen wash over it throws away the only thing it exists to show; a pause dims hard, because what is behind it is a stopped game. Legibility on the front end therefore comes from the widgets: the entries carry their own slab, and the text-carrying screens sit on a dark **plate** (a slab alone cannot hold small text steady when every line lands on a different piece of moving scene). `DimsFrame` is `Manager.Contains<PausePage>()`, and since #114 the scrim it asks for is drawn by the **host's own batch** right before the desktop renders, not set onto the Myra root as a background: Myra's background paint stops a couple of rows short of the viewport's bottom edge (measured — the root lays out at the full 1600×900 and an identical plain `SpriteBatch` quad reaches row 899 where the Myra-drawn background stops at 897; two rows at a 900 client, one at 700), which left a thin strip of undimmed arena across the bottom of every dimmed page. The stack still decides, the page's widgets still draw over the scrim, and the root panel still stretches because it is what places the page's children and what Myra hit-tests.
  - **The front end is laid out as a COMPOSITION and every other page as a stack, and the reason is the bullet above (#217).** A centred column is right for a panel that has the frame's attention, and wrong for the one screen whose subject is *behind* it: the middle of that frame is the island, the drain and the turning scene, and a centred stack of entries under a centred title covered exactly what the page exists to show. So the game's name is pinned **top-right** and the entries **bottom-left**, off one shared inset (`MainMenuPage.FRONT_INSET`), leaving the middle to the scene and balancing the two blocks of type across the diagonal. Mechanically it is one Myra `Panel` with two children — `ScreenRoot` takes `params` for it — because a `Panel` arranges every child against the full client rectangle by that child's own alignment, which is the same machinery that centres a column on every other page. **The pause and both pickers deliberately stay centred**: what is behind a pause is a stopped game rather than a view worth keeping clear, and a picker is a grid that wants the whole frame. Two things about the order are load-bearing: children are added in the order **the pad should step through them**, since `CollectNavEntries` follows the order widgets were added and not where they landed; and the adaptive-quality notice moved to the **top** of the column, because a bottom-anchored stack pushes everything above the point where height is added — as the last child it shifted all six entries the moment the probe spoke.
  - **The front end has its own two type sizes, and that is a constraint rather than tidiness.** `MENU_FONT_BODY` is every button in the game and the **settings page bounds it** — thirteen rows once ran off the bottom of the window and took Back with them (#138), and that column already stands close to the design height at 80. `MENU_FONT_TITLE` is shared as well, by the splash's card and the result screen's headings, one of which is the whole of "CAMPAIGN COMPLETE". So the page with no height budget to keep is loud by itself: `MENU_FONT_GAME_TITLE` (240) and `MENU_FONT_FRONT_ENTRY` (108), through `FrontEndEntry` — `MenuButton`'s own `MenuClickable` core in the bigger type with its label against the left edge of the slab, so the shared brushes, the click sound and the pad's `Tag` cannot drift away from every other page's entries. The slab keeps `MENU_BUTTON_WIDTH`, so the front end and a pause read as the same control differently placed — and because that figure is also what the result screen's score plate is pinned to (#179) and must not quietly become "the front end's width". Verified over the two hardest backdrops as well as the easy ones: the moon (bright regolith under the entries, black sky behind the name) and the mountain (snowfield under the entries, lit sky behind it).
- **A plate must never carry a control's weight, and that is a rule rather than a taste (#179) — but the tone order it was bought with is now the other way round (#216).** `MENU_PLATE` was near-black at alpha 190 and `MENU_BUTTON` was `(11, 11, 11, 212)` — two constants a step apart, so a panel carried a control's weight. #179 fixed it by making the **plate** the lighter of the two, `(42, 42, 42, 214)`, sitting between a button's rest and its hover (11 < 42 < 72 < 120 pressed). #216 then moved the controls past it instead of towards it, so the plate is the **recessed** surface now and the controls are raised on it (50 < 73 < 116 < 140). **The plate's own figure did not move for that**, and that is the thing to know before touching it: the tone is still #179's, and its opacity is load-bearing for a second reason — the result screen carries no scrim (#178) and the small print on a plate is the one type here that is not near-white Anton, so at 190 a quarter of a bright blurred sky came through the numbers.
  - **The inversion serves #179's own goal better than #179's arrangement did**, which is why it was taken rather than worked around: "the card is darker than the things on it" is the reading every player already has, where "the card is lighter" only ever worked as long as a control was near-black. Looked at on all four pages that wrap themselves in a plate (About, Settings, both pickers) plus the result screen: the values, tiles and entries read as raised controls and the card recedes behind them. What the ramp must still guarantee is a clear step in *some* direction — the sign is free, the step is not.
  - **The level picker's tiles are the case to check when this ramp moves**, because a tile is a *readout* as much as a control: `STAR_EMPTY` (92) and the bronze tier (205, 116, 58) are read against the tile's own face, and both lose contrast as that face lightens. At grey 73 the earned run and the hollow remainder still separate (looked at, with a two-star save on every entry), and the hollow pair is fainter than it was — correct, since it is the half that recedes — while a *hovered* tile at grey 116 loses it almost entirely. That is transient, under the pointer, and the detail line below the grid restates the level anyway; a lighter face than this one would need the star greys re-derived with it.
  - **The result screen's score panel is the one plate that stands BESIDE buttons rather than under them**, and that is what #179 was filed about: left to auto-size around a few short numbers it came out visibly narrower than every control beneath it in the same centred stack, which read as a fourth button that could not be pressed. It is pinned to `BS3DGame.MenuColumnWidth` — the same `Scaled(MENU_BUTTON_WIDTH)` every entry is cut to, exposed so there is one copy of the figure — as a `MinWidth` rather than a `Width`, so the unlock note under the total may be longer than the column instead of being clipped to it. Myra applies both to the measured size with the padding already in, so at the minimum the plate's edges land exactly on the buttons' (verified in a capture).
- **Every setting is a button that cycles its value** rather than a slider or a drop-down — one widget kind, one click, nothing that can be left half-dragged, and each change takes effect where it is made, so the scene behind the panel *is* the preview. Fullscreen, **quality** (the bundled tier — see "The quality tier"; it owns `_supersampleFactor`, which is what the shared pipeline's `EnsureTarget` sizes the target from, so changing it is what recreates the target), exposure, sky dome (all eighteen), the FPS counter, **FPS limit** (#124 — the presentation cap: "Monitor" is vsync, "Unlimited" lifts it; the `nocap` launch argument seeds the same flag and stays as the benchmark's startup override; both writers go through `SetGraphics`, whose `ApplyChanges` re-fires `PreparingDeviceSettings` and with it the `PresentationInterval`; and the toggle deliberately does **not** re-open the quality probe — the cap changes what the frame rate may *read*, not what a frame costs, and the probe's floor sits under any refresh by construction), **Aberration** (the lens's corner fringing) and **Film grain** (the print's animated mid-tone texture) — two taste toggles whose Off writes a zero that skips the respective shader branch entirely; see "Color management" in `docs/rendering.md` — and the four volume rows — Volume, Effects, Music, Ambience, each a percentage of the authored mix, cycling downwards because the reason to click one is almost always "quieter" (see "The sound" in `docs/game-feedback.md` for the split). `_supersampleFactor` and `_exposure` stopped being `readonly` for this; the command line still seeds them. The one row that is not a cycler is **Reset progress** (#92): its resting value shows the star total the click would erase ("N stars", in words — the value column is set in Anton, which has no ★ glyph), the first click arms it to "Sure?", the second wipes the save, and arriving on the page (`Enter`) stands it down again — one click on a row that erases every star would be an accident waiting beside ten rows that are safe to click freely.
- **The rows sit in two columns, because thirteen in one did not fit (#138).** Eight display rows on the left under a "DISPLAY" heading, the four audio rows on the right under "AUDIO" and the campaign row under a second heading below them — the headings stay quiet by **brightness** (`MENU_TEXT_DIM`), since they label rows rather than being clickable, and a campaign wipe left unlabelled under "Ambience" would read as part of the mix. They were quiet by *size and face* as well until #243, and that was the bug the owner reported: the **small** face at 58 over rows in the **display** face at 80 made each heading smaller than the rows it headed, and set in the small-print family while they were not — a heading upside down. `MENU_FONT_SECTION` (96, the display face) sits above the rows and well under the page heading's 124, which shares the screen with it. `MENU_FONT_SMALL` could not simply be raised: eight other sites share it and every one of them really is small print. This was never a small-window bug: every size on the page is a 2160p design figure scaled by the viewport's **height**, so a column that overruns the design height overruns it at *every* resolution and aspect alike, and the rows that ran off the bottom took Back — the page's only way out — with them. Two columns roughly halve the stack's height and buy the width back from the height-derived scale: the plate comes out about 1930 design units across (measured — 805 px at a 1600×900 client), so the page asks for a viewport only wider than about 0.9:1, which every display is. **`MenuScroll` was the other candidate and was not taken.** The reason recorded at the time was that the pad and the arrow keys reached entries inside a scroller but could not scroll a focused one into view — **that hole is closed since #245**, so it no longer carries the decision. What does, and always was the better half of it: two columns need no scrolling *at all*, so every row is on screen at once and the page can be read rather than walked. **The row order is unchanged**, read down one column and then the other, and that is also the order the pad steps through — `CollectNavEntries` follows the order widgets were *added*, not where they landed, so the split moved where a row sits and not the sequence: the eight display rows, the four audio rows, the campaign row, Back.
- **The windowed size is the player's, and `SetGraphics` restores it rather than stating the default (#137).** `_fullscreen` is F11's own switch and nothing else; the OS-level maximize the title bar offers (and any drag of the window's edge — `AllowUserResizing` is on) leaves it `false`. So a `SetGraphics` that set `PreferredBackBuffer` to `WINDOW_WIDTH`×`WINDOW_HEIGHT` whenever `!_fullscreen` shrank the swap chain under a window the OS does *not* shrink with it: **measured on the FPS-limit row, maximized — 3840×1529 before the toggle, 1600×900 after**, the window unchanged and the picture upscaled into it. Every caller had it, the FPS limit merely being the one row that is otherwise free of side effects. The fix is `_windowedSize`, recorded in `OnClientSizeChanged` **only while windowed** — the pass that goes fullscreen raises that event at the display's size, and recording it would bring the game back out of fullscreen into a window the size of the screen — with a zero guard for the minimize. Verified both round trips with the game's own `[fps]` back-buffer line: 1600×900 → 3840×1600 → 1600×900, and maximized 3840×1529 → 3840×1600 → 3840×1529. It fixes the plain resize with it, which had the same hole and no issue of its own.
- **Fullscreen is borderless since #157, not a hardware mode switch.** `SetGraphics` sets `HardwareModeSwitch = false`: minimizing exclusive fullscreen tears down the swap chain's DXGI fullscreen state, and with no `Activated`/`Deactivated` handling anywhere in the game the window never restored at all. The back buffer is sized to the display on the fullscreen pass either way, so the picture is identical — there is just no display mode to lose or re-acquire.
- **The menus are drivable from a gamepad (#61), and that decides who owns the highlight.** The D-pad, the left stick and the arrow keys move a focus cursor; **A** or Enter presses the focused entry; **B** is Escape, both going through the one `MenuBack` that owns which screens have a back at all. It is not a nicety — Xbox is a target, the game is otherwise fully playable on a pad, and `Buttons.Back` already opens the pause menu, so without it the pad could open a menu it could not then use.
  - **The entries are found by walking the shown widget tree** (`CollectNavEntries`, on a screen change and on a layout rebuild, never per frame), not by a list registered per button — a hand-kept list is one an entry added later silently falls out of, and the entries that come and go (Continue, Next Level) would need the registration repeating anyway. Skipping `Visible = false` is what keeps the cursor off those for free. Myra's `Container.ChildrenCopy` is internal; `Container.Widgets` is the public one. The click action rides on the button's own `Tag`, because the walk has no way back to the delegate `MenuButton` was handed and reaching into Myra's click plumbing would tie this to a version of it.
  - **Exactly one device drives at a time, and this is the part that is easy to get wrong.** The focus cursor reuses the pointer's own hover brush, so a pad-driven menu reads like a moused one — but the pointer does not have to *move* to sit over an entry: a pause recentres the cursor and it lands wherever the column happens to be, lighting a second entry beside the focused one. So while the cursor is up the hover is switched **off** (`ApplyNavHighlight` sets each entry's `OverBackground` to its own resting brush), and moving the pointer puts the cursor down, which restores it in the same pass. Both halves were seen failing in screenshots before they were fixed.
  - **Which entry is focused does not survive a screen change; *whether the cursor is up* does.** Entry 3 of Settings is not entry 3 of the screen it returns to, so the index resets — but a pad player who backs out of a sub-screen would otherwise land on a screen with no cursor and have to press a direction to get one back, every time. `CollectNavEntries` also re-baselines the pointer, or the frame after a pause reads the game's *own* recentring as the player moving the mouse and takes the cursor straight back down.
  - **A held direction steps once, waits `NAV_REPEAT_DELAY`, then walks at `NAV_REPEAT_INTERVAL`** — both wall-clock seconds and not frame counts, or the list would run faster on a faster machine. Accept pressed with the cursor down only *raises* it rather than pressing anything: firing the top entry would make the pad's first press mean whatever happened to be first — "New Game" over a session in progress, on the very screen that exists to offer Continue.
  - The whole thing sits inside `UpdateMenuChrome`'s `IsActive` block, since a pad reports through XInput whether the window is focused or not — the same gate the precise-aim trigger has. Verified end to end on the keyboard path (wake, step, wrap, activate, back, repeat, pause, and the pointer taking over); the pad shares that code but has not been run against real hardware.
- **The menu camera** is `BackdropScreen`'s, and since #254 it is a **flight** rather than a fixed orbit. It is still the game's one `RecoilCamera` (`Prazsky.Core.Camera`) with the shake at rest, the very one the gameplay screen poses while playing — which is why it has to stop when a session is on the stack, and does (`MenuPage.UpdatesUnderlying`) — and every part of it is advanced by elapsed seconds, so it takes the same time on any machine.
  - **The wide leg is the establishing shot**, about one turn every 90 s: slow enough to read as ambience rather than as a turntable, and roughly level with its target so the frame is a look *across* the scene rather than down onto it, which is what shows the most of a city, a sea or a mountain range at once. Its stand-off is **solved and no longer stated**: the island (radius 26) has to read whole across the frame, and a disc of radius R sits inside a frustum of half-angle t at any distance past `R / sin(t)`, so the figure comes off the **horizontal** half-angle — 38 units at 16:9, 40 at 16:10, 45 at 4:3, 34 at 21:9, where `CAM_RADIUS` was 44 for every window shape at once. A second bound stops a map deeper or broader than anything shipped being cropped; no level in the set reaches it.
  - **Once a cycle it leaves that turn and flies at the map** — and since the owner's follow-up on #261 no two arrivals are shaped alike: each cycle **rolls its legs** within stated ranges (12–20 s wide, 3½–5 s in, 16–24 s close, 7–10 s back out; the wide roll's floor is the probe's ten-second settle below, and its jitter only ever lengthens the hold), rolls whether the crane sets off **from under the cluster or over it**, and jitters this pass's stand-off by a few per cent. The stand-off itself now rides a **clearance floor rather than a fit** — **11–14.6 units across the shipped set, measured** (`Colossus` 11.0, `One` 11.4, `Globe` 12.6, `Helix`'s 24-level column 14.6, against the 21–24 the wordmark's reach used to hold it at): the fit asks for more than the frame outright (`CLOSE_MAP_SHARE` 1.6) and never binds, so what decides the pass is `CLOSE_CLEARANCE` — a unit and a half of air off the cluster's bounding sphere, which is also what keeps the lens outside the glass plate by as much again at the top of the crane — the nearest ball skimming the lens at that, in genuine close-up. The bearing turns half again as fast — but at under a third of the radius, so the pass crosses the frame *slower* than the wide leg while still visibly moving — and the lens **cranes** the whole time, up the map or down it, between just under the cluster (its underside against the sky, which is the face that says the thing hangs) and just over its top (the top balls and the glass in one frame). One reversible scalar mixes the two poses and one more drives the crane, both smoothstepped, which is what keeps it from reading as a second rigid orbit. And the title **steps aside for it without leaving**: the wordmark hangs 7 units in front of the lens, well inside the pass, so across the approach the block shrinks to a third of its size (`WORDMARK_ASIDE_SCALE` — the owner's ruling was that it stays, small, in its corner) and comes back up across the retreat. A ball nearer than the block does pass in front of the small mark now, which is honest parallax; a ball through the full-size name was the broken look the old clearance was spent avoiding (#254).
  - **The framing is the hanging map's, not a constant** (`FrameOrbitFor`): the aim height is the cluster's own middle and the two stand-offs are fits against its measured reach. The set runs from a four-level pancake to a twenty-four-level column, and `FitClusterWorldOffset` hangs a deep field higher on top of that (`Helix`'s top level is eleven units above `Nine`'s) — one radius and one aim height framed neither. A **session** frames it too, as it installs a level, because `ResultPage` flies the lens out onto this same orbit the moment a level ends. A newly rolled preview drifts onto its framing over about a second rather than cutting to it.
  - **What the wide leg's length is really holding** is the adaptive-quality probe: it runs off this same `Update` and settles inside the first ten seconds (a 1.5 s warm-up and 1.5 s windows), so it reaches its verdict on wide frames. A cycle that flew in sooner would let a screen full of cluster decide the tier the whole game runs at. Every front-end route that *reopens* the probe puts the flight back at the start of that leg with it — a fresh program, a rolled preview (which is what returning from a level does, and returning from a level is what reopens it), a release from the result screen; the one exception is the fullscreen toggle, which reopens the probe wherever the flight has got to. That is left alone on measurement: through a full cycle on the heaviest case there is — 959 balls under the neon city, `quality=high`, vsync off — the frame rate stayed between 330 and 420 FPS and the close leg read *higher* than the wide one, the swing being the city's visible-building count and not the balls.
  - **`preview=<n|name>`** pins which map the front end hangs, the way `level=` pins which one is played. With the camera framed per map, two shots of the front end are two shots of different scenes unless it is passed.
  - **`balls=<beach|bubble>`** draws every ball in one style whatever the level files say (#258), for both the preview and the played level. It is a testing lever and not a setting, and the distinction is `scene=`'s: the style is a property of the *map*, chosen by whoever built it, so a player who could override it globally would be overriding the author. What it is for is the only honest way to judge the two looks — the same cluster, the same stand-off, the same dome, once in each material.
- **The scene is a real setting, not a menu backdrop**: `SetScene` is the one place a scene changes, shared by the random pick at startup and the scene screen, and what the player picks is what they then play in. It switches the city's day/neon lighting (a flag and a brightness on the renderer — neither city goes through `SceneRenderer`), applies the sea's and savanna's default domes exactly as the Testbed does, and ends in `ApplySkyLighting`, which is why nothing derives the light rig before it. The scene lights became per-frame for it (the savanna's campfire flickers), replacing the old set-once `ApplyNeonLights` — and they are `SceneLights.Apply` since #75, one copy with the Testbed's. The dark **pit shaft** behind the drain came over from the Testbed for the solid-terrain scenes along with `TerrainHoleRadius`, and that classification is shared too: the five are `SceneRenderer.IsSolidTerrainScene`, which is also where the forest's long absence from two hand-kept lists was finally fixed.
- **Testing it needs either clicks or the pad path, and the pad path is the one to reach for.** The `screenshot` skill's script only sends keys, so a scratch variant that clicks at a fraction of the *client* area is one way; but since #61 the menus are fully keyboard-navigable, and **Down/Enter is deterministic where a click coordinate is a guess** that silently lands on the wrong entry when the layout scale changes. Two things to know when driving it that way: the first Down only *raises* the focus cursor (it does not step), and **whether the cursor is up survives a screen change while the index does not** — so every screen after the first arrives with the cursor already on entry 0, and pressing Down again steps *off* it. The two traps under "The game" in `docs/game-session.md` still apply once a level is running — an external `SetCursorPos` reads as a huge aim delta, and a key has to be *held* across a frame to be seen at all, since the keyboard is polled once per frame. **How long "a frame" is is the part that bites**: the front end at the default 2× supersampling runs at ~10 FPS on this desktop until the adaptive path lowers it, so a 120 ms press — comfortable at 60 FPS — lands between polls and is silently dropped, and the whole scripted flow then walks through a menu that never moved. Hold ~400 ms and pass `ssaa=1` on the command line, which both raises the frame rate and settles the quality dial so it cannot change under the run. And **do not drive the Fullscreen row with Enter to get back out of fullscreen** — going in works, coming out did not, twice, with the window still 3840×1600 at the end of the run; the likely reason is the one above (a mode change moves the pointer, and a pointer move puts the focus cursor down, after which Enter presses nothing), but what is established is only that it is unreliable. **F11 is the same `ToggleFullscreen` with no menu focus in the way**, and the round trip is clean on it.

## The screen stack (Prazsky.Core + Game)

The screen system (#26) lives in **`Prazsky.Core/Screens/`** — `Screen` and `ScreenManager` — and knows nothing about Myra, this game, or how anything is drawn. It is fed a frame and routes it over a stack.

- **Two independent flags, not one "is popup".** `DrawsUnderlying` and `UpdatesUnderlying` answer different questions, and every system that conflates them gets the same case wrong: a pause has to *draw* over the game it froze (or there is nothing behind it to have frozen) while stopping it *updating*, and a settings panel over that pause has to let both draw and neither update. Update walks top-down until something blocks and then runs bottom-up within that slice; Draw does the same and paints back to front. `ResultPage` is the one page over a session that answers **true** to the second flag (#241) — a level that has ended is not a level put down — and "is the game paused" is therefore whether a page that *stops* the gameplay screen is over it, not whether one is over it at all.
- **Stack mutations are deferred to the next frame.** A screen pushes from inside its own `Update` — pressing Play is exactly that — so applying it there would mutate the list being walked. They are applied at the **top** of `Update` rather than the bottom, so a screen pushed on one frame gets its `Enter` and its first `Update` before it is ever drawn; a screen drawn before it has updated shows one frame of whatever its fields started at. `PopTo`'s target type rides in the queued entry rather than in a field, or two queued in one frame would share it and the second would pop to the first one's target. Verified with a scratch harness over the whole set: deferred push, opaque hiding in both senses, a pause drawing bottom-to-top over a frozen screen, a panel over that pause, an overlay that lets what is under it run, `Reset` leaving every screen top-down, several operations in one frame keeping their order, and popping an empty stack.
- **An empty stack is a resting state, not an error** (`Clear`). The game no longer rests there — the backdrop sits at the bottom for the life of the program and something is always on the stack — but the library does not require a bottom screen, and it was that tolerance which let the pages move onto the stack in one step (#26) and the play loop follow in another (#65) instead of both at once.

On the game side, `Game/Screens/` holds a `MenuPage` base, the eight pages (`SplashPage`, `MainMenuPage`, `LevelSelectPage`, `PausePage`, `SettingsPage`, `ScenePage`, `AboutPage`, `ResultPage`), the two screens that are not pages at all (`BackdropScreen` and `GameplayScreen`) and `PlayHud`, which is neither — it is the readout the session draws over itself (see "The in-play HUD" in `docs/game-feedback.md`).

- **What the stack bought is the relationships, not the swapping.** "Settings backs out to the pause that opened it" and "it dims the frame because that pause is a stopped game" were both asked as `_state == GameState.Paused`, from two different places, and a `MenuScreen` enum plus a switch decided what to show. Now the pause is literally *underneath* settings: backing out is `Pop`, and `DimsFrame` is `Manager.Contains<PausePage>()`. The enum, the switch, `CurrentScrim()` and the where-did-I-come-from branch in `MenuBack` are gone.
- **`FrameBlur` is that same question asked about focus, and it is why the two ramps are clocked differently (#178).** A page can also say how far the frame behind it has gone *out of focus* — the pipeline's defocus, see "The end of a level goes out of focus" in `docs/game-feedback.md` — and the host asks the **active** screen for it at resolve time. Two pages answer: `ResultPage`, after a delay, on its own per-frame clock, which is safe because it is the only screen still updating while it is up; and `PausePage`, at once, stamped against the **wall clock** — because `MenuPage.UpdatesUnderlying` is false while a `GameplayScreen` is on the stack, so opening Settings over a pause stops the pause updating, and a ramp advanced in its `Update` would freeze half way through its own third of a second. **Since #214 the session is the third answerer**, and the answer is an interface rather than a type switch (`IFrameBlurSource`: the amount and its *shape* together — a page states the whole-frame 0, the `GameplayScreen` states precise aim's periphery-only 1, each next to the ramp it describes, and `FinishSceneDraw` pattern-matches the active screen once). Still one answerer per frame — whoever is active — so the invariant that nothing writes it from two places stands. Pausing mid-hold is a **cut to the pause's own from-zero ramp**, not a crossfade: the covered session stops updating, so its answer cannot ease out — accepted, because the scrim and the arriving panel own that frame (the full account is in `docs/game-feedback.md`). The default answer is *the pause's answer if a pause is underneath*, which is `DimsFrame`'s shape and the same reasoning: a settings panel opened from a pause still stands over a stopped game, and a frame that snapped back into focus as the panel opened would undo the point of softening it. The pause keeps its scrim as well as gaining the blur; the result screen dropped it.
- **The pages are held for the life of the game**, not made per navigation — nothing is allocated on a button press, and `Contains<PausePage>()` only means something if there is one pause page rather than a fresh one each time.
- **`ShowPage` is the single arrival path**, raised by the page's own `CoveredChanged` — which the manager fires on whichever page ends up on top after a frame's pushes and pops, so a push, a pop, a replace and a reset all come through one place. It refreshes the page, sets its scrim from the stack, puts its tree in the one Myra desktop and re-collects the nav entries.
- **`_screens.Update` and `_screens.Draw` are now the whole frame**, and there is no state split left above them to get out of step. `BS3DGame.Update` advances the wall clock, decides whether edge-driven input may act this frame, and hands the frame to the stack; `Draw` hands it over and then puts the FPS component and the Myra desktop on top. `GameState { Menu, Playing, Paused }` is **gone** — what it encoded is now *which screens are on the stack*: a level is being played when the `GameplayScreen` is on it, and it is paused when a page sits over that screen, which nobody has to ask because the manager simply stops updating what is covered.
- **The splash (`SplashPage`) is the first page that owns a piece of time rather than just a tree**, which is what makes it worth having beyond the issue naming it: it is pushed at boot, ticks in its own `Update`, fades its one label up and away, and **replaces** itself with the front end (it is the only page over the backdrop at boot, so the menu has to take its place — a pop would leave the scene standing with no menu on it). It ignores input for `SKIP_AFTER` — a splash skippable on frame one is skipped by the very click that gave the window focus, and the player never sees it. It samples its own keyboard/mouse/pad snapshots in `Enter` and keeps them **frozen** until `SKIP_AFTER` passes, so a key already down at boot never reads as a skip; sharing the host's is not an option even though `UpdateMenuChrome` now keeps them, because the host tracks no *mouse* edge and its snapshots move every frame. Note for testing: the screenshot harness's title-bar focus click **is** a deliberate press and dismisses it, so capturing the splash needs the focus click suppressed.
- **`LevelSelectPage` is what Play opens** — the set's entries in play order, **as a grid of tiles since #91** rather than a scrolling list, so a player sees the shape of the whole campaign at a glance. A tile (four to a row, built over `MenuTile` — `MenuButton`'s behaviour in tile shape, out of the shared `MenuClickable` core, so the focus highlight, the click sound and the pad's `Tag` activation all come along) carries the number loud, the name under it, and a bottom line that earns its place: the player's best stars, a lock's price ("Locked · 8 ★"), or nothing on an open level not yet cleared. The page is always shown rather than only when the set has more than one entry: making the flow depend on the data surprises the player the day a second level is authored. A missing or broken set offers the built-in cluster rather than an empty grid. `StartGameAt` always rebuilds, even for the entry already standing — picking a level means starting it, and resuming is what Continue is for. The campaign's star total sits under the heading (#92/#111); a locked tile is dimmed to the palette's aside grey (emphasis is brightness, never hue) and its whole mechanics is `Button.Enabled = false` — Myra does not press a disabled button and `CollectNavEntries` collects only enabled ones, so neither the pointer nor the pad has a path onto a locked level. All the state is written in `Refresh` rather than baked into the tree, because what is unlocked moves between two showings of the page while the tree only rebuilds on a resize. The ★/☆ glyphs are Inter's (`FontSmall`/`MenuFontStars`) — Anton, the display face, has neither, and FontStashSharp would silently draw blanks (the settings row's value spells "N stars" in words for exactly that reason). Since #139 a tile's row is **two** labels rather than one string, for the same reason the result screen's is four: the earned run carries the tier's colour and the hollow remainder recedes to `STAR_EMPTY`, and one `Label` is one colour. A level the player took to gold therefore still reads gold on the picker — the two screens share `StarsEarned`/`StarsRemaining` and `StarTierColor` so they cannot drift apart, which is what the old shared `StarText` was for before the halves needed different colours. The lock's price goes in the first label and leaves the second empty: it is a sentence, not a rating.
  - **What does not fit a tile moves to the one detail line under the grid** — the entry's rules, or a lock's full arithmetic ("Gem — unlocks at 8 ★, you have 6") — spoken for the tile under the pointer or under the focus cursor, and empty otherwise (fixed height, so the page does not jump). The pointer's half is Myra's own `MouseEntered`/`MouseLeft`, which fire on disabled tiles too, so a lock's price is hoverable; the cursor's half is `MenuPage.NavFocusChanged`, raised by `ApplyNavHighlight` with the button the cursor stands on — null when the pointer takes over, so exactly one device feeds the line at a time, the same rule the highlight itself follows. The issue's other candidate was a tooltip; a fixed line was chosen because it also serves the pad, which hovers nothing.
  - **It still scrolls** (`MenuScroll`), because it is the one page whose length is data — eleven levels fit the default window as three rows of tiles, but the scroller is what keeps a longer set reachable, and the heading, the star total, the detail line and Back stay *outside* it: the way out of a page must not be the thing that scrolled away. Bounded at build time off the live viewport, which is correct because a resize rebuilds the tree.
  - **The nav walk never actually descended into the scroller, and #91 is where that was finally seen.** This file used to say the pad "will happily highlight one that is out of view"; in truth a `ScrollViewer`'s content is not among an `IContainer`'s `Widgets`, so everything inside `MenuScroll` was invisible to `CollectNavEntries` and the first Down landed on Back — the one button outside it. The old list had the same hole with nothing that made it visible; the grid's detail line spelled it out (Back lit, detail empty). The walk now descends into `ScrollViewer.Content` by name. The walk then **did not scroll the focused entry into view**, which this file deferred to "the day a set outgrows the window" — and #245 is that day arriving twice over: the set has scrolled since thirty entries, and at forty the focus walked clean off the bottom while the list never moved. Captured on `main` before the fix: after 34 Downs the grid still showed tiles 1–20, **nothing was highlighted anywhere in the frame**, and the detail line underneath read "Garland — 54 shots" — the focus was two screens down, invisible, and only the mouse wheel could find it. `ScrollNavEntryIntoView` now runs as the last line of `StepNavFocus`, and both pickers get it (`MenuScroll` is used by the level list and the scene list).
  - **It works in global coordinates and moves the scroll by a difference**, which is the whole reason it is short: `Widget.ToGlobal` already has whatever scroll offset is in force folded into the entry's on-screen position, so the code asks "how far outside the viewport is it?" and shifts `ScrollViewer.ScrollPosition` by exactly that, clamped to `ScrollMaximum`. There is no second copy of Myra's own composition arithmetic to fall out of step with the library. An entry already fully in view is left alone, so stepping inside the visible rows does not nudge the list.
  - **Which scroller an entry belongs to is recorded by the walk that finds it**, as a list parallel to `_navEntries`, rather than looked up by climbing `Parent` at step time — the walk is the thing that descended into the scroller, so it already knows. Entries outside one (the headings, Back) carry null and simply do not scroll, which is correct: Back is always visible, and the list stays where the player left it.
  - **Two guards, both reachable rather than theoretical.** Bounds are zero before the first layout pass and `CollectNavEntries` can run on the frame a page is built, so a zero-height viewport or entry returns without touching the scroll — otherwise every entry reads as out of view and the position goes somewhere meaningless. And the shift is clamped to `[0, ScrollMaximum.Y]`, which is what keeps the margin from pushing past the end of the list at the last tile.
  - **Verifying it needs `quality=low` and a 400 ms hold**, for the reason two bullets up: the presses have to survive a 60 Hz poll, and this walk is forty-five of them since #253. The pass criterion is the pair — **the highlighted tile in frame *and* the detail line naming the same level** — because either alone was already true before the fix.
- **The window's title bar names the level being played** (`ShowLevelInTitle`, numbered as the picker numbers it: `Bubble Shooter 3D — 3. Mosaic`). It is set by `EnterPlaying` and cleared by `ReturnToMainMenu`, which is deliberately **not** the same as the session's lifetime: Main Menu *keeps* the session so Continue can resume it, so hanging the title off `BuildLevel`/`TearDown` left the front end still announcing a level nobody was playing. A pause keeps the name — the player is still on that level, and a paused frame is exactly what gets screenshotted. It is for talking about a level rather than for playing one, which is why the HUD carries no name: a title bar is legible in a screenshot and in a window list, and a name is not something read mid-shot.
- **Each page owns its own tree** (`Game/Screens/*Page.cs`): its widgets, its `BuildTree`, its `Refresh`. `BS3DGame` keeps only the frame's share of the menu — the one Myra `Desktop`, the fonts, the layout scale and the palette — and exposes them to the pages through `MenuPage`'s forwarding helpers, so a page's build reads as a description of that page rather than as calls through a host. The trees are built **lazily** against `MenuLayoutGeneration` rather than all eight eagerly on every resize: a page the player never opens never builds one, and the rebuild still reaches a page that is off screen when the window changes size.
- **The result screen lets the camera go.** A level that has ended has no reason to keep the lens behind a gun that cannot fire, so `ResultPage` eases the camera back and out onto the front end's slow orbit and the arena turns behind the numbers. Three things make it work and each was a choice: it is **driven from the page**, because a pose has one writer or it has none — the session goes on updating under this page (#241) and its own `UpdateCamera` would be putting the lens back behind the gun on every frame this one eased it away, which is exactly why the session's covered frame leaves the camera out; it flies onto **`BackdropScreen`'s own orbit** through `AdvanceOrbit`/`AlignOrbitTo` rather than a second one of its own, so "Main Menu" is a continuation instead of a cut to a different view of the same arena, and the orbit is first **aligned to the bearing the lens already stands on** so the release is straight out rather than a chord that crosses the island; and the blend is a **smoothstep**, whose zero derivative at both ends means the camera leaves at rest (it was standing still) and arrives at the orbit's own rate (by then only the orbit is still moving). Since #254 that alignment does two more things in the same breath, both so the release still lands on a pose this page can hand over: it puts the **flight back at the start of its wide leg** (a page of numbers over a camera that dives at the arena a second after arriving is not the moment being asked for — the pass comes round on its own if the player sits there), and it **snaps the framing** to the level's rather than easing onto it, since the camera is about to lerp in from behind the gun anyway. `camera.Update` runs here too, so the last shot's recoil settles rather than being frozen into the pose the player is left looking at. The in-play HUD stops with it — `_pendingOutcome` gates it, which is why that field is now cleared in `BuildLevel`: it was set-and-consumed on one line while the result screen was its only reader, and a retry would otherwise have played with no readout at all.
- **The result screen takes a snapshot (`LevelResult`), not a reference to the session.** The level does not stop the instant it is cleared — the collapse is held for a beat and a player who keeps firing moves the balls remaining — so a screen that re-read the score keeper when it drew printed a row that did not add up to the total above it, which is exactly what happened once. Freezing the figures where the level ends is what makes the arithmetic on the screen true, and it decouples the page from where the session lives. **The headline of a clear is its star rating** (#111): the ★/☆ row under "CLEARED" (in Inter — see the picker bullet for why not Anton), "New best" under it when a best moved, and — exactly when the star total leaves the next level shut — a small note under the breakdown saying what it unlocks at, which is also the one case the Next Level button is absent on a clear. A failed level shows no stars at all: a loss is not a rating of zero.
  - **The rating is STRUCK rather than printed (#139), and the row is four widgets to make that possible.** A Myra `Label`'s glyphs are one run of text in one font and one colour, so a string of ★/☆ can carry neither a per-star colour nor a per-star timing; `ResultPage` builds one `Label` per slot instead and writes the lot from a single `_revealClock` in `ApplyStars`. Each earned star lands in turn (`REVEAL_DELAY_SECONDS` then `REVEAL_STEP_SECONDS` apart), punching in at `REVEAL_START_SCALE` and settling through a small overshoot — an ease-out cubic down past the resting size, then a smoothstep back onto it, so it arrives at rest instead of stopping dead. Four details are load-bearing. The slots are **all four present from the first frame**, hollow until earned, so nothing under the row moves as the stars arrive — a breakdown that shuffled down the screen mid-reveal would undo the point. `Scale` is a **render** transform and `TransformOrigin` is set to the slot's centre, so a star at 2.4× grows in place rather than swinging out of its top-left corner and shouldering the row along — and, being a transform, it never asks the font system for a size, which is the thing `EnsureMenuLayout` is quantized to avoid. The clock is the **only** state, which is what lets a resize rebuild the tree mid-reveal and have the new one come up exactly where the old one was (`Refresh` just calls `ApplyStars`). And the cue is fired from a `while` rather than a per-frame equality test, because a frame long enough to skip a whole step still owes the player every sound — on the class of hardware the quality probe exists for, that frame happens. It is driven from the page for the same reason the camera release is: the session under it is covered, and therefore frozen.
- **The play loop is a screen too now (#65), which is what finally made the two flags do work.** `BS3DGame` went from 4714 lines to 2349 by splitting along the line between what outlives a session and what *is* one: the **host** keeps the window and the device, the content, the shared setting every screen stands in (sky, backdrop, city, island, drain), the linear-radiance pipeline, the screen stack and the one Myra desktop; the **session** — the Bepu simulation and its contact handling, the cluster, the gun and its magazine, the shot and its trails, the game camera and precise aim, the HUD, the scoring wiring and the level install/clear/lose flow — is `GameplayScreen`. A pause is now literally a page pushed over it, so "draw the frozen game underneath while stopping it running" is `DrawsUnderlying`/`UpdatesUnderlying` rather than a branch anybody writes.
- **Both halves are partial across several files now (#71, #72), and the split is staging rather than architecture.** #65 halved a 4714-line file and the two halves grew back: the host was 2805 lines and the session 2602, and the host had outgrown its own `#region` map — the adaptive-quality region sat *nested inside* the menu's, the settings verbs sat inside "The menu's screens", and a lone static `Random` was declared between `LoadContent` and the menu. So the host is now `BS3DGame.cs` (1058: constructor, `Initialize`, `LoadContent`, `Update`, `Draw`, `UnloadContent`, the level set, the session's place on the stack) plus `.Menu.cs` (786), `.Scene.cs` (480), `.Quality.cs` (256) and `.Settings.cs` (135); the session is `GameplayScreen.cs` (834: every field, the constructor, `CoveredChanged`, `Update`, `Draw`) plus `.Rules.cs` (587), `.Session.cs` (364), `.Input.cs` (256), `.Ripple.cs` (239), `.Ceiling.cs` (183), `.Camera.cs` (170), `.Physics.cs` (135) and `.Draw.cs` (83). Two things about this are load-bearing. **A partial hides coupling rather than removing it** — the `_settingsPage.Refresh()` fan-out, the three writers of `IsMouseVisible`, the input snapshots the menu and the play loop share — so the next reader must not mistake fourteen files for fourteen components; the extractions still owed are named in each partial's own doc, and the cluster ripple is the nearest. And **the ordered scripts stayed put**: `LoadContent` and both `Update`s are in the main files because their order *is* the behaviour (the scene before the startup dome before the quality tier, the menu last; the step, then the landings it produced, then the cinematic gate, then the clear, then the loss, then the tear-down), and each is the one place that order can be read end to end. `GameplayScreen`'s fields all stayed in the main file too — which partial "owns" a field is a claim the code cannot enforce, and several are read by four of them. Both splits were verified by a line-multiset diff of the original against the union of the parts, so what moved is provably only what was there.
- **The Win32 left the host entirely (#71).** `Game/Platform/DisplayRefresh.cs` (the monitor's refresh rate, which MonoGame's `DisplayMode` does not carry) and `Game/Platform/WindowIcon.cs` (`PrivateExtractIcons`, because WinForms' own icon path has only one frame to give) between them hold every `DllImport` and every nested type the file used to have. They stay in the executable rather than moving to a library: the libraries target plain `net10.0`, and `WindowIcon` needs WinForms for `Control.FromHandle`.
- **The frame is not "draw the screens back to front", and that is the one thing a naive screen stack gets wrong here.** The pipeline binds an HDR target, draws the world into it and resolves exactly once — and gameplay draws in slots *inside* that sequence, not over the top of it (the balls have to be composited under the drain's glass, and the weather has to settle over the lot before the resolve). So the host owns the *pieces* and the bottom screen owns the *sequence*: `BeginSceneDraw` (bind, clear to the dome's horizon, clouds, sky, backdrop, island, pit) → the screen's own 3D → `DrawSettingGlass` (the drain's gold beads, then its glass) → `FinishSceneDraw` (the foreground weather, then the resolve). `BackdropScreen.Draw` runs that sequence with the gameplay slots empty; `GameplayScreen.Draw` runs the same one with its cluster, gun and trails in them. Neither is ever run twice in a frame, because `GameplayScreen` does not draw what is under it and the backdrop lies dormant while a session stands over it. **That last point cuts both ways, and it is the trap:** because nothing under the gameplay screen is drawn, a frame it declines to draw is a frame with *no target bound, no clear and no resolve* — the back buffer as the last `Present` left it, with the FPS line and a menu page composited onto it. So its un-built branch falls back to `DrawSetting()` rather than returning. That frame is real and lasts exactly one: the result screen's "Main Menu" tears the session down from inside `Update` (immediately, since a keyboard or pad activation runs there) while the stack's pop is deferred to the next frame.
- **`BackdropScreen` exists so that "a menu with no session still shows the world" stops being a special case.** It sits at the bottom of the stack for the life of the program, and its `Update` is the front end's motion — the slow orbit and the adaptive-quality probe. Both are correctly gated for free: `MenuPage.UpdatesUnderlying` is `!Manager.Contains<GameplayScreen>()`, so the orbit stops the moment there is a session to keep the camera posed instead, and this call of the probe only measures frames the menu is actually what is being drawn. That gate is still right — a menu verdict must be about the menu — but it is no longer the whole probe: `GameplayScreen.Update` calls it as well, because a menu frame is the *lightest* of the session and the tier was being settled against it (#121, see "Adaptive quality").
- **`GameplayScreen.CoveredChanged` is the single door into play**, and it is the input hygiene the old `EnterPlaying` carried. The manager raises it on whichever screen ends up on top, so a fresh push, a retry's re-push and the pop of a pause or a result screen all arrive through one path: the mouse-aim baseline is dropped (or the first frame reads the distance from wherever the player clicked to the viewport centre as an aim delta and yanks the barrel across the field), the pad is re-polled so a held trigger must be released, and the cursor is re-captured. The `_previousKeyboard`/`_previousPad` snapshots stayed on the **host** and are shared by the menu chrome and the play loop, which is what makes the handover clean in both directions — the Escape that paused the game is still down on the first menu frame and must not read as a second press, and the Escape that resumed it must not be seen again by the play loop.
- **The one piece of the world that had to be split down the middle is the glass ceiling.** Its *mesh and renderer* are the host's, because they are lit with the rest of the scene and `ApplySkyLighting` has to reach them (through `SkyLightRig.ApplyToGlass` since #156 — the glass stands against the sky, so its three-light rig is dimmed to that sky's own brightness rather than pushed at the enrolment walk's full strength); its kinematic *body* is the session's, because the cluster hangs off it. `RebuildCeilingRenderer` is therefore a host method the session calls per level — a one-line delegation to `CeilingPlate.Fit` since #75, the plate and every figure of it being shared with the Testbed now — and `ApplySkyLighting` still has to run after it, a freshly made renderer starting with no palette. The split itself did not move into the library, and deliberately: `CeilingPlate` owns the drawn box only, and the caller sizes its collidable from the very `FootprintFor`/`THICKNESS`/`CentreYAbove` it passes to `Fit`, so the glass and the body cannot be given different numbers.

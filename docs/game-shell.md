# The game: shell

The shipping product's frame — its menus, the screen stack they live on, and the quality tier the frame is measured against.

Part of the BS3D documentation. CLAUDE.md holds the project overview, the build commands, the ball grid and the repo-wide conventions, and says which of these documents covers what.

## Adaptive quality (Game)

The default of 2× supersampling costs four times the shaded pixels, and on a weak GPU that is the single biggest thing in the frame. Measured on an integrated Vega 10, the **main menu** ran at 13 FPS — so the one setting that would nearly triple it sat behind a menu the player had to sit through at 13 FPS to reach. `TuneQualityToFrameRate` notices for them (#62).

- **It measures, it does not recognise the adapter.** A vendor/name list is wrong on the first machine nobody tested — plenty of AMD parts are discrete and plenty of Intel ones now are — and it cannot see the other reasons a frame is slow: a 4K display, a laptop on battery, something else on the GPU. This is the same "be universal, not hardcoded" rule the rest of the renderer follows.
- **The front end is its own probe.** It already draws the same city, clouds, glare and tonemap the game does, at the same factor, and on this class of hardware it is the *fixed scene cost* rather than the ball count that dominates (#64) — so no separate probe pass, no synthetic workload, and the player is sitting there anyway. The verdict lands about three seconds in.
- **Warm-up is counted in seconds, not frames.** The opening frames are shader compiles, the first touch of each render target and the window settling. A fixed frame count is itself a function of the frame rate, so on the slow hardware this exists for it would wait the longest — exactly backwards.
- **One step per verdict, never upwards, and it latches.** `_qualitySettled` is one-way: set when the player named `quality=` or `ssaa=` on the command line (`Program` passes nullables — null means "nobody said"), when they touch Quality in Settings, when the machine proves fast enough, or when there is nothing left to lower. A quality dial that keeps moving under the player is worse than one merely set too low once, and raising it again on a machine that recovered would put them back where they started.
- **It steps the tier, not supersampling alone** (#63, below). Stepping the factor by itself left the neon city at 30 FPS with 40 % of the frame still on the table. A tier step resizes the scene target (and would rebuild the city if the tiers still differed in radius — since the sort none does), so it **re-arms `_qualityWarmupLeft`** — left unarmed, the very next window measures that hitch and steps again on the strength of it.
- `QUALITY_MIN_FPS` (45) sits comfortably under any display's refresh, so a vsync-capped machine — the normal case — never trips it. A drop is explained on the main menu on its own dark plate, because that screen deliberately has no scrim and a line of small text over open water is otherwise unreadable.
- **Not persisted.** Nothing in this game is: there is no settings file yet, so every run re-measures. Cheap, and it means a machine that was busy once is not penalised for ever.

## The quality tier, and what the frame actually costs (Game)

One bundled **`Quality: Low / Medium / High`** setting (#63) replaced the lone Antialiasing row, because supersampling was never a performance dial — it is tied to a look decision (it is what keeps the balls' procedural relief sharp) and it was the only setting that reached the rest of the frame at all. `QualityLevel`/`QualityPreset` (`Game/QualityLevel.cs`) hold the tiers; `ApplyQuality` is the **one** writer, on `SetScene`'s rule, so the adaptive probe, the settings row and the command line cannot disagree about what a tier means.

What each tier turns down was **measured, not guessed** (#64), because until it was, both issues were naming candidates by intuition — and intuition got the ranking wrong. `logfps` writes one frame-rate line a second to stdout with every setting that changes what it means, and `scene=`/`sky=` pin the backdrop, without which nothing is comparable: **the scene is picked at random per launch**. `.claude/skills/benchmark` drives the lot.

- **The order of magnitude is the scene, not the setting.** Front end, windowed 1600×900, vsync off, integrated Radeon (Ryzen 7 5700U), at the default settings **when the tiers were chosen**: sea 14.6 ms, meadow 15.5, savanna 16.6, desert 16.9, mountain 19.1 — and then **city 76 ms and neon city 103 ms**. A spread of 6.8× on identical settings. (The front-to-back sort has since collapsed the city figures to 23.0 and 27.5 ms — a spread of 1.9× — see "Drawing the city near to far" in `docs/rendering.md`; the terrain figures stand, there being no city in them. The dream and the cavern, added later and then rebuilt on the fractal noise library, measure 61.7/18.8 and 56.5/17.5 at `High`/`Medium` — their `High` is a desktop setting by design, and the probe steps this class of hardware to `Medium`, where both hold 53–57 FPS.) The five terrain scenes never needed a tier; the two city scenes were unplayable without one, and one launch in eleven lands in each.
  - **Space measures 14.6 ms on the same machine and settings — the sea's own figure, i.e. the cheapest scene in the game** (68.5 FPS against the sea's 69.2; the same run put the city at 77.5 ms, matching its published 76). A full-screen procedural sky sounds expensive and is not, because of what it *replaces*: there is no terrain grid, no sky dome, no cloud deck and no per-pixel cloud shadow, and the cloud cost alone is 1.1–10.6 ms depending on overdraw. The heavy parts (each nebula's warped fBm, the Milky Way's structure, the planet's bands) sit behind `[branch]`es on screen-coherent masks, so most of the frame pays for the void, three hashes of stars and one galaxy cell. It needs nothing in a tier.
  - **A trap in measuring any of this: `ssaa=` does not currently override the tier's factor**, though CLAUDE.md and the benchmark skill both said it did. `LoadContent` calls `ApplyQuality(_quality)` unconditionally and its last line is `SetSupersampleFactor(preset.SupersampleFactor)`, which overwrites whatever the command line asked for — so an `ssaa` 1-vs-2 sweep silently measures 2× twice (it was caught by the two columns coming out identical to a tenth of a frame for all three scenes). The `[fps]` line prints the factor actually in force; read it before believing an A/B that varies it. Not fixed here — it is not this scene's bug, and changing it changes every published figure above.
- **Splitting the frame by `ssaa` 1 vs 2 separates per-pixel work from the rest** (the difference is 3× the 1× pixel cost). The terrain scenes carry only ~2 ms of pixel work at 1×; the city carries 17 and the neon city 22. So the city's window-grid shading *is* the fixed cost this hardware pays for, and everything else is inside a 15–19 ms frame.
- **That killed three of the four candidates outright.** Glare is ~1 ms at this resolution — the two passes are sized off the *back buffer*, so they are independent of `ssaa` and cannot move this frame. The per-pixel terrain relief cannot be more than the ~2 ms of pixel work the whole terrain scene has. And the particle counts are not measurable here: the mountain carries 1400 snowflakes and the sea 2000 spray billboards, and they are the 5th and 1st *cheapest* scenes. None of the three is in a tier, and saying so with a number is worth more than a settings row that buys nothing.
- **Measured, on the pre-sort neon city at 103 ms:** the facade grain (three noise octaves per city pixel) is 11.3 ms; the window-frame moulding is 9.1; the two together 20.8, i.e. they add. The clouds — the drawn deck *and* the per-pixel shadow every lit surface pays — are 10.6 ms here, 2.3 on the mountain and 1.1 on the sea: the cloud cost is **per shaded pixel**, so the city's overdraw multiplied it. Since the sort removed that overdraw, the grain and the moulding together measure **0.9 ms at 1×** (`Low` against `Medium` at the same skyline) — the entries stay in the tier for machines weaker than this one.
- **The surprise, and the reason to measure: the city's biggest cost was overdraw, not per-pixel complexity.** Cutting the block radius 14 → 12 saved almost nothing (1.7 ms — the towers still fill the frame), 12 → 10 saved 19.4 ms, and 10 → 8 another 16. The knee was at 10, which is where `Low` used to stop. **A depth prepass or a front-to-back sort would buy this for nothing visually** — the city was one instanced opaque draw in generator order, so it got no early-Z benefit. **That lead has since been taken** (see "Drawing the city near to far" in `docs/rendering.md`), and the re-measured sweep is flat — 14/12/10/8 blocks read 27.5/28.6/28.3/26.4 ms at `High` — so the radius dial is dead, `Low`'s skyline went back to the authored 14, and the knee paragraph above is the record of why it was ever reduced.
- **The tiers, and what they measure at.** `High` is the authored look untouched (ssaa 2, full city). `Medium` gives up supersampling only, which is the one change that reaches every scene. `Low` adds the city's two per-pixel luxuries (the skyline it once pulled in is back at the authored radius since the sort). Since the sort: neon city **36.4 → 95.6 → 103.7 FPS**, ordinary city 43.4 → 108.0 → 119.2 (they read 9.9 → 30.0 → 43.4 and 13.1 → 38.4 → 55.4 when the tiers were chosen); the sea 68.5 → 125 → 125 (identical, because the city dials are dead in five of the seven scenes — `Low` is there for the cities and for machines weaker than this one). `Low` clears #63's "30+ FPS on this class of hardware" on the *worst* scene many times over, and `High` now sits under the probe's floor only in the two cities.
- **`ssaa=` still overrides the tier's factor** — the expert override the benchmark and the screenshot harness both use — and both it and `quality=` latch `_qualitySettled`. The two are not overlapping writers in the UI, because there is only one row now.
- **A trap the startup order sets.** `ApplyQuality` runs from `LoadContent`, before the menu pages exist, so `SetSupersampleFactor`'s `_settingsPage.Refresh()` had to become null-conditional — a command-line `quality=` reaches it long before there is a row to write the value onto. It crashed on the first run for exactly this.
- **Ways to measure nothing at all** — all four have happened, and the skill lists them. MonoGame's `InactiveSleepTime` is left at its default, so an **unfocused** window is capped near 50 FPS and that cap silently becomes the measurement (the benchmark clicks the title bar for this reason alone). Every A/B must pass `quality=` or `ssaa=` explicitly, or the adaptive probe steps the tier down three seconds in and it reads as a win for whatever was being tested. **The front end's camera orbits**, and the cost swings with the view — one neon run read 43 FPS and climbed to 74 over fifteen seconds as the lens reached an angle with fewer towers across it, so a fixed window samples the *expensive arc* rather than an average, and runs of different lengths are not comparable. And a **stray process** from a harness that died between launch and cleanup halves everything measured afterwards with no sign of why.

## The front end (Game)

The game's menus, built on **Myra** — the project's one GUI library, already carrying the map editor's scene-config panel, so the game's menus and the editor's tools are one stack. Each page is its own class under `Game/Screens/`; `BS3DGame` keeps only the frame's share of the menu (the one `Desktop`, the fonts, the layout scale, the palette).

**The seam the whole thing is built around is the screen stack** (#65), and it is what keeps the menu from having to fight the game for anything. It was a `GameState { Menu, Playing, Paused }` enum with a switch and it is now the stack itself: a menu page is *over* the gameplay screen, so no gameplay path runs — no aiming, no shooting, no physics, no game camera — because the manager stops updating what is covered, and the frozen game goes on being *drawn* underneath because the page draws what is under it. A pause therefore shows the game exactly as the player left it, camera shake and all, and the *setting* (sky, backdrop, island, drain) is on screen whether or not there is a session, because whichever screen is at the bottom draws it. See "The screen stack" for the split and for how the frame is sequenced around the one HDR resolve.

- **The world is built on the first "Play", not at load** (`GameplayScreen.BuildLevel`/`TearDown`). The simulation, the ceiling body, the drain's collision mesh and the cluster are the expensive part of starting up and belong to a session; the menu comes up without paying for them. Two consequences worth knowing: the ceiling's **mesh and renderer** are the host's rather than the cluster's (its kinematic *body* is still the simulation's), because `SkyLitRenderers` must be complete before the first `ApplySkyLighting`; and a rebuild disposes the whole `Simulation`/`ContactEvents`/`ThreadDispatcher`/`BufferPool` set rather than emptying the cluster ball by ball — same order as `UnloadContent`, for the same reason.
- **Returning to the main menu keeps the session.** The gameplay screen leaves the stack but *stands*, so the menu offers **Continue** (shown only when `GameplayScreen.IsBuilt`) as well as **New Game**, which is the difference between a mis-click costing a click and costing a game. "Is there a game to go back to" is deliberately `IsBuilt` and **not** whether that screen happens to be on the stack — the two are different questions and only the first one is the player's.
- **`GameplayScreen.CoveredChanged` is the one door into play, and it exists for the input state.** The cursor is captured and recentred every frame while playing and left alone in the menu, so the first frame back would read the distance from wherever the player clicked to the viewport centre as an aim delta and yank the barrel across the field — and the very click that pressed the button would arrive against a stale "released" and fire a shot nobody asked for. Clearing `_mouseAimInitialized` skips that first frame's aim **and** its shot test, since both live behind it; the gamepad is re-polled there so a held trigger or Back button has to be released first. It is the manager that raises it, on whichever screen ends up on top, so every way of arriving — a first push, a retry, the pop of a pause or a result screen — comes through the one path rather than each remembering to do this for itself.
- **Escape hands over through the same `_previousKeyboard` the play loop uses.** `UpdateMenuChrome` stores its snapshot into that very field — which stayed on the host precisely so the two sides share one, rather than the pages and the session each keeping their own — and that is what makes them hand over cleanly: the Escape that paused the game is still down on the first menu frame and is correctly not seen as a second press, and the Escape that resumed it is not seen again by the play loop. `UpdateInput` **returns immediately** after pausing — firing or traversing on the way out would be an action asked of a game that has stopped.
- **`Desktop.Render()` also reads the input, so it only runs where the game's own input has stood down**, and **only while the window is focused**. `Mouse.GetState()` reports the button and a window-relative position whether the window has focus or not, so a click meant for another application that happened to land over a menu entry would press it — and "Quit" would close a game nobody was looking at. Unfocused, the desktop is laid out and drawn (`UpdateLayout` + `RenderVisual`) but not fed input.
- **The look is deliberately greyscale, and that is a constraint rather than a taste.** The menu sits over eleven backdrops whose palettes are nothing alike — a magenta/cyan neon city, an ochre desert, a blue sea, white peaks, green meadow — so any accent colour that reads as the game's own over one of them fights the next. Emphasis is carried by **brightness and opacity**: dark slabs for entries, a step up the grey ramp for hover, white for the active item and grey for the inactive. There are **no drawn frames** either: a border is one more shape competing with every backdrop, and the tone step at a slab's edge already reads as a control. Myra's stylesheet supplies a framed grey tool button by default, so `Border` has to be explicitly cleared. Note each state brush **replaces** the one before it rather than compositing over it, so a translucent hover would show the scene through the very entry the pointer is on — they are all near-opaque.
- **Sizes are authored for 2160p and resolved at build time** (`MENU_DESIGN_HEIGHT`, `Scaled()`), exactly as `InfoRenderer`'s overlay is — Myra measures in pixels, so without this the menu keeps its pixel size and shrinks to a postage stamp at 4K. It is done by re-resolving the sizes and rebuilding the tree, **not** by `Desktop.Scale`: scaling the desktop also scales the full-screen scrim, which then stops covering the frame (tried, and it left the scrim as a rectangle in one corner). `EnsureMenuLayout` runs from `Draw` rather than off the resize event, so the one place it can be stale is the frame about to draw it, and the rebuild is quantized (`MENU_REBUILD_QUANTUM`) so a live window drag asks the font system for a couple of dozen sizes rather than hundreds.
- **Two typefaces split by ROLE, not by weight: Anton for everything loud, Inter for the small print** (both SIL OFL 1.1, each shipping with its licence beside it, both `EmbeddedResource`s rather than content — no path to get wrong, nothing to install, nothing through the MGCB pipeline). Myra's own stylesheet carries a small bitmap font, fine for a tool panel and far too coarse for a title. **Anton sets the title, the menu entries, a result's headings and the whole in-play HUD**; **Inter stays for `MENU_FONT_SMALL`** — About's paragraphs, a level's rules on the picker, the adaptive-quality note — because a condensed display face is drawn for headlines and closes up at the size those are read at. The menu was set in Inter throughout and read as a document rather than as a game.
  - **Anton is the shippable form of the Impact look, and that is a licence decision before it is a taste one.** Impact is bundled with Windows and **not redistributable**, which is the same rule that keeps Segoe UI (the `InfoRenderer` spritefont's source) out of this assembly — `Game.csproj` states it where the fonts are declared. Anton is the OFL face closest to it and is the better-drawn of the two anyway: looser sidebearings and open apertures where Impact closes up. Loading Impact off `C:\Windows\Fonts` at runtime would dodge redistribution but trades the "nothing to install" guarantee for a system dependency and a fallback path.
  - **Three `FontSystem`s, not three fonts in one** — FontStashSharp falls back through a system's fonts glyph by glyph, so a second face added beside the first would never be reached, since the first has every glyph. Picking a face means picking a system.
  - **A single-weight display face costs you weight as a signal, and something has to replace it.** Anton has one weight, so a "bold" slot resolves to the very same glyphs — see the HUD's low-balls alarm, which was a weight step and is a size step now (`HUD_LOW_EMPHASIS`). Anywhere else that reaches for emphasis has to reach for size, colour or motion instead. `_menuFontSystemBold` (Inter Bold) is still loaded and is what remains for the small print if it ever needs a heavier cut.
- **The pages** each own their tree and are swapped into the one `_desktop.Root`; everything that must agree with the game's state (the resume entry, each setting's value, the marked scene, the figures on the result screen) is refreshed in `ShowPage`, because a page can only change while nobody is looking at it. **Splash** · **Main menu** (title, Continue/Play, Scene, Settings, About, Quit) · **Level select** · **Pause** (Resume, Settings, Scene, Main Menu, Quit) · **Settings** · **Scene** · **About** · **Result**. Settings, Scene and About are reached from both the front end and a pause, and neither has to remember which: they are pushed over whatever was up, so "Back" is a `Pop` and the scrim is a question about the stack — which is where the one asymmetry lives: **only a pause dims the frame.** The front end draws no scrim at all, because the rotating scene is the whole point of that screen and a full-screen wash over it throws away the only thing it exists to show; a pause dims hard, because what is behind it is a stopped game. Legibility on the front end therefore comes from the widgets: the entries are near-opaque slabs, and the text-carrying screens sit on a dark **plate** (a slab alone cannot hold small text steady when every line lands on a different piece of moving scene). `DimsFrame` is `Manager.Contains<PausePage>()` and `ShowPage` sets the scrim from it; a null `Background` simply draws nothing, and the root panel still stretches because it is what centres the column and what Myra hit-tests.
- **Every setting is a button that cycles its value** rather than a slider or a drop-down — one widget kind, one click, nothing that can be left half-dragged, and each change takes effect where it is made, so the scene behind the panel *is* the preview. Fullscreen, **quality** (the bundled tier — see "The quality tier"; it owns `_supersampleFactor`, which is what the shared pipeline's `EnsureTarget` sizes the target from, so changing it is what recreates the target), exposure, sky dome (all eighteen), the FPS counter, **Aberration** (the lens's corner fringing) and **Film grain** (the print's animated mid-tone texture) — two taste toggles whose Off writes a zero that skips the respective shader branch entirely; see "Color management" in `docs/rendering.md` — and the four volume rows — Volume, Effects, Music, Ambience, each a percentage of the authored mix, cycling downwards because the reason to click one is almost always "quieter" (see "The sound" in `docs/game-feedback.md` for the split). `_supersampleFactor` and `_exposure` stopped being `readonly` for this; the command line still seeds them.
- **The menus are drivable from a gamepad (#61), and that decides who owns the highlight.** The D-pad, the left stick and the arrow keys move a focus cursor; **A** or Enter presses the focused entry; **B** is Escape, both going through the one `MenuBack` that owns which screens have a back at all. It is not a nicety — Xbox is a target, the game is otherwise fully playable on a pad, and `Buttons.Back` already opens the pause menu, so without it the pad could open a menu it could not then use.
  - **The entries are found by walking the shown widget tree** (`CollectNavEntries`, on a screen change and on a layout rebuild, never per frame), not by a list registered per button — a hand-kept list is one an entry added later silently falls out of, and the entries that come and go (Continue, Next Level) would need the registration repeating anyway. Skipping `Visible = false` is what keeps the cursor off those for free. Myra's `Container.ChildrenCopy` is internal; `Container.Widgets` is the public one. The click action rides on the button's own `Tag`, because the walk has no way back to the delegate `MenuButton` was handed and reaching into Myra's click plumbing would tie this to a version of it.
  - **Exactly one device drives at a time, and this is the part that is easy to get wrong.** The focus cursor reuses the pointer's own hover brush, so a pad-driven menu reads like a moused one — but the pointer does not have to *move* to sit over an entry: a pause recentres the cursor and it lands wherever the column happens to be, lighting a second entry beside the focused one. So while the cursor is up the hover is switched **off** (`ApplyNavHighlight` sets each entry's `OverBackground` to its own resting brush), and moving the pointer puts the cursor down, which restores it in the same pass. Both halves were seen failing in screenshots before they were fixed.
  - **Which entry is focused does not survive a screen change; *whether the cursor is up* does.** Entry 3 of Settings is not entry 3 of the screen it returns to, so the index resets — but a pad player who backs out of a sub-screen would otherwise land on a screen with no cursor and have to press a direction to get one back, every time. `CollectNavEntries` also re-baselines the pointer, or the frame after a pause reads the game's *own* recentring as the player moving the mouse and takes the cursor straight back down.
  - **A held direction steps once, waits `NAV_REPEAT_DELAY`, then walks at `NAV_REPEAT_INTERVAL`** — both wall-clock seconds and not frame counts, or the list would run faster on a faster machine. Accept pressed with the cursor down only *raises* it rather than pressing anything: firing the top entry would make the pad's first press mean whatever happened to be first — "New Game" over a session in progress, on the very screen that exists to offer Continue.
  - The whole thing sits inside `UpdateMenuChrome`'s `IsActive` block, since a pad reports through XInput whether the window is focused or not — the same gate the precise-aim trigger has. Verified end to end on the keyboard path (wake, step, wrap, activate, back, repeat, pause, and the pointer taking over); the pad shares that code but has not been run against real hardware.
- **The menu camera** is `BackdropScreen`'s: it orbits the scene origin at `CAM_RADIUS` (44, well outside the island's 26) roughly level with its target, so the frame is a look *across* the scene rather than down onto it — which is what shows the most of a city, a sea or a mountain range at once. About one turn every 90 s, advanced by elapsed seconds so it takes the same time on any machine. It is the game's one `RecoilCamera` (`Prazsky.Core.Camera`) with the shake at rest, the very one the gameplay screen poses while playing — which is why the orbit has to stop when a session is on the stack, and does (`MenuPage.UpdatesUnderlying`).
- **The scene is a real setting, not a menu backdrop**: `SetScene` is the one place a scene changes, shared by the random pick at startup and the scene screen, and what the player picks is what they then play in. It switches the city's day/neon lighting (a flag and a brightness on the renderer — neither city goes through `SceneRenderer`), applies the sea's and savanna's default domes exactly as the Testbed does, and ends in `ApplySkyLighting`, which is why nothing derives the light rig before it. `ApplySceneLights` became per-frame for it (the savanna's campfire flickers), replacing the old set-once `ApplyNeonLights`, and the dark **pit shaft** behind the drain came over from the Testbed for the four solid-terrain scenes along with `TerrainHoleRadius`.
- **Testing it needs either clicks or the pad path, and the pad path is the one to reach for.** The `screenshot` skill's script only sends keys, so a scratch variant that clicks at a fraction of the *client* area is one way; but since #61 the menus are fully keyboard-navigable, and **Down/Enter is deterministic where a click coordinate is a guess** that silently lands on the wrong entry when the layout scale changes. Two things to know when driving it that way: the first Down only *raises* the focus cursor (it does not step), and **whether the cursor is up survives a screen change while the index does not** — so every screen after the first arrives with the cursor already on entry 0, and pressing Down again steps *off* it. The two traps under "The game" in `docs/game-session.md` still apply once a level is running — an external `SetCursorPos` reads as a huge aim delta, and a key has to be *held* across a frame to be seen at all, since the keyboard is polled once per frame. **How long "a frame" is is the part that bites**: the front end at the default 2× supersampling runs at ~10 FPS on this desktop until the adaptive path lowers it, so a 120 ms press — comfortable at 60 FPS — lands between polls and is silently dropped, and the whole scripted flow then walks through a menu that never moved. Hold ~400 ms and pass `ssaa=1` on the command line, which both raises the frame rate and settles the quality dial so it cannot change under the run.

## The screen stack (Prazsky.Core + Game)

The screen system (#26) lives in **`Prazsky.Core/Screens/`** — `Screen` and `ScreenManager` — and knows nothing about Myra, this game, or how anything is drawn. It is fed a frame and routes it over a stack.

- **Two independent flags, not one "is popup".** `DrawsUnderlying` and `UpdatesUnderlying` answer different questions, and every system that conflates them gets the same case wrong: a pause has to *draw* over the game it froze (or there is nothing behind it to have frozen) while stopping it *updating*, and a settings panel over that pause has to let both draw and neither update. Update walks top-down until something blocks and then runs bottom-up within that slice; Draw does the same and paints back to front.
- **Stack mutations are deferred to the next frame.** A screen pushes from inside its own `Update` — pressing Play is exactly that — so applying it there would mutate the list being walked. They are applied at the **top** of `Update` rather than the bottom, so a screen pushed on one frame gets its `Enter` and its first `Update` before it is ever drawn; a screen drawn before it has updated shows one frame of whatever its fields started at. `PopTo`'s target type rides in the queued entry rather than in a field, or two queued in one frame would share it and the second would pop to the first one's target. Verified with a scratch harness over the whole set: deferred push, opaque hiding in both senses, a pause drawing bottom-to-top over a frozen screen, a panel over that pause, an overlay that lets what is under it run, `Reset` leaving every screen top-down, several operations in one frame keeping their order, and popping an empty stack.
- **An empty stack is a resting state, not an error** (`Clear`). The game no longer rests there — the backdrop sits at the bottom for the life of the program and something is always on the stack — but the library does not require a bottom screen, and it was that tolerance which let the pages move onto the stack in one step (#26) and the play loop follow in another (#65) instead of both at once.

On the game side, `Game/Screens/` holds a `MenuPage` base, the eight pages (`SplashPage`, `MainMenuPage`, `LevelSelectPage`, `PausePage`, `SettingsPage`, `ScenePage`, `AboutPage`, `ResultPage`), the two screens that are not pages at all (`BackdropScreen` and `GameplayScreen`) and `PlayHud`, which is neither — it is the readout the session draws over itself (see "The in-play HUD" in `docs/game-feedback.md`).

- **What the stack bought is the relationships, not the swapping.** "Settings backs out to the pause that opened it" and "it dims the frame because that pause is a stopped game" were both asked as `_state == GameState.Paused`, from two different places, and a `MenuScreen` enum plus a switch decided what to show. Now the pause is literally *underneath* settings: backing out is `Pop`, and `DimsFrame` is `Manager.Contains<PausePage>()`. The enum, the switch, `CurrentScrim()` and the where-did-I-come-from branch in `MenuBack` are gone.
- **The pages are held for the life of the game**, not made per navigation — nothing is allocated on a button press, and `Contains<PausePage>()` only means something if there is one pause page rather than a fresh one each time.
- **`ShowPage` is the single arrival path**, raised by the page's own `CoveredChanged` — which the manager fires on whichever page ends up on top after a frame's pushes and pops, so a push, a pop, a replace and a reset all come through one place. It refreshes the page, sets its scrim from the stack, puts its tree in the one Myra desktop and re-collects the nav entries.
- **`_screens.Update` and `_screens.Draw` are now the whole frame**, and there is no state split left above them to get out of step. `BS3DGame.Update` advances the wall clock, decides whether edge-driven input may act this frame, and hands the frame to the stack; `Draw` hands it over and then puts the FPS component and the Myra desktop on top. `GameState { Menu, Playing, Paused }` is **gone** — what it encoded is now *which screens are on the stack*: a level is being played when the `GameplayScreen` is on it, and it is paused when a page sits over that screen, which nobody has to ask because the manager simply stops updating what is covered.
- **The splash (`SplashPage`) is the first page that owns a piece of time rather than just a tree**, which is what makes it worth having beyond the issue naming it: it is pushed at boot, ticks in its own `Update`, fades its one label up and away, and **replaces** itself with the front end (it is the only page over the backdrop at boot, so the menu has to take its place — a pop would leave the scene standing with no menu on it). It ignores input for `SKIP_AFTER` — a splash skippable on frame one is skipped by the very click that gave the window focus, and the player never sees it. It samples its own keyboard/mouse/pad snapshots in `Enter` and keeps them **frozen** until `SKIP_AFTER` passes, so a key already down at boot never reads as a skip; sharing the host's is not an option even though `UpdateMenuChrome` now keeps them, because the host tracks no *mouse* edge and its snapshots move every frame. Note for testing: the screenshot harness's title-bar focus click **is** a deliberate press and dismisses it, so capturing the splash needs the focus click suppressed.
- **`LevelSelectPage` is what Play opens** — the set's entries in play order, each with what the set says about it (`DescribeRules`), because choosing a level is choosing its rules as much as its layout. It is always shown rather than only when the set has more than one entry: making the flow depend on the data surprises the player the day a second level is authored. A missing or broken set offers the built-in cluster rather than an empty list. `StartGameAt` always rebuilds, even for the entry already standing — picking a level means starting it, and resuming is what Continue is for.
- **Each page owns its own tree** (`Game/Screens/*Page.cs`): its widgets, its `BuildTree`, its `Refresh`. `BS3DGame` keeps only the frame's share of the menu — the one Myra `Desktop`, the fonts, the layout scale and the palette — and exposes them to the pages through `MenuPage`'s forwarding helpers, so a page's build reads as a description of that page rather than as calls through a host. The trees are built **lazily** against `MenuLayoutGeneration` rather than all eight eagerly on every resize: a page the player never opens never builds one, and the rebuild still reaches a page that is off screen when the window changes size.
- **The result screen lets the camera go.** A level that has ended has no reason to keep the lens behind a gun that cannot fire, so `ResultPage` eases the camera back and out onto the front end's slow orbit and the arena turns behind the numbers. Three things make it work and each was a choice: it is **driven from the page**, because the session is *covered* and therefore no longer updated at all — frozen mid-frame is exactly what a pause wants and exactly what this moment does not (the pause is deliberately left alone); it flies onto **`BackdropScreen`'s own orbit** through `AdvanceOrbit`/`AlignOrbitTo` rather than a second one of its own, so "Main Menu" is a continuation instead of a cut to a different view of the same arena, and the orbit is first **aligned to the bearing the lens already stands on** so the release is straight out rather than a chord that crosses the island; and the blend is a **smoothstep**, whose zero derivative at both ends means the camera leaves at rest (it was standing still) and arrives at the orbit's own rate (by then only the orbit is still moving). `camera.Update` runs here too, so the last shot's recoil settles rather than being frozen into the pose the player is left looking at. The in-play HUD stops with it — `_pendingOutcome` gates it, which is why that field is now cleared in `BuildLevel`: it was set-and-consumed on one line while the result screen was its only reader, and a retry would otherwise have played with no readout at all.
- **The result screen takes a snapshot (`LevelResult`), not a reference to the session.** The level does not stop the instant it is cleared — the collapse is held for a beat and a player who keeps firing moves the balls remaining — so a screen that re-read the score keeper when it drew printed a row that did not add up to the total above it, which is exactly what happened once. Freezing the figures where the level ends is what makes the arithmetic on the screen true, and it decouples the page from where the session lives.
- **The play loop is a screen too now (#65), which is what finally made the two flags do work.** `BS3DGame` went from 4714 lines to 2349 by splitting along the line between what outlives a session and what *is* one: the **host** keeps the window and the device, the content, the shared setting every screen stands in (sky, backdrop, city, island, drain), the linear-radiance pipeline, the screen stack and the one Myra desktop; the **session** — the Bepu simulation and its contact handling, the cluster, the gun and its magazine, the shot and its trails, the game camera and precise aim, the HUD, the scoring wiring and the level install/clear/lose flow — is `GameplayScreen`. A pause is now literally a page pushed over it, so "draw the frozen game underneath while stopping it running" is `DrawsUnderlying`/`UpdatesUnderlying` rather than a branch anybody writes.
- **The frame is not "draw the screens back to front", and that is the one thing a naive screen stack gets wrong here.** The pipeline binds an HDR target, draws the world into it and resolves exactly once — and gameplay draws in slots *inside* that sequence, not over the top of it (the balls have to be composited under the drain's glass, and the weather has to settle over the lot before the resolve). So the host owns the *pieces* and the bottom screen owns the *sequence*: `BeginSceneDraw` (bind, clear to the dome's horizon, clouds, sky, backdrop, island, pit) → the screen's own 3D → `DrawSettingGlass` (the drain's gold beads, then its glass) → `FinishSceneDraw` (the foreground weather, then the resolve). `BackdropScreen.Draw` runs that sequence with the gameplay slots empty; `GameplayScreen.Draw` runs the same one with its cluster, gun and trails in them. Neither is ever run twice in a frame, because `GameplayScreen` does not draw what is under it and the backdrop lies dormant while a session stands over it. **That last point cuts both ways, and it is the trap:** because nothing under the gameplay screen is drawn, a frame it declines to draw is a frame with *no target bound, no clear and no resolve* — the back buffer as the last `Present` left it, with the FPS line and a menu page composited onto it. So its un-built branch falls back to `DrawSetting()` rather than returning. That frame is real and lasts exactly one: the result screen's "Main Menu" tears the session down from inside `Update` (immediately, since a keyboard or pad activation runs there) while the stack's pop is deferred to the next frame.
- **`BackdropScreen` exists so that "a menu with no session still shows the world" stops being a special case.** It sits at the bottom of the stack for the life of the program, and its `Update` is the front end's motion — the slow orbit and the adaptive-quality probe. Both are correctly gated for free: `MenuPage.UpdatesUnderlying` is `!Manager.Contains<GameplayScreen>()`, so the orbit stops the moment there is a session to keep the camera posed instead, and the probe only measures frames the menu is actually what is being drawn (which is what makes it a fair probe — see "Adaptive quality").
- **`GameplayScreen.CoveredChanged` is the single door into play**, and it is the input hygiene the old `EnterPlaying` carried. The manager raises it on whichever screen ends up on top, so a fresh push, a retry's re-push and the pop of a pause or a result screen all arrive through one path: the mouse-aim baseline is dropped (or the first frame reads the distance from wherever the player clicked to the viewport centre as an aim delta and yanks the barrel across the field), the pad is re-polled so a held trigger must be released, and the cursor is re-captured. The `_previousKeyboard`/`_previousPad` snapshots stayed on the **host** and are shared by the menu chrome and the play loop, which is what makes the handover clean in both directions — the Escape that paused the game is still down on the first menu frame and must not read as a second press, and the Escape that resumed it must not be seen again by the play loop.
- **The one piece of the world that had to be split down the middle is the glass ceiling.** Its *mesh and renderer* are the host's, because they are lit with the rest of the scene and `SkyLitRenderers` has to reach them; its kinematic *body* is the session's, because the cluster hangs off it. `RebuildCeilingRenderer` is therefore a host method the session calls per level, and `ApplySkyLighting` still has to run after it — a freshly made renderer starts with no palette.

# Rendering

How a frame is built: linear radiance in, one tonemap out. Colour management, the instanced ball shader, the procedural city draw order, scene lights, the weather, and what resolution costs.

Part of the BS3D documentation. CLAUDE.md holds the project overview, the build commands, the ball grid and the repo-wide conventions, and says which of these documents covers what.

## The noise library (Noise.fxh)

`Testbed/Content/Shaders/Noise.fxh`: gradient noise in 2D and 3D under a quintic fade, fBm with a **rotation between octaves**, ridged fBm (Musgrave's ridge-weighted variant), and jittered Voronoi — both the nearest distance and the **edge field** `F2 − F1`, which is zero exactly on cell borders, in 2D and (since #337) in **3D**, where it also hands back the winning cell's own id so a caller can shade each cell rather than only the web between them. Included like `Clouds.fxh` — by the scene shaders, and since #337 by `InstancedModel.fx` too, for the one field on a ball that a sum of waves genuinely cannot be. GPU-only, deliberately — nothing plants objects on these fields, so unlike the cloud noise they need no C# mirror and the hashes are quality-first (Hoskins' sine-free hash, uniform over any domain a scene reaches).

It exists because of a lesson that cost two scenes: **the look of a procedural image is decided by the quality of its fields before anything else.** A sum of plane-wave sines keeps its planes however many terms it has, and the eye names it instantly — the plasma effect of a 1994 demo. It was reported in exactly those words. What separates a modern procedural image from that screensaver is three things, and the library carries all of them:

- **Fractal detail with rotated octaves** — aligned octave lattices read as a grid through the finished field; the rotation is not optional.
- **Domain warping with noise, twice** — feeding one fBm's output into another's *domain* produces the filaments, eddies and mixing real turbulence has; sines under a sine warp still slide as blobs. And **colour by the warp intermediates**, not the final value alone: where the domain was dragged furthest the fluid "mixed", and shading by that is most of why a warped field reads as a substance with an inside (the dream's marbling does exactly this).
- **Occlusion and contact** — nothing in a screensaver ever shades anything else. SDF ambient occlusion (probes up the normal), crevice darkening from ridged fields, and normals *perturbed by the field's own gradient* so every light in the scene agrees about where the surface leans (the cavern's rock) are what give procedural elements weight.


**And since #297 it carries one thing that is not noise: `PerturbNormalFromHeight`.** The tangent-free normal tilt after Christian Schueler is what every height field in the game ends in — the scenes' terrain, the sea's chop, the moon's regolith, the ball's vinyl and the island's stone — and the audit #297 asked for found it copied **character for character into eleven scene shaders and into `InstancedModel.fx`**. More to the point, it found that the twelve were *not the same function*: the mountains and the volcano carried a **determinant floor** the other ten did not. At a grazing angle `ddx`/`ddy` of the world position go near-degenerate, the determinant collapses, and `normalize(0)` is a NaN that reaches the frame as a speck on a far surface — found on the mountains' far peaks, fixed there, and left standing in the dunes, the sea, the regolith, the savanna, the forest, the meadow, Mars, the outback, the tropics and the shared instanced shader. That is the shape #297 exists to attack: **the fix travels no further than the scene it was noticed in**. The guarded version is now the only version, and every file that had a copy already included this header, so nothing gained an include line for it.
The costs are stated per function; callers budget in noise evaluations per pixel. The dream and the cavern spent their way to the look deliberately — see their sections in `docs/scenes.md` for the measured price and how the quality tier absorbs it.

## Drawing the city near to far (Prazsky.Core)

The city is still **one instanced draw call**, but it is no longer handed the raw generator array: `City.PrepareVisible(camera)` fills a `Visible` scratch array with the buildings inside the frustum, **ordered near to far**, and the three executables draw that. It came out of a report that the game fell under 60 FPS in the city at 3840×1600 fullscreen — which it did, and badly.

- **Measure first, and the measurement moved the target.** Split by supersampling at 3840×1600 in a running level: the city cost **13.9 ms** at `ssaa 2` and **4.4 ms** at `ssaa 1`, against the sea's 4.4 ms at `ssaa 2`. Since `ssaa 2` is four times the pixels, that puts ~12.7 of the 13.9 ms in **pixel work** and everything else — CPU, physics, the instance count, the draw call — at about 1.2 ms together. The frame was pixel-bound by a wide margin.
- **So the obvious fix was the wrong one, and this is the part worth remembering.** The complaint was "hundreds of buildings are drawn and only a few are visible", which points at the instance count — but an off-screen building is clipped before rasterization and costs no pixels at all, so culling it saves almost nothing. What costs is a building that **is** on screen and **behind another one**: every pixel of it runs the city pixel shader (a window grid, facade grain, sun, sky hemisphere, cloud shadow) and is then overwritten. **The frustum cull is the cheap half; the ordering is what pays.** In numbers, from the `logfps` line's own `city N/M` field: about **1090 of 1788** buildings are in the frustum from the play camera, so the cull removes ~40 % of the instances — and those 40 % were nearly free.
- **Measured, in a level, firing, 3840×1600 fullscreen, `quality=high`, vsync off:** city **13.88 → 7.66 ms** mean, and — the figure that actually mattered — the **worst second went from 32.3 FPS to 101.5**. Neon city holds a 85.7 FPS floor. The complaint was about the floor, not the average, and the floor is what moved most: the frames that dropped were the ones looking down a long axis of the city, which are exactly the frames with the deepest stack of hidden facades.
- **It is only sound because `CityPS` neither discards nor writes depth.** A shader that did either forces the hardware to run it before the depth test, and every bit of this would buy nothing. Worth checking before ordering anything else in this scene.
- **The ordering is a counting sort, not a comparison sort, and it is deliberately approximate.** Front-to-back exists so the depth test rejects a hidden pixel before its shader runs; a building one bucket out of place costs a few pixels of overdraw and never correctness. So it is O(n) over `DEPTH_BUCKETS` (256), moves each instance exactly once, and the key is the distance to the building's **near side** (centre less its radius) — a big tower close by should sort ahead of a small one whose centre happens to be nearer, because it is the near surface that does the occluding.
- **Nothing in the pass allocates**, per the render-hygiene rules: the bounds (`_centres`/`_radii`) are taken once with the layout, the bucket table and the `Visible` array are allocated with the city, and the **`BoundingFrustum` is held and re-pointed rather than constructed** — it is a *class* in MonoGame, so `new BoundingFrustum(view * projection)` every frame is a per-frame heap allocation on the gameplay path. Assigning its `Matrix` re-derives the six planes in place.
- **A building's bounds fall out of its world matrix for free.** The boxes are `CreateScale * CreateTranslation` with no rotation, so `M41..M43` is the centre and the diagonal `M11/M22/M33` the full size; half that diagonal's length is a sphere radius that circumscribes the box, which is conservative — it can only ever keep a building that was borderline, never drop one that was visible.
- **Verified image-invariant**, which matters because a culling bug removes geometry silently: the same fixed camera (`campos`/`camtarget`) rendered with and without the change differs only in the **clouds, the individual lit windows and the cluster** — all three of which run off the wall clock and cannot repeat between two launches — while every tower silhouette differs by exactly zero. Reordering opaque geometry cannot change the image; that diff is what proves the cull did not quietly eat any of it.
- The `logfps` line carries `city N/M` in the two city scenes, on the principle the rest of that line already follows: everything that changes what the number means belongs on it, and this is the one figure that says whether the cull is doing anything from where the camera is standing.
- **Re-measured on the tier's own reference machine** (#67's "done when": Ryzen 7 5700U integrated Radeon, front end, windowed 1600×900, dome 13, vsync off, 20 s runs): `High` neon **103 → 27.5 ms** and ordinary city **76 → 23.0**; `Medium` 10.5 / 9.3; `Low` 9.6 / 8.4. The block-radius sweep that chose `Low`'s reduced skyline is **flat** now — 14/12/10/8 blocks read 27.5/28.6/28.3/26.4 ms at `High`, run-to-run noise, against 102.9/101.2/83.5/67.3 before — so the hidden towers the radius used to remove are no longer being shaded, and `Low`'s skyline went back to the authored 14 (`Game/QualityLevel.cs`); no tier rebuilds the city any more. The two per-pixel city luxuries the tier still turns off measure **0.9 ms together at 1×** since only visible pixels pay them (`Low` against `Medium` at the same skyline).
- **The depth prepass stays deliberately unused.** `DrawDepth` and the `InstancedDepth` technique were #67's other candidate; a prepass exists to stop hidden pixels being shaded, and the flat radius sweep is the measurement that they no longer are — there is nothing left for it to reclaim.
- **`Draw`'s unconditional `SetData` stopped being a question.** #67 flagged the city's static instance buffer being re-uploaded every frame for nothing; the sorted order changes with the camera, so the city's instance data is genuinely per-frame by construction — and the upload *shrank*: ~1090 in-frustum instances × 88 B ≈ 96 KB/frame against the full 1788 × 88 ≈ 157 KB before.

## The city's windows (Prazsky.Core)

`CityPS` in `InstancedModel.fx` lays each facade's windows out per building (a wall margin held at every edge, the count solved from the tower's size against `WindowPitchX`/`Y`) and decides per window whether it is lit, on a rhythm of its own. What a window is drawn **as** is the subject of this section, because the owner reported more than once that the frames around them could not be seen (#435).

- **The moulding was there and was right, and it could not be seen.** `WindowFrameProfile` builds a raised fillet round each pane with a lit crest and a cast shadow, and `WindowFrameWidth` 0.1 is in `withinCell`'s half-cell units: about **0.085 world units** across a facade. At 1600×900 and the play camera's field of view that is **one pixel at 60 units** and nothing past it, and the whole moulding is multiplied by `resolvable`, which switches it off wherever a pixel covers more than a window. From the island every tower is further than that.
- **What a window reads by at a tower's distance, per references rendered locally for #435** (a 1930s stone tower from across a street, towers looked up at from a plaza, four windows close up), is four flat cues, none of them relief:
  - **the surround**, the ring between the glass and `WindowFrameWidth` past it, in its own tone (`WindowFrameTone` 1.6 times the wall's albedo — a multiple, so the neon city's dark walls keep dark frames);
  - **the sill** under the pane (`WindowSillHeight` 0.14, running `WindowSillOverhang` 0.1 past the surround each side), in the same tone and lighter again on top, over **its shadow** on the wall below (`WindowSillShadow` 0.22 deep, `WindowSillShading` 0.5);
  - **the reveal's shadow** along the head of the recessed glass (`WindowRevealDepth` 0.16), which darkens the pane and halves the lamp behind it there;
  - **glazing bars**, one upright and one rail a third of the way up (`WindowBarWidth` 0.07), which take glass and lamplight out of the pane.
- **Box-filtered, not smoothstepped, and that is what keeps them at distance.** Each piece is the coverage of an interval across the pixel's footprint (`WindowSpan`). A smoothstep across a feature narrower than the pixel still peaks at a half, so a bar a tenth of a pixel wide draws as a half-bright line and shimmers; the box filter gives it its tenth. The sub-pixel pieces therefore blend into the facade's average instead of vanishing or aliasing, and none of them is multiplied by `resolvable`. Checked with the Testbed camera moved sideways by 0.07 units: on the far towers the new build changes no more pixels by over 30 codes than `main` does (2 996 against 3 074).
- **The Game's quality tier still sets `WindowFrameWidth`** (0.1, and 0 at `Low`), which takes the moulding and the surround with it; the sill, its shadow, the reveal and the bars do not depend on it and draw on every tier.
- **Cost:** **+0.05 ms** in the city (2.01 against 1.96 from the play camera `campos=0,-4,30 camtarget=0,6,-60`, 1.92 against 1.87 looking along a street `campos=0,-2,20 camtarget=-60,8,-20`) and **+0.06 ms** in the neon city (2.33 against 2.27), three alternating pairs each against `main`, 1600×900 at ssaa 2× (a 3200×1800 target), `nocap`, every pair within 0.01 of the others.

## Scene point lights (Prazsky.Core)

Beyond the sun and the dome-derived ambient, a scene can carry its own **point lights that exist under every sky dome** — real lights that illuminate, not emissive surfaces that only glow (the city windows and neon glow but light nothing). `InstancedModel.fx` (`AddSceneLights`, called in `ShadePixel`) and `Savanna.fx` both loop over a fixed set of slots — `SceneLight{Position,Color,Range}[MAX_SCENE_LIGHTS]` and `SceneLightCount` — summing each light's Blinn-Phong contribution with a quadratic distance falloff on top of the sun and sky. Colours are linear radiance (bright, over 1) so they cast real colour. `Prazsky.Core.Render.SceneLights` owns the C# half for both executables (it was a copy each until #75): it caches the four parameter references at construction and pushes this frame's set onto the **shared** instanced effect, which is what makes one push reach the balls, the island and its drain, the cannon or gun, and the city at once, since they all draw through that effect. `SceneLights.MaxLights` (8) is matched by `MAX_SCENE_LIGHTS` in both shaders, and raising it here without raising it there writes past what the shader loop reads. The savanna grass shader is deliberately **not** this class's business — `SceneRenderer.DrawSavanna` writes the same four uniform names on its own effect with the count hard-set to 1, out of its own arrays; what the two paths share is the campfire itself, which is what keeps the light on the grass and the light on the balls in step. Scenes without lights leave `SceneLightCount` at 0 (the uniform default), so the map editor — which constructs no `SceneLights` at all — lights no balls, and the shader loop does nothing at runtime; a scene that carries none also only sends its zero **once**, since nothing touches the arrays while the count is zero (measured: four parameter writes a frame saved on the ten scenes that have no lights of their own).

Five are live:
- **The volcano's crater and its flows** (`SceneRenderer.VolcanoLightCount` / `VolcanoLightPosition` / `VolcanoLightColor`): the only scene here whose **ground is the light**, and the only one whose lamps **move**. Slot 0 stands in the crater and takes the eruption envelope on top of its base glow; every other slot is a flow front travelling downhill, swelling in and dying out over its run, which is why the position query takes a wall clock at all. Its bearing mirrors `Volcano.fx`'s own wander term so a lamp sits *on* the river it is lighting — change one and change the other. Everything it pushes is scaled by `VolcanoSceneConfig.LightStrength`, which is a **readability** dial rather than a brightness taste: red and orange balls hang over red-orange lava, and the cluster's tops have to stay the dome's so the red underside reads as under-light. See "The volcano" in `docs/scenes.md`.
- **The savanna campfire** (`SceneRenderer.SavannaCampfirePosition` / `CampfireColor`): a warm point light on the grass just off the island, flickering off the wall clock, that warms the island's near edge, the grass around it and any balls near it. Its visible flame is a separate **additive** billboard (`Flame.fx`) at the same spot — a procedural flickering tongue, bright enough to bloom through the glare but with the blue kept low so it reads as fire, not a white spark. Light and flame flicker together off the one clock. The position is chosen to sit in the low game camera's view (it looks down `-Z` from about `(0,-3,30)`).
- **The neon city's neon** (the `NeonConfig` defaults in `Prazsky.Core/Render/Config/CitySceneConfig.cs`, read off the host's city config — they were `NEON_*` constants in `Testbed.cs`; since the format-2 refactor the config class IS where the fixed figures live, tunable live in the editor's panel): a ring of alternating magenta/cyan point lights around the island, so the near towers, the platform, the cannon and the balls actually take the neon's colour instead of the windows merely glowing. Neon scene only.
- **Space's planetshine** (`SceneRenderer.TryGetSpacePlanetshine`): the light the planet throws back onto the island's flank, standing `PlanetshineDistance` out along the planet's own direction with a range three times that, so the falloff varies by a few per cent across the island and it reads as directional. It is a **real light rather than more ambient** on purpose — ambient is directionless, so raising it to get a coloured flank flattens the whole scene instead, and only a real light puts a highlight back into the drain's metallic gold beads, which have almost nothing but reflections to show. See "Space (Game)" in `docs/scenes.md`.

A fourth is the **Moon's earthshine** (`TryGetMoonEarthshine`), the planetshine's argument at the Earth's colour. The guards are mutually exclusive by construction — no `SceneKind` satisfies two — so their order in `SceneLights.Apply` is an order and not a precedence; a sixth branch must not rely on being tested last. Adding another light is a slot: build a position/colour/range for the scene there (up to `SceneLights.MaxLights`, matched in the shaders), and add the loop to any scene shader that should also catch it (the sea, desert, mountains, meadow, forest and volcano do not — their terrain shaders have no scene-light loop, and the volcano deliberately so: its lava is emissive in its own shader, and the lamps exist to reach everything that is NOT the ground).

**And one light that is no scene's: a blast's flash (#389).** `SceneLights.SetFlash` adds a light to the *next* `Apply` only, in the first slot the scene's own lamps left free, and `Apply` consumes it whether or not it found one — so a flash lights exactly the frame that asked for it, and a host that never asks (the front end, the Testbed, the editor) lights none. It is **dropped rather than evicting a lamp** when every slot is taken, because a flash that put a campfire out for a frame is a flicker, not a flash; the savanna's ring can fill all eight, while the volcano — where the campaign's bombs are — uses six. The session states it every frame a blast is burning (`Blasts.TryGetLight`, a chain folded into one light), before `BeginSceneDraw`. See "A bomb goes off" in `docs/game-feedback.md`.

## Sun shadows (Prazsky.Core, #469)

**Nothing in the project cast a shadow until #469.** What existed was the cloud shadow (`Clouds.fxh`'s `CloudSunlight`, a sunlight factor from the deck drifting over everything) and `InstancedModel.fx`'s relief self-shadow march inside a surface's own height field. A tree on the savanna was lit by `dot(N, SunDirection)` and the grass under it by the same rule with nothing in between, so the plain #451 filled with a few hundred solids still had every one of them floating on an evenly lit floor — the owner's report, and it read exactly that way: a kopje's boulders as bright on the side facing the pile as on the side facing the sun, a trunk under its own crown as bright as one in the open.

⚠ **A shadow map can be swept against its own absence inside one process, and until 2026-09-19 it could not.** `SceneRenderer.ShadowScale` multiplies whichever scene's `ShadowConfig.Strength` is up; **0 means what a `Strength` of 0 means** — no target, no caster pass, every receiver handed 0 — so there is one code path for "no shadows" rather than two that can drift. The Testbed pins it with `shadow=<0..1>` and **alternates it** (`alt=shadow=0;shadow=1`), which is what this feature was missing: #469, #470 and #471 each had to build a worktree of `main` and run two executables to get a pair, and that is exactly the setup that returns a capture pair differing in more than the thing under test — #476 lost a round to it when a uniform was pushed on a path that had not run yet and both halves of its on/off comparison were *off*. `detail=0` is not a substitute: it skips the map but switches several scenes to a reduced program with it. Nothing in the Game writes `ShadowScale` and it is **not** a quality tier — the tier's lever on shadows is `SceneDetail`. See "Sweeping variants inside one process" in `docs/testbed.md`.

**Measured through it, and it agrees with the old rig.** Savanna, dome 5, 1600×900 at ssaa 4, `nopost nooverc nocap`, `campos=0,6,60 camtarget=0,-8,0`, alternating windows in one process with four warm-ups dropped: **8.89 ms at `shadow=0` against 9.07 at `shadow=1`, +0.18 ms.** #471's forest, tropical and meadow figures came out at +0.19, +0.20 and +0.19 through two builds and a worktree, so the one-process reading lands on top of them — which is the cross-check that says the dial is measuring the same thing.

**One orthographic shadow map from the sun, fitted round the camera.** `SunShadowMap` owns a 32-bit `Single` render target (the map's clip depth in the red channel, since MonoGame exposes no depth buffer for sampling — orthographic, so linear) and the view-projection to draw into it: each frame `Fit` takes a centre, a square extent of ground and a height range, transforms that box's eight corners into the light's view and fits the orthographic window to them. **The window is snapped to whole texels in light space** — the sun is fixed per dome, so the box's light-space size never changes while the camera moves, only its centre, and a centre moving by fractions of a texel re-rasterises every caster's edge differently each frame, which reads as shadows swimming over the grass. The same idea as the terrain grid snapped to its cell.

- **The casters** are drawn through `Acacia.fx`'s `ShadowCaster` technique — every `ScatterBucket` and the hearth stones, culling off (two-sided blades and fans, and a closed solid drawn from both sides cannot peter-pan) — by `SceneRenderer.DrawShadowMaps`, **and then through `InstancedModel.fx`'s `InstancedDepth` since #470**: the island's cap and drum (`ArenaIsland.DrawShadow`) and the gun's barrel, carriage, wheels and rollers (`CannonRig.DrawShadow`). That technique and `InstancedModelRenderer.DrawDepth` had been written for shadow mapping long before and **had never had a caller**; what they were waiting for was a map. They take `Shadows.fxh`'s own `ShadowViewProjection` rather than a `LightViewProjection` of their own, so a caster and a receiver cannot disagree about where the light stands.
  - **The glass does not cast, and that is a rule rather than an omission**: a shadow map has no opacity, so the drain's glass funnel or the gun's glazed loading window drawn into it would cast exactly as solid stone — the drain would read as a dark disc inside the island's own shadow. The cap covers their footprint from any sun that clears the horizon anyway. The gold beads and the pit are left out for the same reason and for size.
  - **Who draws them is the host, not the renderer**: the island and the gun are the executables' objects ("the setting, in one copy"), so `DrawShadowMaps` takes a callback and the host fills it. In the **Game** the gun goes one step further — the rig is the host's but its *pose* is a session's, so `GameplayScreen` hands `BS3DGame.SessionShadowCasters` a closure while it lives and its teardown clears it; without that the front end would draw a gun's shadow with no gun standing in it. The **editor** has neither island nor gun and registers nothing.
- **The receivers** include `Shadows.fxh` — the one copy of `ShadowMap`, `ShadowViewProjection`, `ShadowTexel`, `ShadowStrength`, `ShadowBias` and `SunShadow()` — and multiply their **sun term** by it; the dome's ambient is untouched, which is what a shadow is. Nine point taps in a box (PCF) with a **slope-scaled bias** (`SHADOW_BIAS_UNITS` 0.45 world units, turned into the map's depth units off its range each frame, ×3.5 for a surface edge-on to the sun), fading out over the last 6 % of the map's width so the shadowed region ends in nothing rather than a line. `tex2Dlod` rather than `tex2D`, because the taps run under a `[branch]` on `ShadowStrength` and a gradient instruction inside divergent flow is what the compiler refuses. `Savanna.fx` folds the factor into the sunlight the clouds already dim, so shadowed grass neither glints nor glows backlit, and reads its base normal rather than the combed one so the blades' relief does not put the field in and out of its own bias; `Acacia.fx` applies it to `ndotl`. **`InstancedModel.fx` folds it into the very same line the cloud shadow rides (#470)** — `ShadePixel`'s `sunlight`, which is the one place that knows how a pixel is lit — so the island, its drain, the gun, the city and every ball receive from one push, the way `SceneLights` reaches them from one. That is also why the balls pay for it: the tap is uniform across a draw, so a cluster hanging where nothing shadows it still runs its nine taps. Measured below; if it ever matters, the gate is a per-renderer flag on the pattern of `DirLightStrength`.
- **When it is drawn is the whole trick.** `DrawShadowMaps(scene, camera, sun)` is called by all three executables **before their scene target is bound** — the scene target is `RenderTargetUsage.DiscardContents`, so switching away from it mid-frame and back would clear the sky already drawn into it (the cavern's display-resolution path gets away with the same switch only because that scene replaces the sky). The Game calls it first thing in `BeginSceneDraw`, before even the trophy's foreground layer; the Testbed and the editor at the top of `Draw`. It leaves the back buffer bound. A no-op in a scene without a map (the sea, the storm and the six sky-replacing scenes — see "Ten backdrops have a map" below), at the Low tier (`SceneDetail` ≤ 0.5), at `ShadowStrength` 0 and with the sun's direction under `SHADOW_MIN_SUN_HEIGHT` (0.08 — lower and every shadow is a streak the length of the map), in all of which the receivers get a strength of 0 and skip their taps.
- **The fit**: centre at the camera's XZ on the plain's `LevelY`, `ShadowExtent` 260 units square, from half the hill height below the plain to the hills plus a baobab and a half above. 4096 texels over 260 units is 0.063 units a texel since #484 (2048 and 0.13 until then, and still below High — see "The map's size" below); the penumbra of a nine-tap box is about a fifth of a unit, which is a sunlit edge. Shadows exist within 130 units of the lens and fade out beyond, which is where the play camera, the intro and the front end's orbit look.
- **Cost of the island, the gun and the shared receiver (#470):** the same rig. On the scene cameras it is **at the measurement floor** — looking out over the plain 8.14 / 8.12 → 8.14 / 8.20, at a fire 8.60 / 8.56 → 8.60 / 8.55, one pair up, one flat and one a hair down. Expected, and worth stating plainly: the casters are six small instanced draws, and the only new receiver on screen there is the island itself. **Where the receiver is actually paid for is the cluster**, which those cameras have none of — the Testbed hangs no map unless it is given one. With `Giraffe` (315 balls) filling the frame from `campos=0,6,44 camtarget=0,4,0`: **8.57 / 8.54 → 8.62 / 8.60, +0.05 ms**, the sign holding in both pairs. That is the nine taps over every ball pixel of a cluster nothing shadows, and it is the figure to watch when the map reaches a scene with a bigger one.
- **The cluster casts too, since #470's own remaining half.** `BallRenderSet.DrawShadow` walks every bucket collected that frame — every kind and every LOD, breathing balls and specials alike, since a shadow map has no colour and needs none of `Draw`'s per-kind branching — and casts it through `InstancedModelRenderer.DrawDepth`, the same technique the island and the gun already use. Both hosts call it from `DrawShadowCasters`, unconditionally in the Game (gated only by whatever `ShadowConfig.Strength` already gates) and behind a `ballshadow=` Testbed dial for A/B (`alt=ballshadow=0;ballshadow=1`, on `shadow=`'s own pattern). The Game's collection already ran before `BeginSceneDraw` (both `GameplayScreen.Draw` and `BackdropScreen.Draw` collect before it, which is what makes `Balls.DrawShadow` safe to call unconditionally from the host); the Testbed's did not, and had its own `BeginFrame`/`Collect`/`CollectMagazineBalls` moved to the top of `Draw`, ahead of `DrawShadowMaps`, for there to be anything in the buckets yet to cast.
  - ⚠ **The first cut called `DrawDepth` once per non-empty bucket and it was expensive on a weak GPU, for a reason with nothing to do with fill rate.** Every LOD's renderer wraps the *same* `Effect` instance (see `BallRenderSet`'s constructor), so each call's technique switch — into `InstancedDepth` and back to the main technique — lands on shared state, and a level with a few specials loaded can have a dozen or more non-empty buckets. `DrawShadow` now merges every bucket at one LOD into a pooled per-LOD scratch array first (`_shadowScratch`, grown by doubling like the buckets themselves) and calls `DrawDepth` once per LOD that has anything — at most four calls instead of a dozen-plus, which is where the cost belonged: submission overhead, not fill rate.
  - **Measured on this laptop, not the reference 5900X / RX 6900 XT — re-measure there before this number is treated as final.** `Giraffe` (420 balls today; the `+0.05 ms` receiving figure above was measured against 315, so the level has grown since), `campos=0,6,44 camtarget=0,4,0`, dome 14, `alt=ballshadow=0;ballshadow=1`, medians after four of warm-up. At `ssaa=4` (23 Mpix, the same heavy stress the figures above were taken under) the two variants came out noisy — spreads of ±5–8 ms within one variant on this GPU — but consistently separated: **≈113.8 ms against ≈115.9 ms, +≈2 ms**, after the merge (down from an unmerged first reading over +2.7 ms, itself down from the very first, per-bucket cut's own noisier +2.8).   - **Confirmed by eye at a low sun** (dome 5, the check #470's own note above already asks for): `Giraffe`, `campos=0,3,20 camtarget=0,-5,-5`, `ballshadow=0` against `ballshadow=1` — the second frame carries a plain dark patch on the savanna ground roughly the cluster's own silhouette, sitting exactly where `ballshadow=0`'s frame has none.

The map is 16 MB of `Single` plus its 24-bit depth.

⚠ **At the savanna's own dome the island's shadow is nearly invisible, and that is geometry rather than a fault.** Dome 14's sun is high, the cap stands five units over the grass, and the terrain has the island's own footprint cut out of it — so the shadow falls into the hole it casts from and only a crescent reaches the grass. ### Every scene with a sun over it (#471)

**The map stopped being the savanna's in #471 and became the setting's.** What #469 built was infrastructure with one customer, and the shape of the code said so: the gate read `scene == SceneKind.Savanna`, the three dials sat on `SavannaSceneConfig`, and the receivers were three named quintets of `EffectParameter` fields. Adding one scene meant five more fields, five more lookups and five more `SetValue` lines in three places; adding ten meant fifty. So the generalisation came first and the scenes after it:

- **`ShadowConfig` on the base `SceneConfig`** — `Strength`, `Extent`, `MapSize` — for the same reason `Weather` is on the base: every backdrop with a sun can throw shadows, and a dial on the base is one the map editor's PropertyGrid picks up for all twenty at once. **`Strength` 0 is the default and means "no map at all"**: no target allocated, no caster pass, every receiver handed 0. A scene opts in where the rest of its look is stated, exactly as `Scattered` lets a scene say nothing about weather and render what it always did.
- **`ShadowReceiver`** is the one copy of "an effect that reads the map": the five `Shadows.fxh` parameters looked up once and pushed together. `RegisterShadowReceivers` collects every one of this renderer's at load and `DrawShadowMaps` walks the array. The shared instanced effect stays apart because it is the *caller's* — it registers itself on the first frame one is handed in.
- **Which scenes are listed there is the feature's inventory**, and it must match `TryShadowFit`'s switch: a scene fitted but not receiving casts into a map nobody reads, and a scene receiving but not fitted is handed 0 every frame.

**Ten backdrops have a map through `ShadowConfig`** — the savanna, the meadow, the forest, the mountains, the desert, the outback, the beach, the volcano, Mars and the polar ice — all at the savanna's own figures (0.9 over 260 units at 4096 since #484, which is 0.063 units a texel — 2048 and 0.13 until then, and still what the Game builds below High). **Sea and Storm are deliberately out, and so are the six sky-replacing scenes.** The storm draws no ground at all — `StormClouds.fx` *is* the cloud, and the island in it stands on nothing a shadow could land on. The sea is water: a shadow inside its Fresnel, foam and subsurface terms is a look decision of its own rather than a line, and nobody has asked for one. Space, the dream, the cavern, the Moon, the aurora and the Grid have no sun above the horizon and `SHADOW_MIN_SUN_HEIGHT` already skipped them.

**The city took a different mechanism, not a tenth copy of this one, and it is the eleventh backdrop.** The city is the one backdrop `SceneRenderer` does not own: `GetSceneConfig` answers **null** for `City`/`NeonCity` (the host holds the `CitySceneConfig`), the towers are the host's `InstancedModelRenderer`s, and `CityStreets.fx` is loaded by the host as well. So all three parts of a shadow — the config, the casters and the receiver — live outside this renderer, and `SceneRenderer.SetHostShadowScene(scene, shadows, groundY, below, above, receivers)` is the API a host calls once at load to hand them over: its own `ShadowConfig`, where its ground sits and how far the map's box reaches below and above it, and (optionally) whichever of its own effects should receive on top of it. Both hosts register the city and the neon city — the neon sun sits under `SHADOW_MIN_SUN_HEIGHT` by construction, so it costs nothing and the day/night split stays in one place rather than being restated as "the city only". See "The city's shadows" below for what it settled on.

**What casts, and who draws it.** The split follows the one CLAUDE.md already draws between the renderer and the host:

| Scene | Casters | Drawn by |
|---|---|---|
| Savanna | the scatter and the hearth stones (`Acacia.fx`'s `ShadowCaster`) | `SceneRenderer` |
| Tropical | 110 palms and the waterline's rocks (`Palm.fx`'s own, new in #471) | `SceneRenderer` |
| Forest | the wood — trees, boulders, stumps (`InstancedModel.fx`'s `InstancedDepth`) | the **host**: it owns the `ForestScatterRenderer` |
| every one of the ten | the island and the gun | the **host** (#470) |

`ForestScatterRenderer.DrawShadow` is `Draw`'s depth twin, and it is **eight calls a variant rather than sixteen** (six before #462's snags and logs): the drawn pass splits a tree into trunk and crown because their *tints* differ, and a depth pass has no tint.

- ⚠ **`Palm.fx`'s caster had to learn the sway, and the sway had to become a function.** A palm's crown is displaced in the vertex shader off `PalmTime` and the wind; a caster that skipped it would throw the shadow of a palm standing still while the drawn one waved. `Sway()` is now one copy both techniques call. What is still one frame stale is the *clock*: the map is drawn before the scene, so `PalmTime` on the effect is the previous frame's. At the beach's sway speed that is under a hundredth of a radian, a fraction of a millimetre at a frond tip, and the alternative is handing `DrawShadowMaps` a `SceneFrame` it has no other use for.
- ⚠ **The rocks cast with the sway strength at zero**, for the reason `DrawTropicalRocks` already gives: they are `LatheMesh`es whose `TEXCOORD0.x` is a circumference, which this shader reads as its sway weight. At the palms' strength the stones shear open — and a sheared stone casts a sheared shadow.

**The fit is computed per scene, not authored** (`TryShadowFit`), and that is a deliberate departure from the issue's own suggestion. A height range written into `ShadowConfig` would be a second copy of the terrain's proportions sitting beside the dials it has to follow — `HillHeight`, `DuneAmplitude`, `RockHeight` — and a copy that drifts *silently*: the map keeps rendering, it simply stops covering what stands in it, and nothing in the frame says why. So each scene's case reads that scene's own figures, with two shared constants over them: `SHADOW_FIT_MARGIN` (10 units of slack, because every one of those figures is a mean rather than a maximum) and `SHADOW_ISLAND_HEADROOM` (12 units over `ArenaIsland.TOP_Y`, because in seven of the ten scenes the island and the gun *are* the casters and a box fitted to a flat plain would clip the very thing throwing the shadow).

- **The mountain and the volcano take half their relief, not all of it.** A range fitted to an 82-unit peak or a 140-unit cone coarsens the bias on the snow at the gun's feet for a ridge no 260-unit map reaches: the peaks are terrain, they stand outside the extent, and they shade nothing but themselves. The savanna keeps #469's own expression to the digit — half a rise below the plain, a baobab and a half above them — because that is the fit that was measured and photographed.

**A map nobody casts into is skipped outright**, and the caller that needed it is the **map editor**: it draws no island, no gun and no wood, so in the eight scenes whose casters are all the host's it would have rendered an empty map and then paid nine taps a pixel to read back that everything is lit. The gate is the `extraCasters` callback itself — a host that registers nothing, in a scene with no planting of this renderer's own, gets no map. Both other executables always hand a callback in, so nothing there changes. In the savanna and the beach the editor keeps its shadows, which is right: that planting is the renderer's and the editor draws it.

**Cost, measured against `main` in the Testbed** — dome 8, 1600×900 at ssaa 4 (23 Mpix), `nopost nooverc`, `fpscap=400`, `campos=0,6,60 camtarget=0,-8,0`, 16-second runs with the first four readings dropped, medians of twelve to thirteen, two alternating pairs a scene, on the 5900X / RX 6900 XT:

| Scene | casters | `main` | this | delta |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Forest | 380 trees, boulders and stumps, the island and the gun | 10.17 / 10.25 ms | 10.38 / 10.41 | **+0.19** |
| Tropical | 110 palms, the rocks, the island and the gun | 7.53 / 7.54 | 7.74 / 7.73 | **+0.20** |
| Meadow | the island and the gun, and nothing else | 7.79 / 7.76 | 7.96 / 7.97 | **+0.19** |
| Savanna (control) | unchanged | 8.88 / 8.96 | 8.91 / 8.97 | +0.02 / +0.01 |

- ⚠ **The meadow costs the same as the forest, and that is the number worth keeping.** One caster against three hundred and eighty, and the delta is identical to two decimal places — so what a scene pays for its shadows is **the receiver**, nine taps over a full-screen terrain shader, and the caster pass is lost in the noise beside it. #470 said the same thing from the other end (its caster-only measurement sat on the floor and the cost showed up on the cluster). Two things follow. The map is about the same price in *every* scene, which is what made rolling it out to nine at once a predictable change rather than a gamble; and if the price is ever to come down, the lever is the tap count or the terrain's branch, not the list of what casts.
- **The savanna at the measurement floor is the control the refactor needed**: `ShadowConfig`, the receiver array and the per-scene fit moved every line of #469's path, and the scene it was built for renders at the same cost within 0.02 ms.
- One `main` savanna reading came back at **35.71 ms** and is discarded as the stray it is (`benchmark` trap 6/12): its pair read 8.98, and four fresh alternating runs afterwards gave 8.88 / 8.96 against 8.91 / 8.97.
- Build under measurement: `Testbed.dll e5d8e31c`, `shaders 32 set c60d9f0f`.

⚠ **Photograph a new scene's shadows under a sun that is actually up.** The trap #470 wrote down applies to every scene and bit again here: the first meadow capture was taken under **dome 5**, whose sun is below `SHADOW_MIN_SUN_HEIGHT`, so the gate correctly drew no map and the frame read as "the feature does not work". Under **dome 8** the same camera shows the island's shadow as a long clean ellipse across the grass. Dome 8 (14° elevation) is the dome to check a new scene with; dome 14, the savanna's own, is the one that hides a shadow in the hole the terrain cuts under the island.

### The city's shadows (#471, the host half)

**The towers cast and receive; the street does not, and that is a measured decision rather than an oversight.** The city sat open after the nine-scene rollout above because all three parts of a shadow there — config, casters, receiver — are the host's rather than this renderer's (`SetHostShadowScene`, above), and because the game camera stands ~60 units out among 25-unit-block towers and 9-unit streets, two or three blocks deep in a canyon: a capture from that vantage is two flat, self-shaded facades with no cast shadow anywhere in the frame to judge, which is what stalled this half twice. Settling it needed the `shadow=` dial (`docs/testbed.md`) so the map could finally be swept against its own absence in one process instead of a worktree, and a vantage the game never uses.

- **The towers cast through `InstancedModelRenderer.DrawDepth` (#470) and receive through the shared `InstancedModel.fx` tap — no registration of their own needed**, exactly as the island and the gun do in the ten scenes above. All of them cast, not `City.Visible`'s camera-frustum-culled set: a tower just off the side of the screen is exactly the one whose shadow falls across a facade being looked at, and it is one instanced draw of some 1800 boxes either way.
- **Found from an elevated vantage the game never stands at** (`campos=300,80,-300 camtarget=0,-40,0`, dome 8, `arena=none`), because the game's own is the canyon-floor framing above that shows no cast shadow at all: a block-diff between `shadow=0` and `shadow=1` at that pose came back with 10.5 % of the frame changed, concentrated in the lower two-thirds where the towers stand rather than in the skyline against the sky, peaks up to 87 of 255 — a crop of one near tower shows a clean dark band across its upper stories where a taller neighbour stands between it and the sun, gone at `shadow=0`. That is the tower-on-tower shading the issue asked whether the map was worth keeping for.
- ⚠ **The street receiver was built, measured and taken back out.** `CityStreets.fx` carried a `Shadows.fxh` tap for one session, gated identically to every other receiver. A street-level capture (`campos=45,-90,90 camtarget=45,-97,220`, inside an actual canyon) paired against `shadow=0` showed the **facades** changing dramatically — the same dark-band effect as above, now covering nearly the whole visible wall in a canyon this deep — while the **pavement** barely moved at all. The reason is in `CityStreets.fx`'s own header: the shader already scales the sun a street pixel gets by an occupancy-density term tuned so a canyon between two 100-unit towers 9 units apart reads as shadowed at *every* sun height, map or none — that term was built for exactly this before the map existed (#399), and the map's extra tap was answering a question the shader had already answered for itself. So `SetHostShadowScene` is called for the city with no `receivers` argument; `CityStreets.fx` no longer includes `Shadows.fxh` at all.
- **Cost, one process, paired**: Testbed, dome 8, 1600×900 at ssaa 4, `nopost nooverc`, `fpscap=400`, `campos=0,6,60 camtarget=0,-8,0` (the game's own vantage), `alt=shadow=0;shadow=1`, eight to nine cycles after warm-up, on the 5900X / RX 6900 XT: **shadow=0 medians ~7.08 ms, shadow=1 ~7.30 ms, +0.22–0.23 ms**, sign holding every cycle. That is the same figure the meadow and the forest measured for one caster against three hundred and eighty (+0.19, +0.19) and matches this session's own finding again: the map costs about the same wherever it is asked for, because the receiving is the expense and not the caster count — **and removing the street receiver did not move this number**, confirming its own cost was already inside the noise floor and the reason to drop it was that nobody could see it, not that it was expensive.
- **Verified**: all four solutions build, `LevelGen` and `ScoreSim` exit 0, the Game plays `Bolt` (a city-scene level) at 108 FPS with no visible regression, the towers' shadow is visible by eye and by block-diff from the elevated vantage, the street pavement is visibly unchanged with the receiver removed.

### The map's size, and the tier it follows (#484)

**The owner's playtest note: "shadows in the scene are blocky — they shouldn't be at High."** They were 2048 texels over 260 units, 0.13 units a texel, and from the play camera the staircase along the island's shadow on the savanna's grass was that texel read at a few units' distance. The issue's two halves — is the map under-tuned, and does the game want an "Ultra" rung above High — were taken in that order, and the first answered itself: **High's budget carries a 4096 map for 0.03 ms**, so the default is 4096 and the second half stays the owner's question.

- **Measured through the Testbed's `shadowmap=` dial** (`docs/testbed.md`), the savanna's play vantage `campos=0,-4,30 camtarget=0,-8,0`, dome 14, `Full.json`, 1920×1080 at ssaa 2, `nopost`, uncapped, alternating windows in one process on the 5900X / RX 6900 XT: **2048 3.45–3.49 ms, 4096 3.50–3.53, 8192 3.66–3.70** — +0.03 ms for 4096 and a further +0.16 for 8192. The receiver reads nine taps a pixel whatever the map's size and the caster pass is a handful of instanced draws, so a size costs its rasterisation and its cache, and neither shows until 8192.
- **What the pictures say.** At 4096 the staircase along the island's shadow is half its size; at 8192 a quarter, and still a staircase — the nine-tap box is three texels wide whatever the texel is, so a finer map *sharpens* the penumbra rather than smoothing it. A soft edge is a PCF question (a wider kernel, rotated or Poisson taps), not a size one, and nobody has asked for it.
- **Memory is what stops at 4096.** The map is a `Single` target over a `Depth24` buffer, eight bytes a texel: **33.5 MB at 2048, 134 MB at 4096, 537 MB at 8192.** Half a gigabyte of card memory for one shadow map, on a game whose other machine is a laptop on shared memory, is not a High setting — that is why 8192 stays a Testbed dial, and what an "Ultra" rung would have to be allowed to spend.
- **The tier owns the size, through a cap rather than a value.** `ShadowConfig.MapSize` is the scene's authored figure, 4096 for every scene ("High — the look the game was authored at"); `QualityPreset.ShadowMapCap` holds it under **2048 at Medium and Low** and is 0 at High for the scene's own; the Testbed's `SceneRenderer.ShadowMapSizeOverride` pins a size outright over both. A cap because a tier only ever takes away (#298): a scene authored small must not come out *larger* on a lower rung. `Low` skips the map through `SceneDetail` anyway, so its entry is for honesty. Seeded in the renderer's initializer exactly as `SceneDetail` is, because a `quality=` pinned on the command line is applied before the renderer exists.
- **The Testbed has no tier and builds every scene's own 4096.** Every figure above and in #469/#471 was measured at 2048; compare against them through `shadowmap=2048`, not against the new default.
- ⚠ **A stray line in the map's rebuild block, found here and moved (#476).** #476 left `_trailWarp?.Dispose()` inside the shadow map's *rebuild* block rather than in the renderer's `Dispose`, so the first map a process built — the first shadowed frame after a scene's build — disposed the savanna's trail-warp texture while the savanna effect was still bound to it; only a savanna entered after another shadowed scene had already built the map kept its field. Nothing threw (the measurements above ran through exactly that path), which is how it stood for two days. It is disposed where the map is now.

## The weather (Testbed)

Procedural clouds that drift, shade the sky, throw shadows across the scene and flatten the light when they cover the sun. They live on **a flat plane at a finite altitude**, not as a texture on the dome, and that is the decision everything else follows from: a dome texture sits at infinity and casts no shadow anyone can derive from it, so what you look at and what shadows you would have to be two fields, and two fields drift apart. With a plane there is one field and three ray-plane intersections into it — the sky shader crosses it with the view ray, the scene shader with the sun ray, the CPU with the sun ray from the arena.

The field is defined **once**, in `Testbed/Content/Shaders/Clouds.fxh` (`#include` works in the MonoGame content pipeline, and `.fxh` edits *do* trigger a rebuild of the `.fx` that includes them — both verified). It is split in two, and the split is what removes the usual CPU/GPU noise-synchronisation problem:

- the **weather** layer is coarse, and mirrored in `Prazsky.Core.Render.CloudField` — the only part that exists twice. Its hash is built from `frac`/`dot`/multiply and no `sin`, because a sine-based hash is exactly where a C# and an HLSL copy would part company.
- the **detail** layer is fine, lives only in the sky shader, and nothing the shadow or the light rig looks at ever touches it.

`CloudField.ApplyTo(effect)` hands the shared parameters to both shaders, so the drawn cloud and the shadow it throws cannot be tuned apart by accident. That is the whole of a **weather** — the shape (plane, scale, wind, coverage) and the character (the detail strength, the opacity, the two billow figures, the character swing and the shadowed underside) — pushed per frame since #221, because all of it changes when the sky does. What `ApplyStaticParameters` still pushes once at load is only what no two skies disagree about: the absorptions, the silver lining, the horizon fade and the sun disc's angular size. Everything following the dome — the lit side's colour and the sun's own direction — is `ApplyDome`'s, which also *remembers* the dome's tint for the underside the per-frame push finishes. All of it is `CloudField`'s own since #75; the values had stood in each executable identical to the last digit, which is drift a shared field should never have been able to have — see "The setting, in one copy" in `docs/scenes.md`.

**The sun itself is drawn since #220** — until then every surface answered a sun that was never on screen (`SunDirection` reached `Sky.fx` only to shade the clouds; the sea's glint sparked off a sun that was not in its sky). The disc is one dot product and a `smoothstep` in `Sky.fx`, added to the dome's gradient **before** the deck composites over it, which puts it behind the weather for free: the same field that shades it occludes it, so a cloud in front of the sun is exactly a cloud drawn over those pixels, and no second ray march is owed. Its shape (radius, edge) is static — one angular size for every sky, pushed by `ApplyStaticParameters`; its colour and its direction are `ApplyDome`'s (per dome), the colour taking the rig's `sunRadiance` **without** the second lerp towards the horizon the clouds' lit side gets — a cloud is a lit surface and the sun is the source, and the rig's own first carry is as tinted as the source itself gets. The radius is deliberately far larger than the true sun's quarter degree (1.6°): the glare pass's sparse sampling grid flickers on a small bright thing — the same lesson the Moon's Earth taught from under the glare threshold — and a disc hot and wide enough to be sampled coherently blooms steadily instead. Verified per dome by shooting six of the eighteen in the running Testbed — day, cool, gold, violet dusk, fiery dusk and near-black — the fiery and violet domes being the ones a warm disc could have clashed on, and none did; a shot with the arena confirms the disc lands where the light already came from, the two answering the one direction.

**And since #220's second pass each sky states where that sun stands** — `SkyDome.SUNS`, an elevation and an azimuth per dome, turned into a direction on every `DomeNumber` assignment and read off the dome by `SkyLightRig.SetSky`. Until then one constant lit all eighteen, so a blood-red dusk and a bright morning threw identical shadows and nothing in a frame but the two palette colours said what time it was. **The elevation is the half that carries the mood**, being what decides how much sun a horizontal surface gets at all, and it is read off the palette: a lit zenith is a high sun (dome 1 at 48°, dome 11 at 55°), a lit horizon under a dark zenith is one on its way out (dome 16 at 4°, dome 7 at 4°, dome 2 at 6°). **The azimuth is spread over the whole circle, and the first cut fenced it into one half on a wrong picture of the camera** — worth writing down, because the wrong picture is an easy one to hold. The gun's orbit is free 360° (`EnsureOrbitAngleInBounds` only *wraps*) and the camera stands behind the gun along its `StandBearing`, so **the camera's own azimuth sweeps the whole circle while a level is played**: every dome is seen front-lit, side-lit and backlit in the course of one level whatever azimuth it states, and the disc comes into frame in every dome as the player walks round. Fencing the suns into the half behind the opening view therefore bought no safety it did not already have — it only threw away half the variety, and with it the one thing the issue asked for by name, that a low-sun scene be *framed against its own light*. What an azimuth actually decides is which **world** direction the light arrives from — so shadows, the sea's glint and the desert's sheen fall differently per dome — and what a level's **opening** frame looks like, the orbit starting at `+Z`: azimuth 180° puts a dome's sun dead ahead of that first view, 0° puts it behind the player's shoulder.

Three things worth knowing about it:

- **Elevation 35° / azimuth 40° is exactly the direction all eighteen used to share.** `-DefaultLighting.Light0Direction` — BasicEffect's own key light — decomposes to those two to the last digit, so that pair is how a dome says "unchanged", and it is what the four sky-replacing scenes keep (`SkyLightRig.DOMELESS_SUN_DIRECTION`). They keep it because they draw no dome to read a sun off, and letting the level's sky byte swing it would walk the Moon's Earth through its phases and slide the space planet's terminator across its face on nothing but which palette a hidden dome carries. **Measured rather than assumed**: the Moon under dome 1 against dome 16, island crop, is a mean absolute difference of **0.000/255**, and space **0.011** against a same-dome run-to-run floor of **0.008** — while the mountain's own before/after over the same crop is **33.1**.
- **What it bought, by looking, on the six scene/dome pairs the shipped levels actually use.** The desert's dunes (dome 6, 35°→22°) get light and shadow sides and read as dunes instead of a flat tan wash; the mountains (dome 8, →14°) get the alpenglow their violet twilight dome was always describing, the sun sitting on the ridge line; the neon city (dome 16, →4°) stops having a noon sun on its night plaza and lets the neon carry the scene. The savanna (dome 14, →30°) and the two day domes are near-neutral by design. **The mountain level is the one that shows what the whole thing was for**: at azimuth 155° its sun stands just over the ridge in the level's own opening frame, which is exactly the "framed against their own light" the issue asked for and exactly what the fenced first cut made impossible. Shot in `BS3D.exe` itself — Five, Basket, Helix, Hourglass, Heart and Cube, one per pair, at their real opening camera rather than at a Testbed vantage, because the opening frame is the only thing an azimuth decides that a player does not immediately walk away from. **The cost is visible and is the island's**: its deck is a horizontal surface and its drum a vertical one, so a low sun both dims the deck and stops raking the drum — the arena reads darker and heavier under the dusk domes. It stays legible with a cluster over it in the running game, which is the check that mattered.
- **The map editor deliberately did not get the disc.** It draws the dome through `BasicEffect` and the disc lives in `Sky.fx`, so giving it one means building `Sky.fx` there — which drags the whole cloud field's evaluation into its sky pass for a deck it does not want (6.4 ms of a 24.9 ms frame, measured below), or a `[branch]` in the game's hot sky shader for a tool's benefit. And it would buy less than it looks: the editor's sky has no weather at all, so a sun in it is no more faithful than a sun-less one, while everything an author actually judges — the terrain, the water, the trees, the balls' lighting — **already follows the dome's sun there**, through the very `SceneFrame` and light rig the game uses.

**Every scene has a sky of its own since #221, and it is a word rather than a set of dials.** One weather was all there could be before it: `CloudField` was one instance at one set of defaults, so the deck over the meadow, the sea, the desert and the savanna was *the same field* — same coverage, same feature size, same wind, over all eighteen domes — and the only variation it knew was **off** (the four sky-replacing scenes' `SuppressOn`). The dial its own documentation called "the one that decides whether the weather is *noticeable*" could not be turned by anything that mattered.

There are **five authored skies** — `clear`, `scattered`, `broken`, `overcast`, `storm` — each a named bundle in `WeatherLooks`, and the vocabulary is the point: a level naming `overcast` asks for a sky somebody authored and can be shown a picture of, where a level carrying eleven floats asks for whatever those floats happened to be and a mistyped one is a sky nobody can name. It is the same curated-pool argument `ProceduralMusic` makes for itself. **`Scattered` is the pre-#221 weather to the last digit**, so a scene that never states one renders exactly what it rendered before there was anything to state.

- **The character had to move with the coverage, and that is most of the change.** Five values told one kind of cloud from another — `DetailStrength`, `Opacity`, the two billow figures and `CharacterStrength` — and they were **constants shared by every executable**, pushed once at load. A fair-weather cumulus and a storm front differ in far more than how much sky they cover, so all five are part of a `WeatherLook` now, and they go out on the **per-frame** push along with the shadow colour: they change when the weather does, and every frame while one is changing. What stayed a shared constant is what no two skies have reason to disagree about — how a cloud swallows light on the way through, how the sun scatters forward through its edge, where the deck fades into haze.
- **The shadowed underside is the weather's *and* the dome's**, so it is the one value that needs both. `ApplyDome` now **remembers** the dome's tint instead of pushing a finished colour, and the per-frame push multiplies the weather's own radiance by it — which is what lets a storm's undersides sit at a quarter of a fair-weather deck's while still taking the dome's colour (the #50 rule, unbroken).
- **Where a sky is authored: the scene says what the place is usually like, the level what it is like today.** `SceneConfig.Weather` is on the base class, so all fourteen scenes state one and the map editor's PropertyGrid picks the dial up for every scene at once; `Level.Weather` overrides it with a word, the way `sky` and `music` already do. An unrecognised word falls back to the scene's own weather rather than throwing — the same leniency the scene and the music take, so a typo is a level under its usual sky and not a level that will not open. The defaults: the desert is `clear` (a desert sky is empty, and that is *why* the scene reads as one), the volcano `storm`, the city `overcast` (morning with the neon off, and a flat light is what a chapter about telling neighbouring hues apart wants behind it), the sea, mountains and forest `broken`, the meadow, savanna, outback and tropical beach `scattered`, and the four sky-replacing scenes `clear` — which is saying so rather than leaving a value nothing can reach.
- **A change of sky fades over 8 s** (`CloudField.WEATHER_FADE_SECONDS`, 2.5 until #411), which is the ambience's rule and not the frame's: a backdrop may be swapped in one frame because it is a different *place*, where a sky closing over is the same place changing. **The owner liked the change and asked for it slower (#411)**: at 2.5 s the smoothstep spent its whole visible middle in about a second and read as a dissolve between two skies; at 8 s it reads as weather moving, and a level's own sky is still up before the chapter intro's 9.5 s hands the gun over. The 2.5 had been chosen to match `SkyLightRig.StepOvercast`'s response so the deck and the light rig arrived together — but that rig is stepped by the Testbed alone, and it follows the deck's *cover* with its own 2.5 s lag whatever the deck's fade is, so the deck still leads and the rig arrives a beat behind it, which is the right way round for light under cloud. ⚠ The issue named `OVERCAST_RESPONSE_SECONDS` as the dial; it is not — the Game never steps it (see "The clouds are the Testbed's weather, minus its ambient half" in `docs/game-session.md`), and it is left at 2.5. `SetWeather` called with the sky already up does nothing, so a caller states its scene's weather on every scene change without having to know whether it changed; caught mid-fade it carries on from where it stands rather than snapping back.
- **What one field cannot do, said rather than discovered later.** #221 asks for this honestly, and the answer is that the five differ in a cloud's *size, density, erosion, darkness and drift* — not in its topology. A **front** with a leading edge sweeping across the sky, lenticular wave clouds standing still in a wind, and a thunderhead's anvil are structures two octaves of gradient noise have no term for; `storm` here is a heavy shredded overcast and not a front, and calling it Storm is a promise about how it *feels*. Giving it one would be a second field and a second set of octaves, which the marched-slab measurements below price out of reach.
- **Verified by looking, which is the only thing that can judge this.** All five were shot over one backdrop under one dome (`weather=<name>` on the Testbed exists for exactly that — five skies cannot be compared while each is tied to the scene that wanted it) and come out plainly distinguishable: clear is empty with the sun disc in it, scattered is fair-weather cumulus, broken has real holes with the sun through them, overcast is a closed sheet with none, storm is dark, low and shredded. The per-dome pass #50 demands was run on the two new extremes: **storm** over a clear day dome, a violet dusk and the near-black 16 takes each one's colour — purple under the dusk, sepia under the dark — and reads as a storm in all three rather than as grey mush in any.
- **The map editor gets the dial but cannot preview it**, and that is the same shape of limitation #220 recorded for the sun disc: the editor draws no deck at all (it builds no `Sky.fx`, so the cloud uniforms stay zero and `CloudSunlight` returns a flat 1). The property is still on `SceneConfig` rather than hidden from the grid, because it is a real property of the scene and the editor is the scene-config editor — what it cannot do is show it.
**The dome's own gradient banded, and the cause was in the palette rather than in the mesh.** The captured sixteen latitude rings carry about ten distinct colours **in pairs** — two rings the same, then a step — so the drawn gradient was a flat plate, a ramp, a flat plate. A slope going zero, steep, zero is a Mach band, and it photographed as a straight horizontal edge across every sky in the game; the closed decks above are what made it impossible to ignore, because no cloud stands in front of it there. Both halves are fixed in `SkyDome`: consecutive rings sharing a colour **collapse into one stop** at the middle of their run, the stops are interpolated into a 256-entry table and the table is **boxed twice**, which rounds the corner at each stop without moving the colours anywhere the eye can find them; and the drawn mesh goes from the captured 16 rings to a generated **64 × 48**, so no single straight segment spans the 19.8° the capture put across the horizon.

- **The capture is untouched and stays the authority on what a sky *is*.** `ZenithColor` and `HorizonColor` are still averages over its 92 entries in its own order, so **the light rig is bit-for-bit what it was** — which is the whole reason the drawn dome and the read palette are now two separate things rather than one set of vertices trying to serve both. Its triangle list went with the drawing: the only load-bearing thing about it was the winding, and that was *measured* off the positions (all 180 faces point outward, which under `CullCounterClockwiseFace` is a front face to a camera inside) and is reproduced by the generator.
- **One trap, which the first cut fell into and a screenshot caught.** The capture's vertex order is the content pipeline's welding order and is nothing like top to bottom — it opens at y = −9.09 and its second entry is y = −9.93, four rings apart in the middle of the lower half. Read as a descending ladder it paints the sky its own ground colour, which is exactly what the first build did: a beige zenith over a beige horizon.
Things that took a while to get right:

- **Shading needs unclamped thickness, opacity needs the clamped one.** With one `saturate` for both, everything inside a cloud is exactly 1 and the interior comes out as a flat wash with a nice edge. `CloudThickness` runs past 1 on purpose.
- **The ACES curve eats cloud contrast.** Two linear values close together up in the highlights tonemap to the same white, so the shadowed underside has to be *well* below the sunlit top (0.18 against 1.7), not a shade under it.
- **Detail strength must be read against `CoverageGain`**, which is what the weather is multiplied by. At 0.55 against a gain of 2.8 the detail modulated the thickness by six percent and the clouds came out airbrushed.
- **Cloud shadows plug into the existing `keyShadow`** in `ShadePixel` — one line, and balls, city, floor and cannon all get them, because they all come through there. They use the coarse layer alone: a shadow thrown from that height has a penumbra hundreds of units wide, so the fine octaves would not survive the trip anyway. `SunDirection` is a separate uniform, not `KeyLightPosition`, which is a point forty units out whose direction fans across the scene.
- **The key light is dimmed per pixel by the shader, the ambient on the CPU, and never both.** Dimming the key on both sides counts the same cloud twice and darkens everything uniformly, which is the look this exists to avoid. Sun goes, sky stays: overcast reads *flat*, not dark.
- **Ambient reads an area, not a point** (`CoverAround`, five taps). "How much sky is over me" is an average by definition, and a single tap lurches between 0 and solid every time a cloud edge crosses it — smoothing afterwards does not turn that back into the quantity that was wanted.
- **Coverage is also the dial for whether the weather is noticeable at all**, which is a different question from whether it looks right: a handsome sky with a fifth of it covered leaves the arena in unbroken sun for minutes, because a shadow only lands when cloud happens to sit on the one patch its sun ray crosses.
- **Cloud color follows the dome** (`CloudField.ApplyDome`, re-run on every dome and every scene switch and never per frame). A cloud has no color of its own — its lit side is the color of the sun and its underside the color of the sky — so the lit side starts from `SkyLightRig.SunRadianceTinted`, the sun's own radiance already carried towards the horizon by `SkyLightRig.SKY_TINT_STRENGTH` and bit-for-bit the sun colour the scene shaders get for the same frame, and is then carried **further** towards the dome's horizon colour (`CloudField.SunTintStrength`) than the scene's key ever is: the scene under a dusk dome still needs a working key light, but a cloud is *in* the sky, and a deck keeping daylight radiance over the neon city's dusk dome read as pasted on — which is what issue #50 filed. Over a bright near-white day horizon the second lerp is close to a no-op, so the day domes kept their look (verified per dome; a launch per `sky=<n>`, because synthetic NumPad presses do not reach the window). The underside takes the zenith, harder — the shadow tint is `CloudField`'s own private figure, since nothing outside it has any business reading how hard a cloud's underside takes the sky. One fixed white cloud over all eighteen domes read as a grey smear pasted onto turquoise day, blood-red dusk and near-black night alike.
- **The deck is shaded off its own slope now, and that is what made it stop reading as a map of its density.** `Clouds.fxh` grew a derivative-carrying twin of its gradient noise (`CloudNoiseD` — analytic, not finite-difference: the shading wants the field's slope, and re-evaluating a seven-octave field twice more per pixel to approximate what the noise already knows is the expensive way to be one step stale) and `CloudThicknessD` returns thickness plus its gradient. `Sky.fx` treats the underside as a surface hanging off the plane where the field is thick (`CloudFormStrength`), tilts its downward normal with the slope, and swings the Beer-Lambert result about its authored value by how much each facet leans into the sun (`CloudShapeStrength`) — flat stretches shade exactly as before by construction, sunward lobe edges come out brighter than the flat top beside them (deliberately unclamped above one), leeward ones darker. **The derivative stops at the fourth detail octave while the value runs to seven**: a gradient scales with its octave's frequency, so summed to the end the finest grain owns the whole slope and the form fizzes — the normal wants the lobes, the value wants the grain on them (iq's `fbmd_8` splits the same way, and his rainforest shader is where the whole approach comes from). The two extra value octaves take the finest grain from ~7 world units — which subtended tens of pixels from the arena and was the deck's whole "low resolution" read — down to ~1.6, with the footprint fade already owning where they may show.
- **Two banks in one sky are different animals now** (`CloudCharacterStrength`): a third, very low octave — far below the weather layer, so it selects whole clouds rather than patches of one — swings the detail strength ±70 % about its authored value, and a bank it lands high on comes out shredded and fibrous next to a rounded, dense neighbour. GPU-only like the detail: nothing the shadow or the light rig reads ever sees it. At ±45 % the swing was invisible against the base strength, which is worth keeping: a variation dial has to be turned until two kinds of thing genuinely coexist in one frame, or it is paying its cost for nothing.

**The deck was given real vertical extent once, and it was rejected on looks — do not build it again without reading this.** The complaint it answered is real and stands: the clouds are a 2-D field read at one altitude, so from a camera underneath they have no silhouette with height in it and streak into horizontal smears towards the horizon. Four approaches were built and measured against it, and the conclusion is that the flat deck wins **on looks**, not merely on cost.

- **Displacing the deck's underside** (the ray hits the surface `CloudFormStrength` already pretends to shade — `nor` is exactly that surface's normal). Fixed-point iteration **diverges** towards the horizon: the contraction factor is the sag depth times the field's slope over the ray's climb, and at climb 0.16 a 60-unit belly moves the sample ~400 units across a field whose features are ~450 wide. The sky comes out marbled like oil. Offset limiting (divide by `max(climb, 1)`) converges and is nearly invisible, because it turns a vertical drop into a distance *along* the ray and a shallow ray descends barely a tenth of it — it stabilises the case that matters by deleting it. A linear search is robust and does work, but the displacement needed to read as volume is **larger than the field's own features**, so the texture smears instead of billowing. The proportions are the reason: ~450-unit features at 190 up with 60 of thickness is a thin, wide sheet, and no 2-D treatment of one has a silhouette.
- **Lowering the deck and strengthening the billow shading**, both free: the first is a wash, the second breaks the clouds into speckle (the normal is owned by the fine octaves at that strength).
- **A slab the view ray marches** — four slices of equal *height*, marching `CloudCover` alone so the shadow's field is untouched and the fine octaves are still applied once at one point under one footprint. This one genuinely delivers: banks with layered, billowing undersides and real interior depth. It was carried all the way to a working `QualityPreset.CloudSlices` (4 at High, 0 — a separate code path — at Medium and Low), and then **judged worse than the flat deck by eye**: blurry, and reading as a hallucinated scene rather than as sky. That verdict is the reason it is not here.

Four traps were found on the way and every one of them is the kind that looks like correct code:

- The marched body **cannot run negative** over clear sky the way `(weather + bias) * gain` does, so `max(thickness, 0)` no longer wipes the fine octaves there and, ungated, the detail alone paints thin cloud across every clear pixel and the dome closes over.
- The path lean is `1/climb` and left unbounded it makes the lower sky a **solid navy wall** — the same lid, arrived at from the other side. Cap it with a softened reciprocal, not `min`: a `min` is continuous but its slope is not, and the crease lands as a Mach band along one line of elevation.
- Sampling the texture where the ray *first meets* cloud **quantises** the sample point onto the slice heights: the same cloud appears at two offset positions with a seam, reading as a doubled cloud. It has to be a continuous function of the ray (a density-weighted centre of mass).
- Two sources of ruled wavy bands, both fatal to the illusion. A belly ramp narrower than the slice spacing makes the sum a **staircase in coverage**, one step per slice, drawn along the field's contours — band-limit the profile to the rate it is sampled at. And the footprint must be measured on the **plane crossing**, not on the moving sample point: the seven octaves each retire against it, so an unstable point retires them along seven contours of their own. Even then the slices stand `depth / climb` apart along the ground, which at the skyline is several whole features — four readings of one cloud become four independent fields sliding at four rates, a **moiré** that no slice count fixes; only fading the depth out with the climb does.

The cost, measured properly on the Game's front end (reference APU, meadow, ssaa 2): the flat deck's whole cloud pass is **6.4 ms of a 24.9 ms frame** (40.1 FPS against 54.0 with `SkyPS` returning the bare gradient), and 7.3 ms on the sea. Marched, the frame goes to 38.0 ms — **40.1 → 26.3 FPS** — and almost none of that is in the slice count: six slices read 40.3 ms against four's 38.0, so cutting a third of the samples bought 2.3 ms of 13. The rest is a fixed penalty, on an integrated GPU almost certainly register pressure against occupancy. **That is the number to weigh any future attempt against**, and it means a quality tier could only switch such a thing off, never scale it down.

**Both of the cheap ideas #104 identified as fresh ground were measured and neither pays.** Testbed, sea, dome 13, `nocap`, a *fixed* camera on the sky, three paired runs each: the deck's own shading costs **1.56 → 0.75 ms** (turning the density to zero), so it is over half the frame there. Against that, approximating the sun-shadow's second `CloudCover` from `thickness` alone **and** cutting `CloudDetailD` from seven octaves to five, *together*, measure **1.56 ms** — the same number, against a run-to-run spread of ±0.03. Not a small win: no win. The deck's cost is spread across the field evaluation itself rather than sitting in either of those, which is the same conclusion the marched-slab experiment reached above from the other direction ("the rest is a fixed penalty… almost certainly register pressure against occupancy") and the same one the forest floor, the cavern and the dream each reached in 2026-08 (see `docs/scenes.md`). **A first sweep of this was run on the Game's front end and produced an impossibility** — two reductions together measuring *worse* than either alone — because that camera orbits and the sky's share of the frame swings with it; a fixed camera is not optional for measuring this scene.

**And the third idea — drawing the deck into a lower-resolution target — has a measured CEILING that does not justify it.** It is the one #104 was left open for, and it needed a number before it was started rather than after. The bound is easy to take without writing any of it: the whole win is the difference between shading the sky at 2× and at 1×, so point a fixed camera straight up (`campos=0,0,0 camtarget=0,80,0`, which frames sky and nothing else) and measure both. On the **project's weakest machine**, Testbed, dome 13, `nocap`, 22 samples a run: sea **6.86 → 3.92 ms**, neon city **6.58 → 3.55**, mountain **7.54 → 4.66**. The saving is **2.9–3.0 ms in all three** — reassuringly constant, because what it buys back is the sky pass's *resolution* rather than anything about the cloud field, which is exactly what the idea targets. But that is the ceiling **with the sky filling the entire frame**; a played frame gives it a third to a half of that, and the tier that would collect it is `High`, since the machines that need the help most are already stepped to `Low`, where `ssaa` is 1 and the saving is **zero by construction**. Against that: a separate target, a composite pass, the softening of a deck whose finest grain is deliberately down at ~1.6 world units, and the quantisation-seam failure mode sitting next door. **Not taken.** For scale from the same run, a frame of *water* costs **42.5 ms at 2×** — six times a whole frame of sky — so the sea's own shader, not the weather over it, is where that scene's time actually goes.

The flat deck cost nothing measurable — 265–338 FPS on `Dense20x10x15` uncapped, the same CPU-physics ceiling as before. The shaded deck costs real sky-pixel work (~1.8× the field's ALU: the derivative twin, two more value octaves, the character tap); the one A/B taken so far was on a session running far below this machine's documented form (the *baseline* read 22 FPS uncapped against the 265–338 on record, so nothing absolute from that day is comparable) and showed ~18 against that 22 on the dense map — re-measure on a healthy session before treating either figure as the cost. `SkyDome` takes an optional `Effect`; leave it null and it draws the plain gradient through `BasicEffect`, which is what the MapEditor does (it does not build `Sky.fx`, so it gets no drawn clouds). The cloud shadow in `InstancedModel.fx` is no longer stubbed: `Clouds.fxh` is always included, and the MapEditor simply never sets the cloud uniforms, so `CloudCoverageGain` stays zero and `CloudSunlight` returns a flat 1.0 — full sun, no shadow.

## Color management (Testbed)

The Testbed renders in **linear radiance** and leaves it exactly once. The scene always draws into a half-float HDR `RenderTarget2D` (`SurfaceFormat.HdrBlendable`, sized backbuffer × `_supersampleFactor`), and `Tonemap.fx` resolves it in one full-screen pass: box filter over the supersampled block, exposure (`DEFAULT_EXPOSURE`, `exposure=<f>` to override), the ACES filmic curve, then sRGB encode. The text overlay and the crosshair draw after it, in display space. The plumbing — the scene target, the bloom pyramid, the resolve and every cached parameter — lives **once**, in `Prazsky.Core.Render.PostProcessPipeline` (#74), and all three executables run it; each passes its own look figures (threshold, intensity, exposure, aberration, grain, factor) through `required` properties, so none can be forgotten and none is re-sent per frame. #75 did the same for what the frame *contains* — the island and its drain, the glass ceiling plate, the dome-derived light rig, the scene's point lights, the drain's collider and the cloud deck's look are one copy each now, and one of the two copies they were reconciled from was measurably wrong about how the ground darkens a ball resting on it. Both facts belong together and the second is recorded in "The setting, in one copy" in `docs/scenes.md`.

The resolve also carries the lens's one deliberate flaw: **lateral chromatic aberration** — the red channel sampled a touch outward of the pixel and the blue a touch inward, along the direction from the frame centre, growing with the **square** of the distance so the middle of the frame (where the cluster and the gun live) stays perfectly registered and only the periphery fringes. The peak displacement at the corners is `CHROMATIC_ABERRATION` and is resolution-independent, the underwater blur's rule.

**That number has been round the houses, and the effect under it is not what it was (#126).** It shipped at 0.0016 — under two corner pixels — and the verdict was that nobody would ever notice, so it was raised to **0.004** to read as a deliberate lens at a glance. At 0.004 it was noticed, for the wrong reason: **one point sample per channel does not blur an edge into a fringe, it makes three displaced copies of it**, and on a line thinner than a pixel the eye reads three separate coloured lines. The island cap's slab joints are a whole regular *field* of such lines, so they came out as a flickering rainbow lattice — which is what #126 was filed as, against the slab shader. Four changes to the joint field moved it not at all; forcing the aberration to zero removed it completely, from the joints and from the balls' rims alike.

**#126 closed leaving a residual, and #351 is that residual: with the aberration off the joints still bead into strings of bright dots, worst on the ones running away from the eye at a grazing angle.** The cause is one sentence and it is not about the groove at all: **`PerturbNormalFromHeight` builds its normal out of `ddx`/`ddy`, a screen derivative is one value per 2×2 quad, and a bevel that climbs inside one or two pixels therefore tilts whole quads at a time** — a row of 2×2 bright blobs strung along the joint. The fix is in `SlabGrooveAxis` and is one number: the bevel's run-out widens with the pixel at **2.5×** the footprint where the joint's own width widens at 0.5×, so the same climb is spread over ~5 pixels and several quads and reads as a line again. The joint's dark core and its near-view bevel highlight are untouched, because at close range both terms fall back on their authored constants.
Four things were measured on the way, each of which killed an otherwise reasonable fix: it **is** the direct highlight (forcing `SurfaceSpecular.Highlight` to 0 on that surface removes the beads outright, so no amount of work on the darkening would have helped); **Toksvig is useless here** — the textbook cure, damping the highlight by the screen-space variance of the normal, measures a variance of exactly zero for the reason above, and drawn out as a colour the whole cap came back black; the beads are **not on the pixels the groove's coverage marks** (damping those to zero left every bead standing); and they are **not a sub-pixel problem** — at the brightest beads the pixel measured *smaller* than the joint, so the joint was fully resolved and beading anyway. Verified at `ssaa=1` and `ssaa=2` in the Testbed and at all three quality tiers in the Game.

So the resolve samples the **spectrum** now: `ABERRATION_TAPS` steps along the shift, each weighted into RGB by overlapping triangles centred on 0, 0.5 and 1. Three taps, which is exactly what the three point samples cost — measured 1.49 ms against the old 1.50 on the sea from a fixed camera, so the fix is free (five taps measure 1.60 and seven 1.69, and neither is distinguishable by eye at this strength). It must not go below three: a single tap lands on the green weight alone, the red and blue weights come out zero, and the frame renders green. What three taps buy over the old code is that the red and blue lobes land at **two thirds** of the shift rather than all of it, and **green** — which carries the luminance edge the eye actually reads — is averaged across the whole span instead of taken from the centre.

With that, the strength is back to **0.0015**, near the figure once called invisible, because the effect it drives is no longer the same one: a soft colour wash at the periphery rather than three thin copies. Measured by eye on the joints from a fixed camera — clean at 0.0015, fringing again at 0.002, and at 0.004 the spectral version is still much too strong. **What was not measured is the other half of the old argument**, whether it now reads at a glance the way 0.0016 failed to; if it wants to be louder, that constant is the dial and the island's joints are what to check it against. Green anchors the geometry, since the eye reads luminance mostly from green, so nothing appears to move when the effect toggles — and it does toggle: the game carries an **Aberration** row in Settings whose Off writes a zero that skips the shader's whole branch, while the Testbed and the editor run the shipped constant for preview parity. The box filter moved into a `SampleScene` helper on `tex2Dlod` for this — a gradient instruction inside a branch is not allowed even on a uniform condition, the underwater blur's own lesson.

The lens's other flaw is the print's: **film grain**, a monochrome per-output-pixel modulation re-rolled every frame — the seed advances off the wall clock the caller hands `PostProcessPipeline.Resolve`, which is what makes it read as film rather than as a dirty pane. It is applied **after** the ACES curve and before the sRGB encode (grain lives on the print, not in the light) and is weighted by `4·luma·(1−luma)`, which peaks in the mid-tones and falls to zero at both ends: blacks stay black — no lifted noise floor over the space scene's void — and highlights stay clean, which is where cheap grain gives itself away. The peak modulation is `FILM_GRAIN` (0.10 — about ±12/255 at 50 % grey; 0.05 shipped first and was invisible at arm's length, the aberration's lesson), one grain per *output* pixel whatever the supersample factor (`OutputSize` is the back-buffer size, not the HDR target's). Its **Film grain** Settings row works exactly like the aberration's: Off writes a zero that skips the branch; the Testbed and the editor run the shipped constant.

Between the scene and the tonemap sits **the bloom pyramid** (`Glare.fx`, #69). A bright pass keeps each pixel's excess over `GLARE_THRESHOLD`, tinted with the pixel's own color so a red ball glares red, into a target **half** the back-buffer size (not the supersampled target — glare is blurry anyway); a chain of dual-filter passes (Bjørge's 5-tap down / 9-tap tent up) then downsamples it to a thirty-second of the frame and accumulates back **up**, each level tent-upsampled *additively* into the one above, so the head ends carrying its own tight halo plus every wider one. `Tonemap.fx` adds the head back **before** the ACES curve, so a glaring ball bleaches through the same highlight roll-off as everything else instead of looking like a decal. This replaced a **six-armed streak star** — long the game's signature, and by the end its most dated element: a star filter reads as a nineties lens-flare pack where the wide soft pyramid is how every modern renderer spends its glow. Two things carried over from the star's tuning history: the intensity dial has been retuned at every optical change (2.6 → 1.3 at the winding fix, 0.9 with the raised threshold, **0.5 now** — the pyramid *accumulates*, so the same subjective glow needs a fraction of the gain), and the point is unchanged — make it unmistakable that the balls, the neon and the crystals emit light, without modeling a lens. Measured on the neon city at `High` on the APU: 27.5 → 27.9 ms, inside run-to-run noise — the pyramid's five tiny targets cost what the one big streak pass did. It also softened the star's one real fault: a one-pixel bright point was sampled stochastically by the old sparse quarter-resolution grid and flickered; the half-resolution bright pass and the tent chain read it every frame.

**The pipeline's one optional stage is the DEFOCUS** (#178) — the frame taken out of focus by the caller's amount, which in the game is the result screen letting the arena go soft a few seconds after a level ends, a pause doing the same at once, and since #214 precise aim softening the frame's *periphery* while the aimed centre holds (see "The end of a level goes out of focus" in `docs/game-feedback.md` for the moments it serves; only the Game ever asks for it, and the Testbed and the editor pass a zero). The shape is the resolve's `defocusFocus` (0–1): at 0 the whole frame takes the amount alike, at 1 — precise aim's — the mix is zero dead centre and grows with the **square** of the distance to the frame's edge, the chromatic aberration's own rule and for the same reason, that the middle of the frame is where the aimed cluster and its landing ghost live (the ghost's display-pixel dissolve dither does not survive being averaged). One uniform and one multiply inside the branch that already ran; the blurred copy itself is built exactly as before. `PostProcessPipeline.DrawDefocus` reads the very HDR scene target the resolve is about to read and `Tonemap.fx` mixes the result in **before the glare add and before the ACES curve**, which is the whole reason it is done in the pipeline rather than as a blur over the finished picture: averaging tonemapped pixels spreads a firework spark's clipped white into the dark around it and gives a grey smudge, where the same spread in linear light carries its energy with it and stays a glowing orb. The glare is added afterwards, from the *sharp* scene, so bright things go on blooming through a frame that has gone soft.

- **It works at a quarter of the back buffer per axis** (`DEFOCUS_DIVISOR`), and that divisor is the trick: a tap's spacing there reaches four back-buffer pixels, so `Glare.fx`'s thirteen-tap separable Gaussian spans a width no full-resolution blur could afford, and the bilinear upsample the tonemap's read performs costs nothing on an image that is blurred anyway. The scene comes down to it in **two** dual-filter steps rather than one, so each working texel is an average of the whole block it stands for — and the kernel's corner taps are placed off the **destination's** texel and not the source's, because the scene target is `SupersampleFactor` times larger again and offsets sized off it would sample one corner of that block and alias the rest away.
- **The tap count is fixed and the SPACING grows** (`DEFOCUS_MAX_STEP`, 1.4 working-resolution texels at full effect). A kernel that gained taps as it widened would step; a fixed-radius blur crossfaded over the sharp frame would read as a double exposure rather than as a lens going soft — so the crossfade is got out of the way early (`DEFOCUS_MIX_IN`, 0.3 of the ramp, while the blur is still narrow) and the rest of the ramp is the radius growing. Verified at four points of the ramp on the meadow at 1600×900: barely soft at 8 % with no ghosting, plainly out of focus at 43 %, and a field of colour and glow at 100 % with the fireworks still reading as bursts. The weights are normalized to sum to 1 because the pass runs in linear radiance, where a kernel that does not visibly dims or blows the frame.
- **Its four targets are built lazily and never in the tools.** `EnsureDefocusChain` runs from `DrawDefocus` and nowhere else, so an executable that never blurs never allocates them; the size test in it is also what carries a resize, and the texture is bound to the tonemap *there*, where it can change, rather than per frame — a rebuild that left the shader pointing at a disposed target would show as a blurred frame going black on a resize. **Costs nothing measurable:** meadow, dome 13, `quality=medium` (ssaa 1), 1600×900, `nocap`, no fireworks — 86–107 FPS across a 30-second run with no step where the blur ramps in. Four passes over a quarter-resolution target is not what this frame is made of. **At `quality=high` (ssaa 2) during play it does measure** — the first step-down reads the supersampled scene target — and #214 is the first caller inside a live level: on the weakest machine (laptop iGPU, level 20 Mountain, 1600×900, `nocap`), a held precise aim ran ~48.1 FPS with the blur against ~50.5 with `ADS_DEFOCUS` zeroed, ≈1.0 ms. Paid only while the button is held, and the adaptive probe watches gameplay since #121, so a machine that cannot afford High with it is stepped down to the tier where the cost measured as nothing.
- **One layer is exempt from it: the sharp foreground** (#225). The result page's trophy cup is the thing the blur is supposed to make room for, not one more thing it takes, so it draws into a second HDR target the pipeline owns — `ForegroundTarget`, configured exactly like the scene's (supersampled or MSAA by the same rule, so the cup's silhouette stays antialiased) and, like the defocus chain, built lazily on first use and never in the tools. Three things keep the exemption invisible as an exemption: the defocus is built from a scene the cup is not in, so no blurred copy of it stands behind the sharp one; the resolve's glare pass bright-passes the foreground layer *additively* into the bloom head, so the metal keeps the glints it was lit for; and `CompositeForeground` tonemaps the layer onto the resolved frame — same exposure, ACES curve, film grain and sRGB encode, run by a second technique of the same `Tonemap.fx` — through premultiplied blending, the layer's alpha being its box-filtered coverage. `Tonemap.fx`'s grain block is a shared function since #225 for exactly that: grain lives on the print, and a composited layer with none would read as pasted on. The layer is drawn in `BeginSceneDraw`, BEFORE the scene target is first bound, and that placement is a measured trap rather than a preference: MonoGame clears a render target on binding it when its usage is `DiscardContents` (that is the platform's whole implementation of "discard"), so the first version's mid-frame re-bind of the scene target — draw cup, swap, swap back — wiped everything already drawn into the scene and left the result page over a black frame on the MSAA path. Every target gets the one bind-draw-unbind lifecycle the framework promises, and nothing is bound twice in a frame. **The premultiplied composite is also what made the crystal trophy possible** (#228): the layer's alpha is not only coverage — a cup drawn translucent writes an alpha below one and the *resolved, defocused* frame underneath simply shows through it, so a see-through reward over live bokeh costs nothing beyond its own draw. Note what that means for the layer's own draw states: they belong to whatever is being presented, not to the frame around it, so `TrophyPodium.Draw` states its blend and depth per tier (opaque metal writes depth, the crystal blends with the write off) and `BeginSceneDraw` only saves and restores around it. **Since #242 the layer carries the campaign-complete confetti as well**, for the cup's own reason — it fell inside the HDR pass and so was defocused with the arena — and that is what turned "the trophy's layer" into a general one: it opens on `trophy.Active || confetti.Active`, the two draws are ordered so the cup still covers the paper, and **anything joining it has to be premultiplied**, which the confetti was not (its `NonPremultiplied` blend squared the alpha into a transparent target, under-reporting every partly covered chip). What a joiner gives up is stated where the confetti gives it up: no scene depth out here, so nothing in the scene occludes it. **And #242 also moved WHEN the composite happens: it is the last picture the frame draws, after the Myra desktop and before the screenshot writer**, where it used to be the first thing after the resolve so that the HUD and a page's panels sat over the cup. The owner ruled against that on both counts — the sparkles should fall over the UI, and the cup may cover a panel — so `FinishSceneDraw` now only *records* the filled layer and `BS3DGame.CompositeForegroundLast` spends it at the end of `Draw`. That is the one place a field was unavoidable: while both halves lived in `BS3DGame.Scene.cs`, "what the frame's close consumes is what its opening produced" held locally, and "last" now means later than the frame's close. It is cleared by the composite itself, so a frame that never reaches the close cannot leave a stale target for the next one to blend, and it must stay ahead of `ServiceScreenshots` or a capture saves the frame without the thing the page is about.
- **And one layer goes the other way: the OVERLAY layer (#438), for what is drawn after the resolve and still wants to go soft with the frame.** The game's in-play HUD and its crosshair are display-space `SpriteBatch` passes over the finished picture, so a page's defocus — a resolve parameter — never touched a pixel of them, and a paused frame had a pixel-sharp score over a blurred arena. Under a page's blur the session draws them into `OverlayTarget` instead: the back buffer's own size, `SurfaceFormat.Color` (finished sRGB display colour, never radiance), no depth and no multisampling, cleared **transparent** by the caller because the alpha is the coverage the composite blends by and MonoGame's discard clear is an opaque purple. `DefocusOverlay` then runs the **scene's own chain** over it — `DrawDefocusChain` is the one copy both callers share: the two dual-filter steps and the separable Gaussian, borrowing the chain's three scratch targets and landing in a quarter-resolution `Color` target of its own, because the scene's `_defocusBlurred` is rebuilt on every frame the resolve reads it — and `CompositeOverlay` lays the result over the resolved frame through `Glare.fx`'s `OverlayComposite`: the sharp layer lerped into the blurred one by the same `DEFOCUS_MIX_IN` takeover the resolve uses, under the same premultiplied `One / InverseSourceAlpha` blend the foreground composite uses (`SpriteBatch`'s default `AlphaBlend` *is* that blend, which is why the HUD needed no change to draw into a transparent target). **Two things had to change in `Glare.fx` for this and neither is visible on the bloom's path:** `BloomDownPS` and `DefocusBlurPS` carry all four channels now rather than writing an alpha of 1 — a premultiplied layer blurred with its alpha spreads its coverage exactly as far as its colour, so a softened glyph's edge stays a soft edge; the bloom pyramid reads only the `.rgb` of what they write. **The order in the frame is the foreground's rule applied again:** the layer is the *first* thing `GameplayScreen.Draw` draws and its blur runs right after, before the scene target is bound, so every target keeps its one bind-draw-unbind lifecycle and the back buffer is still bound once, at the resolve; the composite draws into the already-bound back buffer where the direct HUD draw would have gone. Lazy like the foreground: nothing is allocated until the first frame a page's blur is above zero, and the tools and the Testbed never allocate it at all. Which blur reaches it is the session's call and not the pipeline's — see "The end of a level goes out of focus" in `docs/game-feedback.md` for why precise aim's lens deliberately does not.
- **And the glass in it bends what is behind it (#426).** A layer composited by coverage shows the frame under it undisplaced, and the back buffer it lands on cannot be read while it is being written, so refraction takes a second target and a second resolve:
  - **`PostProcessPipeline.RefractionTarget`**, configured exactly like `ForegroundTarget` (the same size and sampling, so the composite's box filter reads both alike and their edges agree), with a depth buffer of its own. The crystal cup draws itself into it a second time through `InstancedRefraction` (InstancedModel.fx), depth written, so only the nearest surface of each pixel survives: red and green are the screen-uv shift that surface sends the eye by - its view-space normal carried `RefractionDepth` world units in, projected at its own depth, so the bend is a distance through the glass and keeps its look as the cup dollies - and blue is how edge-on it is.
  - **The composite re-resolves the frame under the glass.** `MainPS`'s linear half is `ResolveLinear(uv)` now, one copy (its glare read became `tex2Dlod` so it may be called inside a branch; there are no mips on that target). Where the refraction layer has coverage, `ForegroundCompositePS` resolves the frame again at the shifted coordinates, once per channel with a small `REFRACTION_DISPERSION` along the shift, darkens it towards the glass's edge-on outline (`REFRACTION_EDGE_DARK`) and lays it under the layer in place of the undisplaced frame: premultiplied, the source carries it at `(1 - layer.a) × coverage` and claims alpha `layer.a + coverage − layer.a × coverage`. Pixels with no glass take a branch on a uniform and then one on their own coverage, and pay nothing more.
  - **Only the Game presents glass, and only on the result page's crystal tier** (`TrophyPodium.Refracts`); the target is built lazily like the foreground's, so nothing else allocates it.

Where a color is decoded depends on what it is. **Textures and model material colors** are sRGB data and are linearized in the shader at the point of use (`SrgbToLinear`, a cubic fit). **The light rig and the sky palette** are decoded on the CPU by `Prazsky.Core.Tools.ColorSpace`, in `SkyLightRig` (one copy for all three executables since #75), because everything applied to them there — the zenith and ground-bounce scales `1.3`/`0.75`, the tint lerps — are multiplications, and `SrgbToLinear(a*b) != SrgbToLinear(a)*SrgbToLinear(b)`. Scaling an sRGB value by 1.3 does not make that much more light: doing this in display space is what once had the ambient running some 38 % brighter than the rig asked for, and those two scales were unnamed inline literals in every executable until they became `SkyLightRig`'s named constants. `InstancedModelRenderer.SkyColor`/`GroundColor` therefore hold **linear** values and are not clamped to 1; `LinearLightRig` selects whether `SetLightTint` decodes the rig. All three executables set it **true**, and since #75 through the one `SkyLightRig.ApplyTo` rather than each for itself: the map editor renders in linear and tonemaps like the game, so its `ApplySkyLighting` decodes the sky palette to linear too and its `SkyDome` is built with `linearVertexColors: true` (the dome's gradient is drawn through `BasicEffect` into the HDR target and has to be converted from sRGB once per palette write, or the tonemapper reads display values as radiance). `SrgbToLinear` in the shader always decodes now — the `#if OPENGL` no-op is gone. Adding a color to the frame without linearizing it is the recurring bug in this area: it has happened twice (the balls' sheen, the ambient scales).

Lighting is Blinn-Phong plus a **specular ambient**: `SkyRadiance` evaluates the dome analytically (the domes are two-color vertical gradients, so a prefiltered cubemap would only reproduce this at lower resolution), sampled along the normal for diffuse and along the reflection for specular, with roughness — derived from the Blinn-Phong exponent, so no material was re-authored — lerping towards the sky average. Fresnel is Schlick over a **dielectric F0 of 0.04** modulated by the material's specular color; handing `SpecularColor` to Schlick directly makes F ≈ 1 at every angle and veils the whole scene. A `Metalness` uniform (0 for every surface except the funnel's gold rims) lerps that F0 from the dielectric `0.04 * specular` up to the specular color itself, which is what a bare metal needs — its reflectance is high and colored, so a gold rim reflects the sky in gold rather than near-white. It must be set on **every** `Draw` (it is, alongside `SpecularAmbientStrength`), or a leftover 1 would turn the next surface to metal. A third uniform joins them since #228: **`SpecularAlphaWeight`**, how far both specular terms are scaled by the material's own alpha. It is 1 for every renderer in the game — which is what the shader always did, and is free on an opaque surface, whose alpha is 1 anyway — and 0 for the result screen's crystal trophy alone. Alpha says how much of what is *behind* a surface comes through, and a reflection is light coming off the *front* of it, which is the argument `EmissiveTint` already made for a glowing pane; written into a premultiplied target, an unattenuated reflection composites as light **added** over the background rather than as a fraction of a surface. It is set unconditionally for `Metalness`'s reason: a 0 left standing would strip the next surface's reflection of the alpha it is supposed to be scaled by. A fourth uniform joins them since #156: **`DirLightStrength`**, how much of the three-light rig reaches the surface — per-renderer where the `DirLight*` colors cannot be, those being one set of values for the whole scene (the first cut of #156 scaled the tints themselves and dimmed every renderer drawn after the glass). It is 1 for everything except the ceiling glass, whose backdrop is the sky itself: `SkyLightRig.ApplyToGlass` dims it to that sky's own brightness (the Rec. 601 luminance of the rig's sky ambient over a daylight reference, saturated), which is what keeps the plate from standing as a sunlit slab over a sky-replacing scene's near-black backdrop — a dome scene's glass was always hidden by being lit from the very palette that shows through it, and this makes the dark scenes keep that pact. The scene point lights sit above the scale in the shader on purpose (a cave's own glow still reaches the pane), and it too is set unconditionally, for `Metalness`'s reason: the glass's figure left standing would put the rest of the scene under the same dark sky.

## Ball rendering (Testbed)

Balls are procedurally generated spheres (`SphereMesh` in Prazsky.Core) drawn with GPU instancing at four LOD resolutions picked by **projected size**: a `BallRenderSet` frame fills one bucket per `BallType` × LOD (no camera culling — measurements showed it saved nothing on this scene) and `InstancedModelRenderer` issues one `DrawInstancedPrimitives` call per bucket. The meshes, the LOD ladder, the buckets and every figure of the balls' look are `Prazsky.BS3D.BallRenderSet`'s since #76 and all three executables read that one copy; the walk that fills a frame is `ClusterCollector.Collect` over a simulated cluster and `BallDrawFrame.AddMap` over a static map (see "The rest of the hardware, in one copy" in `docs/testbed.md`). Per-instance data (`ModelInstance`, TEXCOORD1–5) carries the world matrix plus the ambient-occlusion vector (direction towards occupied neighbor cells + base factor, recomputed every frame from the grid — and since #303 the base factor also carries **burial**: `AirDepthField` runs a multi-source BFS from every air cell once per frame, so the factor drops from 0.45 with a full first shell to 0.10 three shells in, where the twelve-cell count used to saturate at the first shell and a ball in the dead centre of the cluster read exactly like one just under its surface; measured at 117 µs a frame on a blob denser than the stress level, invisible in the pinned FPS run). The shader (`Testbed/Content/Shaders/InstancedModel.fx`) does per-pixel Blinn-Phong with a positional key light ("sun"), hemisphere ambient tinted by the sky dome palette (`SkyDome` extracts zenith/horizon colors from the dome's vertex colors; `ApplySkyLighting` distributes them, `DefaultLighting` holds the base rig), directional AO, and a per-type diffuse tint (`GetDiffuseTintByType`). There are **thirteen ball types** — red/green/blue/white plus **cyan/magenta/yellow/black**, joined by **orange/brown/silver/navy/olive** with #152 (`BallType.Type1..Type13`, their tints and ambient in `BasicEffectParamsProvider`); `BallRenderSet.TYPE_COUNT` sizes the instancing buckets for all three executables — derived from the enum itself since #152 (`BallTypes.Count`; it was a hand-pinned `const (int)BallType.Type8`, and a member added without repointing it existed in logic and physics but was silently never drawn) — `RandomBallType` spans all thirteen, and the map editor picks the first nine on NumPad1–9 and cycles all thirteen with NumPad +/−. The shader needs no change for a new type — the pattern is purely tint-driven, one per-draw uniform per bucket. Three types carry a deliberate quirk of the white-gore pattern: **white** is drawn beige, **yellow** a rich gold and **silver** a cool slate so their pattern reads against the white gores (a pure-white, pale-yellow or light-silver primary would be patternless), and **black** is near-black with near-zero ambient — its form comes from the glossy highlight and its white gores, and since emission radiates the primary it is the one ball that stays dark while the rest pulse (navy, brown and olive are dark enough to pulse dimly, but each still radiates its own hue where black radiates nothing). The other three new tints hold their neighbours at arm's length by construction: orange's green channel (0.5) splits red's (0.2) and gold's (0.82), navy is a deeper and more violet blue than Type3, and olive far darker and yellower than green.

**Navy read "far darker than blue" until #246, and that is what was wrong with it.** Being far from one neighbour had put it on top of the other: its whole difference from the near-black Type8 sat in the **blue channel**, the one the eye weights least (0.072 of luminance against green's 0.715), and measured off a `Thirteen_Colors` capture under the darkest dome the two balls came out at luminance **13 and 31 of 255** — both at the bottom of the display's range, where a lit room takes the difference away before the eye gets it. **Lifting navy alone does not fix it**, and that is the finding worth keeping: it walks navy straight into Type3. In CIEDE2000 off that capture, navy at (0.08, 0.22, 0.62) took black/navy from **25.3 to 30.8** and pushed navy/blue from **24.4 down to 16.7** — the palette's tightest pair, one confusion traded for another. So **Type3 came up with it** (0.35, 0.45, 1.0 → 0.45, 0.60, 1.0) and the whole blue family was re-spaced, black → navy → blue; both pairs then improved (**30.8** and **33.4**) with no other pair in the palette meaningfully tighter and the palette's own minimum unchanged (15.6 → 16.1, `white/yellow`). Navy stays a **blue** rather than moving into the palette's one free hue (violet, 240–300° is the only gap): the level designs use it as one — Globe's ocean is 71 navy against 75 cyan and 74 blue (#301's redraw; it was 92/108/57) — and the violet variant measured worse on both pairs anyway. Two method notes, both paid for: **ΔE76 is the wrong instrument here** (it scored a pure lightness gap the same as a hue gap, ranking black/navy *ninth* while `white/yellow`, `orange/red` and `silver/blue` all measured tighter and nobody has ever complained about them), and a capture of this row carries **±0.4 dE of pulse noise**, because the balls are emissive on a heartbeat and the phase differs between runs — so shoot twice before believing a small delta. Both ambients moved with their tints, because the ball's *unlit* half is where the old confusion would otherwise have survived. **Olive was the same story told in the other direction, and #294 measured it that way.** Its (0.42, 0.45, 0.08) was "far darker than green" on purpose, and being far from one neighbour had again put it on top of others: off a `Thirteen_Colors` capture under dome 1 its three nearest were **black (24.8), brown (26.0) and silver (26.0)** — a dark-and-neutral band with no green in it, which is exactly what the owner reported it reading as. The fix is a lift **and** a chroma recovery — (0.42, 0.52, 0.02), the blue dropped almost to nothing because 0.08 of it was desaturating a colour whose identity is that it has none — giving black **29.6**, brown **32.6** and silver **27.6** under dome 1, and 35.2 / 36.3 / 29.0 under dome 13. What closes in exchange are pairs separated by lightness rather than hue (beige/olive 24.0 → 22.4, green/olive 32.9 → 26.0, both under the dark dome), and both stay clear of the palette's own tightest pairs. **The stopping point was measured, not judged:** a further lift to (0.44, 0.56, 0.025) opens the dark band wider still but drops green/olive to **22.5** — two greens to tell apart, #246's trade arriving from the other side. And a method note that cost a round: **a colour like this has to be measured under a bright dome and a dark one**, because olive rides the dome's own light harder than its neighbours do — dome 1 and dome 13 disagree about which of its pairs is the tightest, and tuning against either alone walks it into the other's confusion.
 Balls draw with the `InstancedModelPattern` technique: a procedural beach-ball pattern (gores alternating between the type tint and white, white discs at the poles) evaluated in object space so it turns with the ball and makes rotation readable; `PatternGoreCount` on the renderer enables it, the tint passed to `Draw` becomes the primary gore color, and `PatternGoreWidth` sets how much of each pair of segments the color takes. Balls are **emissive and alive**: they radiate the ball's own type tint — `primary`, not the blended pattern color, whose gores and polar discs are white and made half of every ball radiate white light (`EmissiveStrength`, off `BallRenderSet.EMISSION`) on a `Heartbeat` curve — two gaussian bumps per cycle, lub-dub, not a sine — whose phase is offset by world position along `PulseDirection` so the beat travels up the cluster as a wave instead of every ball flashing together. `PulseTime` is driven from wall-clock time — it is `BallRenderSet.Draw`'s own argument rather than a property a caller could forget to set — so the pulse survives a paused or slowed simulation. **The resting emission follows the occlusion since #303, squared — the bubble's own measured correction arriving on the opaque styles** (`BallEmission` in the shader): this document said "deliberately not occluded: a light buried in the pile is the one that should still glow out" for a long time, and that argument is about one ball seen alone — added flat to every ball of a pile it was a floor under the whole cluster that no amount of AO could take it below, and the single biggest reason burial did not read (#303's issue walks the arithmetic). What still punches through is the announcements: the heartbeat's swing rides unoccluded so the wave stays legible inside the pile, and the ripple and the ceiling's alarm are added after shading and never dimmed. The two emission-identity styles occlude linearly instead (plasma always did; the lava joined it), each saying why at its own glow. `TranslucencyStrength` adds light carried through the shell from a source behind. Note the ad-hoc Fresnel "vinyl sheen" that used to live here is gone — it was a sky reflection, which the specular ambient now does for every surface, and two Fresnel sky terms stacked on a sphere bleached the balls out.

**The LOD ladder is calibrated in display pixels, not in world units, and the reason is that there are three cameras.** `BallRenderSet.LOD_RESOLUTIONS` is `{64,32}`, `{40,20}`, `{24,12}`, `{14,7}` — slices then stacks, stacks always half the slices, which is the balanced ratio because a sphere's outline is a full circle whose left and right extremes fall on slice boundaries and whose top and bottom fall on stack ones, so at 2:1 the slice count alone describes the whole silhouette (the retired ladder ran 4:3 and spent a third more triangles on stacks already finer than their slices). What picks a level is the ball's **projected radius**: `BallRenderSet.SILHOUETTE_BUDGET_PIXELS` (0.35) is how far an outline may sag off a true circle, a polygon of `slices` segments sags by `projectedRadius · (1 − cos(π / slices))`, and inverting that gives the thresholds — {113.5, 40.9, 14.0} px — which are therefore *derived* from the mesh table and cannot drift from it. `SolveLodDistances` turns them into squared camera distances once a frame, so the per-ball pick is still one compare against `DistanceSquared`; the scale comes out of `Projection.M22` (`= 1 / tan(fov/2)` for any `CreatePerspectiveFieldOfView`), which means precise aim's narrower lens and the recoil's FOV punch sharpen the meshes for free. **It replaced a ladder keyed on raw camera distance whose coarsest level covered everything past 30 units — and the Game's play camera stands a solved 33.2 out, so every ball of every played level was drawn at 10 slices.** Measured on a 1616×939 window that is a projected radius of ~18 px and a silhouette sag of 0.88 px, which is plainly faceted: the dark balls on the cluster's edge showed countable straight runs and corners against the sky. **Costs nothing measurable** — same scene, dome, supersampling and level, vsync off: old ladder mean 34.1 / median 36.0 FPS, new ladder mean 33.9 / median 35.5, a 0.6 % gap inside a run-to-run spread that bottomed at 25.8 in both. This frame is not triangle-bound at that stand-off. **The height is the back buffer's and not the bound target's**, deliberately twice over: a facet is a systematic deviation of the outline that the resolve's box filter softens but does not remove (unlike the stair-stepping supersampling exists for), so the budget belongs in display pixels; and the three executables bind the scene target at *different* points in the frame — the Game collects its balls before binding it, the Testbed and the map editor after — so a `Viewport` read would mean the display in one and the supersampled target in the other two.

**The ball dissolve dithers in display pixels.** `ModelInstance.Dissolve` (TEXCOORD6) clips pixels against a hash, and that hash is now keyed on `floor(SV_POSITION.xy / DissolvePixelSize)` — a block of the **screen** — where it used to be keyed on 7³ cells of the ball's own object space. Object-space cells are cubes in the world: they turn with the ball, take its perspective, and read as a lumpy three-dimensional mottling of the surface rather than as pixelation of the picture, which is the whole point of cutting rather than fading. The measured trap that originally argued for object space is real but was an argument against the *wrong* screen-space form — a cell one **target** pixel across is averaged straight back into a smooth cross-fade by the box filter that resolves a supersampled frame. A cell a whole display pixel or more across is not, because every target sample inside it takes the same decision; `BallRenderSet.DISSOLVE_DISPLAY_PIXELS` (**1**) is multiplied by `BallRenderSet.SupersampleFactor` before it reaches the shader. It was **3**, on the prediction that a one-pixel cut would read as a haze or a film grain — as the ball being *faint*, which is what the dither was chosen over a transparency to avoid — and on the note that a block several target pixels wide keeps a 2×2 shading quad inside one cell, so the `clip` leaves whole quads rather than fragmenting nearly all of them. Played rather than reasoned about, the first argument came out the other way round: at three the blocks read as a **mosaic laid over** the ball rather than as the ball resolving, and one display pixel is what was asked for. The quad point still stands and is what the cost of this is now paid in; it was never the reason for the three. The factor is asked of the caller (mirroring `SceneRenderer.SupersampleFactor`, which `Space.fx` needs for the same "sized in output pixels" reason) rather than measured off the device, for the render-target-ordering reason above; the Game sets it in `SetSupersampleFactor`, the other two at construction. `Tonemap.fx`'s film grain quantises to `OutputSize` for exactly the same reason and is the in-repo precedent.

The same technique gives the ball its inflatable-vinyl surface: a sine-sum height field (molded micro-relief plus a groove along every panel weld) tilts the normal through `PerturbNormalFromHeight`, breaking up the highlight, and a Fresnel term adds the grazing-angle sky sheen (`PatternReliefStrength`, `PatternSheenStrength`). Each relief octave band-limits itself against its own wavelength using the screen-space pixel footprint (not camera distance), because the perturbation is derivative-driven and turns into a checkerboard once a wave gets near pixel size. The scene therefore renders into a supersampled `RenderTarget2D` (`_supersampleFactor`, default 2, `ssaa=<n>` to override) that is box-filtered onto the back buffer: more samples shrink the footprint, which is what keeps the fine octaves alive and sharp. MSAA antialiases geometry edges but not shading, so it is switched off while supersampling is on. The text overlay and the crosshair draw after the resolve, at native resolution. The old ball shadows are gone with the drawn ground: the `InstancedDepth` shadow-map pass and the `ShadowOverlay.fx` ground overlay were dropped when the city became the scene (`DrawDepth` and the `InstancedDepth` technique still sit in `InstancedModelRenderer`, unused; the orphaned `ShadowOverlay.fx` file — no `.mgcb` referenced it — has been deleted). The scene objects — the ceiling and cannon as single-instance draws, the city and arena floor as instanced arrays — render through the same shader (`InstancedModelRenderer.Draw(...)` with per-part material colors/specular read from the model), so the whole scene shares one lighting model; `SkyLitRenderers()` enumerates every sky-lit renderer for `ApplySkyLighting`. The ceiling is a procedurally generated translucent glass box (`BoxMesh` in Prazsky.Core), regenerated at the exact field size whenever a map is loaded (`CeilingPlate.Fit`, shared with the game since #75), drawn after the opaque scene, and lit through the rig's glass push rather than that enumeration (`SkyLightRig.ApplyToGlass`, #156): the plate stands against the sky itself, so its three-light rig is scaled to the sky's own brightness. Models whose materials carry a texture use the `InstancedModelTextured` technique. Models without one can set `DetailTexture` on their renderer — a texture that only modulates the material colors — mapped either world-space (`DetailMapping.Triplanar` + optional procedural masonry joints, used by the arena's marble floor, whose boxes carry no UVs worth the name) or through the model's own UVs (`DetailMapping.ModelUVs`: moving objects must not use the world-space projection or the texture would swim across them — the cannon used this before it became procedural, and nothing uses it now). The UV path can add a `DetailNormalMap` for actual relief; its tangent frame comes from screen-space derivatives (`CotangentFrame` in the shader), so no model needs tangent vertex data. On top of any of these, `SurfaceReliefStrength`/`SurfaceReliefFrequency` add a procedural micro-relief (seven summed sines in world space) that tilts the normal through the same `PerturbNormalFromHeight` — no texture, no tiling, and it holds its grain to whatever the pixels can still resolve. The scene path band-limits each octave through `ReliefOctaveDirectional`, which measures the footprint **along that octave's own wave direction** (`abs(dot(dpdx, waveDirection)) + abs(dot(dpdy, waveDirection))`) rather than as one scalar: a pixel only fails to resolve a wave when it is wide across its crests, and on a surface seen at a grazing angle — a pixel covering meters along the view but millimeters across it — a scalar footprint reports the long axis and fades out every octave at once, leaving the surface geometrically perfect exactly where it should look roughest. The ball path still uses the scalar `ReliefOctave`, spheres being small enough that it does not show. `TextureSampler` is anisotropic for the same reason: isotropic mip selection blurs a textured surface into a smear at grazing angles. The arena's marble floor uses it (the cannon did too, before it became procedural). A model is rarely one material throughout, so `SetMeshSurfaceStyle(meshName, SurfaceStyle)` declares per mesh what it is made of — `Masonry` (coursed blocks with mortar cut in as real recesses), `Wood` (upright boards, seams between them, grain stretched along their length) or `Plain`; meshes left undeclared default to `Masonry`. Nothing calls it now that the multi-material castle model is gone, so it — and `MasonryStrength` — sit unused as library features. Testing CLI args: `autoshoot` (logs FPS + LOD distribution once per second), `aimcheck` (logs per-map aim reachability, PASS/FAIL), `aimshoot` (auto game-mode aim-and-shoot scan; see "Cannon controls" in `docs/testbed.md`), `nocap` (no vsync), `sky=<n>`, `scene=<name>`, `ssaa=<n>`, `exposure=<f>`, `switchmap=<path>`, and `campos=x,y,z`/`camtarget=x,y,z` (place and aim the free camera at startup, so a screenshot can be framed from any vantage — e.g. `campos=0,-18,24` under the sea), `width=<n>`/`height=<n>` (set the windowed back buffer so a screenshot can be captured at the desktop's play resolution; see "Resolution and aspect" below), `nopost` (zero the film grain and the chromatic aberration) and `nooverc` (pin the overcast lerp to 0, which is the ambient the Game always draws — see "Judging colour through the Testbed" above) — see `.claude/skills/verify/SKILL.md` and `.claude/skills/screenshot/SKILL.md`. `Maps/Dense20x10x15.json` (3000 balls) is the perf stress map; the frame budget ceiling there is the CPU physics (~300 FPS uncapped), not rendering.

**A map chooses what its balls are made of (#258).** `BallStyle` is `Beach` — the moulded vinyl described above — or `Bubble`, a hollow glass bubble, and a level file names it (`"balls": "bubble"`, absent meaning vinyl; see "Levels and serialization" in `docs/formats-and-tools.md`). It decides the shading and nothing else: the lattice, the physics, the match rule and the score are all about a ball's `BallType`, so a bubble level plays move for move like a vinyl one. `BallRenderSet.Style` is the one switch, every caller **states** it rather than inheriting it (the Game has one render set and both the front end's preview and the played level hang clusters through it), and the Game's `balls=<beach|bubble>` argument overrides every level at once so the two looks can be photographed on the same cluster at the same stand-off under the same dome. The map editor cycles it on **L** and `F4` writes it into the level. **The Testbed cycles it on L too and takes `balls=<name>` (#318), and a level opened there now comes up in the material it names** — until then it set `BallRenderSet.Style` nowhere at all, so it drew vinyl whatever a level said, and nine of the ten materials could not be put in front of a fixed camera. That matters more than the convenience: `campos`/`camtarget` are the Testbed's, so the framing rig every shading judgement in this project is made through could not see the styles it was judging. **Every chapter of the shipped campaign hangs a different material, and since #295 all ten styles have a home** — and since #325 there are **eleven chapters against those ten**, so exactly one material now hangs two. In play order: the Meadow **bubble**, the Gallery **wool**, the Coil **marble**, the Tower **ice**, the Reveal **the vinyl beach ball**, the Quarry **metal**, the Nebula **plasma**, the Eruption **lava**, the Spectrum **porcelain**, the Arcade **gem**, the Mirage **porcelain** again. The reprise is chosen against the two ball *kinds* rather than against the scene, which is what makes it not merely the leftover: neither the rock nor the clear glass takes the level's material, so a Mirage level is its style standing beside a ball with no colour in it and a ball that was never worked, and the glaze is the deepest colour and the most worked surface in the set — the widest read from both at once. The bubble is the trap in one word (transparency beside a transparent kind says nothing), and the marble is its mirror: the granite's own header records that what separates it from a grey marble at play distance is its **roughness**, which is the distinction a marble level would spend. (The Reveal hung the lava until #295 moved it to the volcano its own entry always said had the better claim; the vinyl — designed against dark backdrops, its emissive heartbeat loudest there — takes the cavern, which also retires #313's "weakest pairing" note about lava reading like plasma two chapters on.) It is a property of the block, stated once per chapter in `LevelGen`'s `BALLS_*` constants, which carry the reasoning for each placement; two of them are scene-bound (the metal needs something to mirror, the plasma needs a dark dome) and both are placed accordingly. The vinyl is also still what everything unauthored draws — the map editor, the Testbed, and any level that says nothing.
**The style picks a technique, and it is an enum on both sides of the library boundary (#304).** `BallStyle` is what a level *file* names; `Prazsky.Core.Render.BallShading` is which of `InstancedModel.fx`'s ball programs was compiled, and `InstancedModelRenderer` indexes a technique **table** with it (`BallTechniqueNames`, checked against the enum at load, so a shading added without a technique beside it throws where the message can say what to do rather than drawing whatever the previous draw left bound — #152's lesson, which cost a ball colour that existed in logic and physics and was silently never drawn). Two enums and a `BallRenderSet.ShadingOf` between them, because they answer to different owners: the style cannot change without a format decision, and the library that owns the shading cannot see the level format at all. This was a `bool GlassBubble` until #304 — two materials fit in a flag, and the styles proposed in #272 do not. `BallRenderSet.ApplyStyle` is a `switch` with one case per style for the same reason: it pushed the film's three figures at a vinyl renderer that has no use for them, which is harmless at two styles and the wrong shape at eight. And `Draw` asks **`BallStyles.IsTransparent(_style)`** where it used to ask `_style == BallStyle.Bubble` — the same answer only while the bubble was the one transparent style, and a second one added under the old test would have been drawn as a single opaque wall with nothing anywhere reporting it.

**One ball can refuse the map's style: the rock (#324).** `BallShading.Stone` is the eleventh member and the one with **no type colour at all** — every other shading takes the ball's tint and does something with it, because every other one draws a colour the player matches, and this draws the ball that cannot be (`BallKind.Rock`; see "Special ball kinds" in `docs/game-session.md`). It is not in `BallStyle` and a level cannot name it: a rock is stone on a bubble level and on a lava one alike, because "that one is different" has to survive all ten materials or it is not a signal. So the rocks are a **third bucket region** in `BallRenderSet`, drawn by `DrawRocks` before the style's own passes, on exactly the argument the still plane rests on (#252): the technique, the emission and the pulse depth are per-*renderer* uniforms, so "this one ball is stone and does not breathe" cannot travel on the instance and has to be its own draw. It is a region rather than a plane because a rock has no colour to bucket by — `LodCount` buckets, not `TYPE_COUNT × LodCount`, so four draw calls rather than fifty-two — and it goes **first** because opaque goes before transparent, or a rock behind a bubble would be blended on top of it. The renderers are put back with `ApplyStyle` rather than by restoring fields, and a field with no rocks in it never touches a renderer for it at all — which was every level shipped until #325 put the Mirage's five rock levels in the campaign (Anvil 82 rocks, Seam 155, Keystone 132, Cairn 209, Obsidian 59 — counted off the level files, and three of the five had drifted from the figures that stood here until #356: the generator has rewritten them since).

**The rock's silhouette is not a circle, and #340 is the one place in this game where that rule is broken.** The style shipped shading a perfect sphere and its own header said so — "nothing here builds geometry" — resting the read on relief alone. The owner's playtest asked for the opposite in as many words (a rock may be visibly less round, sharper, more like an actual chunk of stone) and confirmed it when asked, so `InstancedModelStone` is now the **only ball technique with a vertex shader of its own**. #271 decided the same question the other way for the **gem**, and both rulings are right for their own style: a gem is *cut*, so a polygonal outline on it is a defect in the cut; a rock is *broken*, and an outline that is not round is the single clearest thing that can be said about one — and the only thing about it that no band limit can take away at distance. Three decisions sit in that vertex shader. It **carves inward only**, because the lattice packs balls at exactly two radii apart and one that grew would push into its neighbour's cell (carving is also what makes a rock: stone is what is *left*). Its **normal is analytic** — for a radial surface `r(d)·d` the normal is `d − ∇ₜr / r`, closed form because the field is a sum of sines, and reusing the sphere's direction instead would light a carved ball exactly like a round one, making the whole change a silhouette and nothing else. And the field is **coarser than everything else on the ball** (1.7–5.7 waves against the lumps' 3.5–49), so the two tiers do not double-count: one is the *shape*, the other the *surface*. A power over the carve turns undulation into gouges — the first build was a smooth potato, obviously not spherical and not what was asked for. One limit is worth knowing: **every rock carries the same carving**, because the instance stream has no free channel for a per-ball seed and the only per-instance value available (the ball's position) moves, which would make the shape swim as a rock fell. What saves it is that the field is in object space, so one stone shown at a hundred orientations is a hundred rocks — but **that orientation has to be supplied, and until #356 nothing supplied it.** The header rested it on the physics and the physics does not: `BallsConstraintsBuilder` creates every body at identity and a hanging lattice never turns one, so on Cairn (209 rocks, the densest of the five stone levels) the mean tilt from identity over sixty seconds measured **0.05°** and the largest **0.28°** — one solid at one angle, down to the same pale swirl in the same place on all 209 of them. `RockTurns` now folds a fixed per-cell turn into the world matrix the stream already carries, which makes the object-space argument true rather than hoped for, costs no channel and no shader change, and cannot reach the simulation (a ball's collider is a sphere and its constraints anchor off its body pose, and the draw matrix is neither). The map editor and the menu's backdrop take the same turn through `BallDrawFrame.AddMap`, so a bench heap and a played heap are the same heap.

Two things followed from it. The grain went back up from 14 to **22** — the argument that had pushed it down was that a fine figure band-limits into invisibility at play distance, and that argument no longer has to be carried by the grain, because the silhouette carries it. And the body went from 0.63 to **0.54**: it could not simply be darkened (the note on `StoneBody` explains why a physically reasonable 0.42 read as the 8-ball), but a carved ball has real form, and value that had to come out of the albedo now comes out of the shape. **Measured** on the meadow at dome 1: two rocks at 84 and 75 mean luminance against Type8 (black) 42 and Type11 (silver) 81; at dome 13, 66 and 67 against 29 and 80. Still beside the silver and still at twice the 8-ball on both, which is exactly what that note asks for. **Cost**: 7.47/7.50 → 7.50/7.52 ms at ssaa 4 on `Rocks.json` (thirteen rocks in a cluster of 196), inside the run-to-run spread.

The look is granite: a warm grey body, a fleck field of pale quartz and dark mica, coarse lumps tilting the normal, matte. Three things about it were measured the expensive way and are stated at the constants:

- **A product of waves cannot be band-limited per octave.** The fleck field is `sin·sin·sin` (a sum gives creases, which is the marble's veining and is what this must not look like), and attenuating each factor multiplies the three attenuations — a field that should fade to 45 % fades to 9 %, so the grain was simply gone one ball-width from the camera. One factor, measured against the **sum** of the three frequencies, because that is where a product's finest content lies. The count came down from 30 to 14 with it: a grain fine enough to be physically right is a grain no ball at play distance can show, and what identifies a rock across the arena is its value, the absence of gores, the absence of a heartbeat and the *lumps*.
- **Three pure waves multiplied are a golf ball.** They give a regular lattice of identical dimples in even rows. The phase each wave is read at is displaced by the lump field — the marble's own construction for its veins, warping the input rather than the output — and it costs nothing because the field is already in hand.

**And a second one refuses it, at the opposite end of the palette: the clear glass (#325).** `BallShading.Hollow` is the twelfth member and the other one with no type colour, drawn for `BallKind.Transparent` — the ball that has no colour *yet*, where the rock is the ball that can never have a usable one. It is a **fourth bucket region** (`DrawHollow`), on the rock's argument exactly; what differs is the order and the states, and both follow from it being transparent rather than opaque. It goes out **last** of the balls, because everything it should show through it has to be in the target already, and it goes out under the bubble's own two-pass shell — far wall depth-read, near wall depth-write — which it repeats rather than inherits, since on an opaque level there is no `DrawShell` to have set them. **The collision this had to solve is that the game already had a transparent ball material**: on a `Bubble` level every ball is a see-through film, so "transparent" was taken and this kind would have had nothing left to say. What it says instead is **emptiness** — no dye anywhere in it, no gores, no film interference, `HOLLOW_BODY_OPACITY` at 0.06 against the film's — so beside a dyed film it reads as the one shell nobody has coloured in. It is therefore *not* the bubble with a white tint: a white film still carries the film, and the whole of this technique is those terms being absent, leaving a rim, a highlight, a mould seam and the sky bent through the middle. ⚠ Its **rotation cue** is the one part that had to be designed rather than inherited (contract point 6): everything else about clear glass is a function of the eye and the sky, so an undyed shell drawn without one is the mirror trap the ball-technique header names — a spinning ball drawn as a still one. The answer is the **cast seam** a glass marble actually has (`HollowSeamFrequency`, two of them, a mould having two halves), object-space so it turns with the ball, and put into the *normal* rather than into a colour, because a material with no colour has nowhere to draw a coloured figure (#311's rule arriving where it bites hardest).


**And a third, which reads by MOVING rather than by anything drawn on it: the live bomb (#326).** `BallShading.Bomb` is the thirteenth member and the third with no type colour, drawn for `BallKind.Bomb`. It is a **fifth bucket region** (`DrawBombs`), opaque, so it goes out beside the rocks; a bomb is never loaded in the cannon, so like the other two it needs no still twin, and it is colourless, so it is `LodCount` buckets and not `TYPE_COUNT × LodCount`.

- **What makes it read as ARMED is the beat — but only once the figure it lights is big enough to see, and that took the owner's eye to find.** The design argument was the stone's own finding turned round: the rock's entry above records that a rock is legible across a whole field by being the one ball that does *not* breathe, motion being the first thing the eye reads, so the bomb takes the other end of that channel (`BOMB_PULSE_DEPTH` 1.0, `BOMB_EMISSION` 1.25, against a cluster resting at 0.55). It shipped unreadable at play distance and the report was exact: *the red lines are too narrow, so from a distance the bomb doesn't look like a bomb — the red blinking isn't visible.* **Three faults at once.** The grooves were too narrow and too sharp (0.22 at sharpness 2.4 — an argument about a bomb held at arm's length, applied to a ball two dozen pixels across). The band limit faded the pattern out **six times too early** — a factor of 2.0 on the band count zeroed it at a footprint of 0.1, while a band was still several pixels wide, so the casing dissolved into a flat glow exactly where the figure had work to do; Nyquist for a band spanning `π / BombBandCount` puts the fade at 0.5. And the beat was **too fast to see**: `Heartbeat`'s lit window is a fixed *fraction* of its cycle, so speeding it up blinks *shorter*, not more — at 2.6 the flash was some 30 ms, near the floor of what a player registers, and `BOMB_PULSE_SPEED` is 0.5 now (a slow, unmistakable throb, still nothing like the cluster's rhythm). **Measured after, at the stand-off a level is played from, eight captures across one cycle: the casing runs 106 to 151 codes of red, a 1.43× swing that several of the eight caught, where the same sampling used to catch the flash once in six.** The ten-material sweep passed throughout: the bomb is the odd one out against every style, the lava's glowing crust included — that one glows in a web of cracks, this in straight latitude bands. Emission, depth and speed being per-renderer uniforms is *why* this is a draw of its own rather than a flag on the instance (the still plane's argument, #252, for the third time).
- **And it was still the wrong colour, which is #341.** That same measurement wrote the casing down as going *"from dim brown to bright orange and back"*, and the owner's verdict was that a bomb blinks **red**. The fault was one constant (`BombCharge`) and the fix is a hue and nothing else: **red was already pinned at 1.0, so what made it amber was the green**, and 0.46 of it is nearly a fifth of the red in linear light. At 0.15 the hue falls from 17–20° to **3.8°, flat across the beat**. Measured both ways in one session, twelve phases of one beat through one fixed camera: the casing floor is 96 codes of red **both times** and the beat's peak is 142 **both times**, a 1.48× swing either way — *only green moves*. Dropping it costs about a third of the charge's luminance (green carries 0.7152 of it against red's 0.2126) and none of that loss is visible, because a dark casing with light in its joins is read by its red. The compensation was tried anyway on the strength of the luminance figure — `BombRestingGlow` 0.5 → 0.65 — and put back: it lifts the floor and the flash by the same linear amount, so it buys brightness by spending the swing (1.48× → 1.36×), and the swing is what #326 was protecting.
**And a fourth, whose whole problem was being told apart from two things that already existed: the live zap (#327).** `BallShading.Zap` is the fourteenth member and the fourth with no type colour, drawn for `BallKind.Zap` as a **sixth bucket region** (`DrawZaps`) on the bomb's argument in every respect — opaque, colourless, never loaded in the cannon, and read by a motion that is a set of per-renderer uniforms.
- **Its collisions are structural, not tonal, which is why none of the three is solved by brightness.** The **plasma style** (#309) is already this game's crawling-filament look and on a plasma level *every* ball is one: what separates them is that a plasma ball glows all over, in its own type colour, with soft filaments drifting through a lit body that brightens towards the middle of its disc, while a zap is a **dark** shell in one fixed cold blue-white whose figure is a handful of hard thin arcs on **fixed great circles** that flicker rather than wander. The **bomb** is the other dark special and a player has to know which one they are landing beside: its figure is latitude *bands* and it throbs slow and deep; this is *oblique great circles* crossing each other, and it flickers fast and shallow — opposite corners of the same two dials, plus a warm dark against a cold one (`BasicEffectParamsProvider.Bomb` against `.Zap`). The **vinyl's gores** are meridians, so a pole-to-pole figure was out before it was drawn. Photographed against all three: on a plasma level, on a lava level, and beside the bomb in one frame.
- **⚠ Fast is right here and was wrong for the bomb**, which looks like a contradiction and is not. `Heartbeat`'s lit window is a fixed *fraction* of its cycle, so a faster beat flashes *shorter* — which ruined the bomb, whose read **is** the flash. A zap's read is the arc figure, kept lit by a floor of its own (`ZapRestingGlow`, added outside `BallEmission` for #326's measured reason — the resting term is multiplied by occlusion squared, and a special buried in the pile is the one that most has to be seen); the beat only adds jitter on top of something already visible, and jitter is supposed to be brief.
- **The arcs were too wide first, and the fix was the bomb's lesson read in reverse.** At `ZapArcWidth` 0.17 the close-up was right and the play-distance read was a cow pattern — white blotches on black, because lines that wide merge as the ball shrinks. At 0.115 the close-up is a cage of thin bright arcs crossing on a dark shell and the distance read falls back to the floor glow, which is exactly the architecture the bomb ended up with: the figure carries close up, and colour plus flicker carry far away. The arcs are combined with `max` and not a sum — a sum doubles the light where two cross and turns the crossing into a blob, when what should read there is the *shape* of two lines meeting.
- **⚠ A bomb's beat cannot be phase-swept with `F5`.** Freezing the simulation is the standing trick for stopping the cluster swaying between captures and #326's rig used it — but it pins the pulse clock too, so a settle sweep then photographs one phase over and over and the beat looks like it has collapsed. Measured during #341 on one build: **twenty captures across a cycle with `F5` spanned 109–115 codes; twelve without it, same camera, spanned 96–142.** The cluster hangs still enough by nine seconds that the sway costs nothing on a ball this size. This applies to every pulsing ball, not just the bomb.
  - **⚠ Three earlier rounds of "measurements" of this were taken against a Testbed that was not running the shader being edited, and every conclusion from them was withdrawn — including one written into five files.** The captures come from `bin\net10.0-windows` (the **Debug** output) while the builds were being made with `-c Release`, and MGCB additionally **skips** an `.fx` whose `.xnb` is newer and then copies nothing into the output at all. Before believing any capture of a shader change, check that `bin\<tfm>\Content\Shaders\InstancedModel.xnb` is **newer than the `.fx`**. Tinting a constant green is the two-minute way to prove the pixels on screen came from the file on disk, and it is what finally caught this.
- **The emission is multiplied by the SEAM MASK, not by the ball.** `BombPS` cuts the casing into six latitude bands — spaced evenly in *angle*, through `acos`, or they crowd at the poles and read as a wound ball rather than a cast shell — and the charge burns in the grooves between them, with a ring of rivets round the waist as the one part of the figure that survives when the bands blur together. A ball flashing this deep and this fast over its whole area would strobe; a dark shell with light coming out of its joins says *there is something in there* and pulses without shouting. The grooves cut **in** and the studs stand **proud**, in one height field, which is the sign difference that keeps them two features rather than one dented surface.
- **The casing is dark and deliberately warm.** `BombCasing` is (0.115, 0.098, 0.092) — dark so the charge has something to be bright against, and warm because a neutral near-black ball is Type8, the one colour it could be confused with. That is the rock's own trap from the grey end. Not black either: a body at zero has nothing for the sky to sit on and the silhouette disappears against a dark dome, which is also what `BasicEffectParamsProvider.Bomb` is for — the stone's entry with the ambient turned *down* rather than up, because a bomb's unlit side is the one thing that must stay dark.
- **⚠ `DrawBombs` puts the pulse SPEED back by hand, and that is the one uniform `ApplyStyle` cannot restore.** The depth comes back for free — `DrawPlane` states it on every ordinary draw, which is this file's own discipline — but the speed is set once when the renderers are built and nothing states it per frame, so a bomb left in it would put the whole cluster on the bomb's heartbeat for the rest of the frame. It is restored in `DrawBombs` rather than by teaching `DrawPlane` to state it, because that method returns early when nothing is loaded and so cannot be relied on to restore anything.

**And a fifth, the first ball figure in this game drawn in WORLD space: the live acid (#328).** `BallShading.Acid` is the fifteenth member and the fifth with no type colour, drawn for `BallKind.Acid` as an **eighth bucket region** (`DrawAcids`) on the bomb's and the zap's argument in every respect — opaque, colourless, never loaded in the cannon, read by a figure that is a technique plus a set of per-renderer uniforms.

- **The world-space part is the one thing here that is not taste.** Every other ball figure is built in object space and turns with the body; an acid's figure is a statement about **gravity** — it is the only special with an axis, and the look has to say "downward" before the ball is fired at. A figure that rotated with the body would say nothing. On a sphere the world normal *is* the outward radial direction, so `AcidPS` reads `-n.y` for "how far down this point faces" with no rotation matrix and no dependence on how the body is lying — right in the lattice and right again on the way down the drain.
- **Its collision to solve is the leafy green ball, not the other two specials.** The three dark kinds separate by hue before either lights up — a warm dark (bomb), a cold one (zap), an acrid one (acid) — but `Type2` is a *lit* green ball, so the shell is kept dark enough that the two are never the same thing: what reads is a dark shell with green liquid crawling on it, against a bright green ball with white gores.
- **The figure is drips plus a pool, and the pool is the half that does not band-limit.** Nine lanes run down from a per-lane height (`AcidHash` off the lane index, so they are ragged rather than combed) into a cap at the underside. The lanes fade out as the footprint grows past a lane's own width — the ice crack's and the zap's arcs' rule — converging to `AcidFarGlow` rather than to nothing, while the pool is a cap and stays resolvable at a dozen pixels. That is the bomb's lesson applied a third time: what a special is read by at play distance is the **size** of the lit figure, and at distance an acid is a ball with a glowing green underside, which is still the direction cue.
- **The relief is POSITIVE where the zap's is negative**, and the sign is the whole of why one reads as liquid and the other as a discharge: a drip is a bead lying *on* the shell, an arc is light cut *into* it. The surface is also the wettest of the three (`AcidSmoothness` 0.86 against the zap's 0.74), which is what a running liquid needs.
- **Slow, deliberately, and the three uniforms say so.** The lanes already creep inside the technique on `PulseTime`, so the heartbeat adds a swell rather than a flicker: `ACID_PULSE_SPEED` 0.8 against the zap's 3.1, and a shallower depth. A zap is charged; an acid oozes.

**And a sixth, the first special drawn with a SHAPE rather than a shading, and the first that takes the type colour: the frozen ball (#329).** `BallShading.Frozen` is the sixteenth member, drawn for `BallKind.Frozen` as a **ninth bucket region** — and the only one of the six that is a full `TYPE_COUNT × LodCount` **plane** rather than four colourless buckets.

- **The colour is the point, which is the exact opposite of the bomb's rule and follows from the same argument.** A bomb, a zap and an acid wear a `BallType` nothing may read, so one tintless draw serves the lot; a frozen ball is a ball that *will be* red when the ice breaks, and a player who cannot see which colour cannot plan around it — which is all this kind is for. So the tint is a per-draw uniform again, i.e. a bucket per colour, and `DrawFrozen` is `DrawPlane`'s loop with a technique of its own around it. It costs thirteen more null references and, in a frame, one draw call per colour actually frozen — one or two on a level with ice, and nothing at all on the hundred and ten that have none.
- **⚠ ITS COLLISION TO SOLVE IS `BallStyle.Ice`, and it is the worst on the list.** On an `Ice`-style level every ordinary ball is *already* a frosted ball with its colour inside it, so no amount of frost, crazing or cold rim could separate the two. What separates them is **shape**: this is the only ball in the game with flat faces. Photographed side by side on an `Ice` level — a row of thirteen frosted spheres over a row of the same thirteen frozen — the two rows are unmistakable at a glance and at any distance, which is the check the kind had to pass before anything else about it mattered.
- **The block is cut in the VERTEX shader out of the same sphere every other ball uses**, exactly as the rock's silhouette is (#340), so this kind needed **no second mesh, no draw order and no transparency sort** — which is what #329 expected a "geometric overlay" to cost. `FrozenVS` scales each vertex to the superellipsoid `(|x|^p + |y|^p + |z|^p)^(-1/p)` at `FrozenCubePower` 4.5, normalised by its value at a corner so the **corners touch the sphere and every other point is inside it**: inward-only, so the drawn block never leaves the cell the simulation gives it, and the physics body stays a sphere. The lattice is uniform at exactly 1.0 between neighbours in every direction, so corners-on-the-0.5-sphere is touching and never overlapping. Faces land at `3^(1/p − 1/2)` = 0.74 of the ball radius, which is why a block reads a little smaller than the balls around it — forced by the cell, not chosen.
- **The normal is exact, which is what a solid of revolution buys over the rock's noise field.** Working the rock's `n = d − tangential(∇r)/r` through for this `r`, the two terms cancel outright: `∇r/r` is `−g/S` with `g = |d|^(p−1) sign(d)`, and `g·d = S`, so the normal falls out as **`g` itself** — no chain-rule factor, no slope term, no near-pole guard. That is also why the flat faces come out flat rather than very slightly domed.
- **Contract point 6 is answered twice over**: the block turns with the body (a rotating cube is the strongest rolling cue in the file) and the frost and the core feather under it are object-space on top of that. The one view-space term is how much colour comes through — which is what a solid seen through scattering ice actually does, and it is what gives each face a coloured centre inside a pale rim.
- **Drawn OPAQUE, deliberately.** What makes ice read as ice is its refraction, its rim and the colour glowing out of the middle, and all three are things a surface can do; a transparent draw would add seeing the cluster *through* the block, which is worth less than it costs — see `DrawShell` on why nothing in here is sorted, and what that would have meant for a silhouette that is a cube.
- **⚠ Both frost figures were wrong in the same direction and it was photographed, not argued.** The first cut ran `FrozenFrostFrequency` 26 at amplitude 0.010 and came out as regular **diagonal corduroy** on every block. The arithmetic says why, and it is the trap the word "frequency" hides here: `ReliefOctave` is evaluated on a **unit direction**, so 26 is 26 radians across the whole ball — four cycles — and four cycles at a tenth of a ball radius of relief is a set of ridges rather than grain. Calibrated against `IceFrostFrequency`/`IceFrostDepth`, which run 30–72 at a quarter of the amplitude and look like frost because of it: **41 with ratios 1.0/1.43/0.71/1.87 at 0.0028**, four waves rather than three because three left one dominant. The core feather is three rectified octaves for the same reason a single one is never enough anywhere in this file — one wave is a stripe.
- **Its three uniforms are the quietest set on the list, on purpose.** A bomb, a zap and an acid each say "a landing beside me sets this off", so their figures are lit and moving; this one says "not yet" and must not compete with them for the eye. `FROZEN_EMISSION` 0.42 and `FROZEN_PULSE_DEPTH` 0.22 at the **cluster's own** pulse speed — so it is the one special draw that does not have to put the speed back by hand afterwards, and the visible half of the ruling that it is an ordinary ball waiting behind glass rather than a device.

**And a seventh, the second that takes the type colour and the one whose figure had to be corrected twice against photographs: the sick ball (#331).** `BallShading.Infectious` is the sixteenth member, drawn for `BallKind.Infectious` as a **tenth region** and the second full colour **plane** — the ball's own colour under a film of luminous slime, blotches with wet rims and a bead at the underside.

- **The colour is the counterplay**, which is why this is a plane and not four colourless buckets. A sick ball can be matched and shot out like any other, so *which colour it is* is the shot that kills it; a player who cannot read it has no counterplay at all.
- **⚠ Its collision to solve is the acid, and the two are one step apart in hue.** Both are green and wet. What separates them is structural and has to stay so: an acid is a **dark** shell whose figure runs strictly *downward* into a pool, because an acid is a statement about **gravity**; this is the ball's own colour under a film whose blotches creep in **object** space, because an infection is a statement about the surface it is on. One has an axis, the other has none. The bead at the underside is the single world-space term here and is kept deliberately small — slime does run downwards, and taking that away entirely would leave the film reading as paint.
- **⚠ THREE FIGURES WERE WRONG AND EVERY ONE OF THEM COST THE KIND ITS COUNTERPLAY.** Photographed across all thirteen colours, twice, before it was right:
  - **The coverage threshold was set as though the field's mean were a half.** It is a sum of **rectified** sines whose amplitudes total one, and the mean of `|sin|` is `2/π = 0.64` — so a threshold at 0.42 sits *below* the average and passed four fifths of the surface. The ball came out as slime with the type colour gone. **Anything cutting a rectified field has to be measured against 0.64, not 0.5.**
  - **The blotch edge was soft enough to smear the film over the whole ball.** At `InfectEdge` 0.12 the smoothstep's band covered most of the field's own spread, so the film was *partially* present nearly everywhere: a wash of tint and darkening rather than patches. Binary now (0.05), and between the patches the ball is simply itself.
  - **The heartbeat substituted the slime's colour for the ball's instead of mixing them.** Every ordinary ball radiates *its own* colour on the beat, and that glow is a large part of how the thirteen are told apart; handing this technique the slime's colour took it away, and all thirteen photographed as **dark greenish balls with yellow spots** — red as brown, navy and silver both as dark green. It is a `lerp(primary, slime, film)` now: the ball's colour where the film is not, the slime's where it is. This is the one to remember, because it looks like a detail and it is the whole sentence *"an ordinary ball that is sick"* being either true or a claim.
- **Its three uniforms are the loudest of the seven**, deliberately, and the frozen ball's read turned the other way round: ice says *not yet* and must not compete for the eye, slime says **hurry** and is the only thing on the field with a deadline attached. Emission 0.95 on a 1.9 beat at depth 0.45 — faster than the cluster's, well under the zap's 3.1, which is a bad fluorescent tube by design and would say "electrical" rather than "alive".

**And an eighth, the third that takes the type colour: the gravity well (#332).** `BallShading.Gravity` is the seventeenth member, drawn for `BallKind.Gravity` as an **eleventh region** and the third colour **plane** — the ball's own colour behind a lensed shell with rings of light falling **inward** across it.

- **The figure's job is to say "this is why your shot went there"**, which is #332's own warning stated as a look: a curve the player cannot see coming is a bug with a physics explanation. So what is drawn is a **motion**, and its direction is the whole message — rings contracting towards the centre. The bomb's beat swells outward and the acid's liquid runs downward; both say *this will happen when you touch it*. This one says *this is happening now, to anything that comes near*, which is the only honest thing a field can say. The relief is **negative** (the rings are troughs cut into the shell) where the acid's drips are beads lying on it, which is the second half of the inward reading.
- **⚠ And the rings had to be re-spaced, because a figure that only reads from close up says nothing** (the owner's report: *"the blue bands are too narrow — from any distance they are almost invisible"*). They were spaced in the grazing term `1 − dot(normal, eye)`, and a sphere's drawn radius is `sin θ`, not that: evenly spaced in the grazing term means **crowded into the limb**, so three rings landed at **55 %, 87 % and 99 % of the drawn radius**, each thinner than the last. Photographed at 14, 26 and 40 units over five phases, what survived past about twenty units was one thin crescent on the edge, and by forty the well was flat violet. The parameter is the disc's own radius now (`sqrt(1 − facing²)`, the same dot product, no extra cost) and the count is **two at width 0.42** rather than three at 0.30: measured on the well's own pixels, the figure's luminance contrast goes **19.8 → 23.3 at 26 units and 18.9 → 20.5 at 40**, and what the eye gets back is a target contracting inward instead of a lit edge. ⚠ The contrast figure is worth what it is worth and no more: it ranked *both* candidates above the old look and could not separate the two (a plain widening scored the same), so the choice between them was made on the pictures. **It does not crawl**: five consecutive frames at 40, 60 and 80 units show the figure fading out on its band limit, with the well changing about twice as much frame to frame as an ordinary ball in the same frames (12–16 codes against 7–9) — which is rings moving, not speckle; blown up eight times, a distant well is a smooth ball with no figure left on it.
- **The lens is a fake and that is what keeps this opaque.** The scene is not re-sampled; the shell bends the normal the *environment* term reflects about, outward and hardest at the rim, which at a grazing angle is most of what a refracting sphere actually shows. So it draws with the opaque kinds and stays out of the order `DrawShell` has to keep.
- **Its collisions are `Plasma` and `Gem`**, the other two styles that bend what is behind them. Plasma drifts and glows all over; a gem is faceted and still. This is smooth, dark-rimmed and moving in **one** direction, which is what neither of them does.
- **The coloured planes and the colourless regions divide the specials exactly along the line #323 drew**: a kind whose colour the player may *read* needs a bucket per colour (frozen, infectious, gravity and now heavy — all matchable or plan-able), and a kind whose colour nothing may read needs one draw (rock, glass, bomb, zap, acid).

**And a ninth, the fourth that takes the type colour: the heavy ball (#333).** `BallShading.Heavy` is the eighteenth member, drawn for `BallKind.Heavy` as a **twelfth region** and the fourth colour **plane** — the type colour greyed, crushed and set on a cold iron floor, under a metal's raised sky reflection, with one mould seam ringing it. It is the last region #256's ten kinds need.

- **⚠ The look must not try to draw the mass, and that is the whole brief.** What says "heavy" is the branch under it hanging lower, which the simulation gives for free and is the reason the kind is a mass rather than an effect (see `docs/game-session.md`). This technique answers the narrower half the physics cannot: *which* ball is the heavy one, on a still frame, before anything has moved.
- **⚠ Darkening alone was the first build and it was wrong — measured, on a capture.** A tint that is only crushed keeps all of its hue, so a heavy yellow photographed as a chocolate-brown ball: an ordinary `Type10` with the lights off, which is the one reading this must not have. **Desaturation is the axis that says "metal" rather than "dark colour"**, and it is the marble's own finding once more — a figure has to move the colour along an axis the shading does not. The shipped pair is 0.30 of the ball's light with the tint carried 0.40 of the way to its own grey, photographed across all thirteen: every casting is recognisably the colour of the ordinary ball beside it and none of them reads as `Type8`.
- **⚠ `SurfaceSpecular.Highlight` and `.Environment` are SCALES where 1 is what every other ball gets**, and the first build set them to 0.86 and 0.80 — i.e. asked for *less* light than an ordinary ball, and photographed as dull plastic. A polished casting picks the sky up: 1.30 and 1.25, which is the marble's measured move (a dark ball with a raised sky in it reads as metal at a distance where no figure is resolvable at all).
- **⚠ The mould seam replaced a cast grain, and the grain's failure is the lesson.** A mid-frequency octave sum roughening the polish was photographed at three strengths: invisible at play distance at all of them, and at the strongest, from one ball away, reading as faint **facets** rather than as sand. A figure that only appears as an artefact is worse than none and is paid for on every heavy pixel. One hard line is the opposite — `SeamLine` fades the *line* rather than the wave's amplitude, so it survives to a pixel wide and then leaves — and a parting line is what a cast ball actually wears. It stands **proud** of the surface (the well's rings and the ice's cracks are cut in; a flash is metal that escaped between the mould halves), is rougher and a touch darker than the casting either side of it, and rings an axis the vinyl's welds do not use, so it is a line no ordinary ball can wear.
- **It is the one special that emits nothing of its own**, and its two animation channels ask for *less* than every other kind's: half the cluster's pulse rate at a deeper amplitude. That is the rock's legibility argument from the other end — a rock reads by not breathing at all, this one by breathing slower and deeper than the cluster around it — and the depth is what keeps a half-rate beat visible instead of reading as a ball that is merely unlit.

**A shot in flight is drawn stretched along its own velocity (#402), and what that is worth is a question about the CAMERA rather than about the blur.** `BallDrawFrame.AddOriented` takes a `stretch` vector — the direction of travel scaled by how much longer the ball is drawn — and applies it as the world-space outer product `I + k n nᵀ` post-multiplied onto the ball's own turn. World space and therefore *after* the turn is the whole of why it is an outer product rather than a scale matrix: a ball spins as it flies, and a scale composed into its local frame would swing the smear round with the pattern instead of holding it along the trajectory. It is nine multiply-adds on a handful of balls a frame, written out rather than built as a `Matrix` and multiplied, which is `BestPractices.md`'s own rule about the translation one step further along the same loop. **No new pass, no velocity buffer, no second draw** — the issue's own instruction, and what the per-frame hygiene demands of anything that would compete with supersampling's figures.

- **Only a shot in flight is offered it, and that is a rule rather than a saving.** A released ball falls just as fast and must **not** smear: its fall is the payoff the player is watching, and a cluster raining stretched ellipsoids reads as the frame rate having collapsed. The shot is the one ball whose speed the player aimed.
- It reads the body's own **velocity** rather than differencing interpolated poses, and it grows from the speed *above* `STRETCH_FLOOR_SPEED` so the effect arrives from nothing instead of stepping in at full strength; `STRETCH_CEILING` caps it at half again as long as it is wide, because an uncapped proportional stretch at a shot's muzzle speed draws a streak several balls long that stops reading as a ball at all.
- ⚠ **And the finding that matters more than the feature: from the play camera the shot flies AWAY from the viewer, so this stretch is foreshortened to almost nothing exactly where the report came from.** That is correct behaviour — a true per-pixel motion blur would be just as small on a vector pointing down the view axis — but it means a velocity stretch is not the answer to "the flying ball has no blur" as seen from behind the gun. Where it does read is where the shot **crosses** the view: the drop cinematic, the result page's orbit, and any camera off the firing line. **The half of #402 that would read from the play camera is the cannon's own traverse**, whose swing is across the frame rather than along the view — the same `StretchAlong` helper pointed at the gun's world matrix and driven by the traverse rate, which is left undone rather than guessed at (the issue asks for design thought and the barrel is a long rigid object where a rigid stretch may well read as a fault).

**The clear-to-colour crossing is drawn by the dissolve's two signs, and costs no new machinery at all (#325).** When a shot colours a glass ball, the ball is logically its new colour at once and the *drawing* crosses over `ClusterCollector.COLOUR_FADE_SECONDS` (0.35 s): `BallDrawFrame.Route` puts the ball out **twice** — into its new colour's bucket at `−d` and into the hollow region at `+d` — and the two signs partition its pixels between them exactly, because `clip(D >= 0 ? noise − D : −D − noise)` keeps complementary halves of the same screen-space dither. No blend, no sorting, no third program, and no new instance channel: `Dissolve` was built for the magazine's transmute and a cluster ball's is otherwise always zero, so the channel was free. It is visible in the frame census as it happens — a landing that coloured four balls drew **five more instances than usual** for a third of a second (four ghosts plus the shot), which is the cheapest possible confirmation that the crossing is running and transient. `DrawHollow` deliberately leaves `DrawnCount` alone for the same reason a bubble's second wall does: the censuses count balls, not draws.

**The thaw is that same crossing pointed at a third bucket (#329)**, at the same duration for the same reason — they are one event seen twice, a ball stopping being one kind and becoming an ordinary one, and two durations for that would read as two mechanics. It needed a **timer of its own** on `PhysicsBall` all the same, and that is forced rather than untidy: the sign arithmetic is identical, but *which* second bucket a crossing draws out of is not something a number of seconds can carry, so sharing `ColourFadeRemaining` would have drawn every thawing ball as a ghost of clear glass. `ClusterCollector` advances it in the same once-per-frame visit as the other three pieces of per-ball state, which is why there is exactly one of them.

**The infection's two crossings share ONE timer, and the rule that decides which is worth stating (#331).** A ball made sick crosses out of its ordinary colour bucket; a ball hardened crosses out of the sick plane into the rock region — and both are told apart by the ball's **new kind**: sick means it was ordinary, stone means it was sick. So one field serves, where the thaw needed a second one because a thawed ball ends up `Normal`, which says nothing about where it came from. The two can never collide, because `BallsMap.SpreadInfection` reads the whole sick population before any of it acts. The hardening half is also **the one case in `Route` where a kind is drawn out of two regions**, and it is the half the player pays for — so it is the half that has to be seen to happen rather than appearing between two frames.

**And the wildcard's lock-in is the first crossing with BOTH ends in the ordinary colour planes (#437).** A wildcard is built to be seen never settling — it dissolves between colours continuously, and that is the whole of how it says what it is — so the instant it lands and resolves, that motion simply stopped wherever the shared cycle happened to be, and nothing told the player it had become a colour. It now crosses: the colour it was wearing going out at `+d` while the colour it resolved to comes in at `−d`, over `ClusterCollector.LOCK_FADE_SECONDS` (0.5 s). The mechanism is #325's exactly and needed no shader work, but two things about it are new. **It is the only crossing whose second bucket is not a region but another colour**, so it is the only one that has to carry an index beside its timer (`PhysicsBall.LockFromType`) — every one before it crosses out of the glass, the ice, the slime or the ash, which a kind or a second timer can name. And **it is the least contrasty crossing in the game**, which is why it is the longest: the glass one starts from a look nothing else in the frame has and is legible the moment it begins, while this one starts and ends on an ordinary ball of an ordinary colour, so what the eye has to catch is a colour travelling rather than a material changing. Still under the second the group it may have just completed takes to fall, which is `COLOUR_FADE_SECONDS`'s own argument. **It runs only when the colour actually changed**: a wildcard that lands beside nothing matchable keeps what it was showing, and a crossing from a colour to itself is two draws partitioning one ball's pixels between two identical looks. The outgoing colour is read off the ball's own `Type` before the resolve overwrites it — the game keeps a wildcard's type equal to whatever the shared cycle shows on every frame it is in the air, so that type *is* what the player was looking at, and nothing has to sample `WildcardCycle` a second time (#330's one clock stays one clock).
- **It is not drawn without emission, and physical honesty was wrong three times running.** A rock is solid and does not glow, so the first build gave it none — and it came out black, next to the 8-ball rather than next to the stone it is. The player looks **up** at the cluster from the island, at its unlit side, and every other ball lights that side by two routes this material has neither of: its own glow and `TranslucencyStrength`, the key light carried *through* a hollow shell. Raising the body tint did nothing; nor did giving it the strongest ambient in `BasicEffectParamsProvider` (which it needed anyway — the draw passed `null` effect params at first and fell back on `DefaultLighting`'s dim **blue**). The emission stands in for the translucency, at 0.45, and `PulseDepth` stays zero: the rock is the one ball in the game that does not breathe, and that is what names it across a whole field. A fourth figure came from the **map editor**, which is exactly what that program is for: `StoneEnvironment` was cut to 0.20 as though matte meant blind to the sky, and at this smoothness the term delivers diffuse *skylight* — the reflection is blurred all the way to the sky's average, which is most of what lights a real rock outdoors. At 0.20 the rock read near-black in the editor while every colour around it was washed with sky; at **0.55** the two programs agree on it. Measured under a controlled A/B — the same level file opened in both, the same `D1` forward view, so the same dome and a comparable vantage — the rock comes back **117/100/81** in the Testbed and **106/94/79** in the editor, inside the run-to-run spread. The *coloured* balls in that same pair did **not** agree (green 63/113/44 against 31/190/26) — which was read at the time as the Testbed's own overcast lerp and, measured properly since, is not: that term is worth two codes on a ball. Whatever separates the two programs on colour is still open (#367) and is not about this material either — see "Judging colour through the Testbed" below.

**Judging colour through the Testbed carries weather the Game never has.** The Testbed steps `SkyLightRig.StepOvercast` every frame off its own cloud cover, lerping the hemisphere ambient towards an overcast palette; the **Game deliberately never does** (that palette is authored for daylight and would *lighten* a dusk city as the weather thickened — the argument is at `BS3DGame.Scene`'s own rig), and the map editor has no cloud deck to step it with. So the one program the project frames its shading judgements in — `campos`/`camtarget` are the Testbed's — is the only one of the three that applies the term. That much is structural and is why `nooverc` exists.

**What the term is actually worth was then measured inside one program, and it is small** (2026-09-04, the controlled A/B the finding never had: same executable, same run, `weather=overcast` so cover reaches **1.000** and `SkyLightRig.Overcast` settles at **0.982**, which moves the hemisphere ambient from the dome's own `(0.093, 0.315, 0.638)` to the overcast palette's `(0.609, 0.633, 0.679)`). Against that enormous change of ambient, on `Thirteen_Colors` under dome 1 in the meadow: **every ball colour moves 1–2 codes** (green 23/147/20 stepped against 22/146/18 pinned), **no CIEDE2000 pair moves more than 1.4 dE**, and the tightest pair keeps its identity. Under the meadow's own weather — cover 0.142, term 0.149 — it is ≤0.5 dE, inside the ±0.4 the emissive pulse costs anyway. And the ordering is the reverse of what was first reported: over the same pair of captures the arena's **stone** moves most (mean |Δ| 9.7/6.2/1.5 codes), the balls next (6.3/5.2/3.9), the grass least (2.1/1.5/0.5) — a neutral surface takes ambient more readily than a ball does, the ball's own key light and emission carrying most of its read.

**So the two-program figures this paragraph used to carry are not the term.** A green ball reading 63/113/44 in the Testbed against 31/190/26 in the editor cannot be produced by something worth two codes; measured again today the Testbed's own green sits at 31/179/31 against the editor's 45/184/36 through comparable framing. What made the original pair differ that much is still unidentified — the two `D1` presets do not stand at the same distance, and the meadow carries a horizon haze — and it is open as its own question (#367) rather than settled here.

**`nooverc` pins it** (#334), and pass it for any colour A/B the way `nopost` is passed for a shader one. It stops the lerp being stepped, so `SkyLightRig.Overcast` stays at the 0 it starts on and `Lerp(a, b, 0)` gives the dome's own ambient bit-exactly — which is what the Game always draws. It takes away **that term and nothing else**: the deck still drifts, still draws and still takes the sun away per pixel, because the Game has all of that and removing it would make the Testbed *less* like the program it is used to judge. Every run prints one `[overcast]` line saying which regime it is in, in Release as well as Debug, so a capture can be traced afterwards.

**And the Testbed goes on stepping it** — decided 2026-09-04, closing #334. It is that program's own weather feature and it is a playable programme in its own right; the flag is what its other job, the measurement rig, needs, and the flag now demonstrably delivers a reproducible reading (paired captures of the whole palette under both domes agree within ±0.3 dE). **Re-read under it, none of the three colour decisions taken through the unpinned rig moves**: black/navy (#246) is 10.2 dE at its tightest across six styles and two domes, olive's nearest neighbour (#294) 10.1, and black/brown (#315) 6.1 to 10.2 — where #315 left it. The palette's genuinely tightest pair is one nobody has filed: **red/orange, 4.0 dE on the gem under dome 13**.

**A style may not hold two of the thirteen closer together than the vinyl does (#315).** The tints in `GetDiffuseTintByType` were tuned against the moulded ball and are separated on **value as much as on hue**; a style that shades them indirectly can spend the value axis without noticing, and three of them had. Measured whole-disc CIEDE2000 on `Thirteen_Colors`, each style under its own chapter's scene and dome, against a vinyl control whose own tightest pair is **7.9** (black/brown, and it has never been reported): **lava orange/brown 2.6** — the tightest pair anywhere in the game — plus lava black/silver 4.2, **plasma black/silver 4.6**, and **gem black/brown 4.9** with black/silver 4.5 and brown/silver 7.3. The owner's report named black and brown; the instrument found the pair they had seen and two worse ones beside it.

Two faults, two cures, both in `InstancedModel.fx` beside the styles that need them. The **emissive** pair (plasma, lava) share one line: the tint *normalised to its peak channel*, which is what makes the 8-ball glow white instead of not at all and is right — but normalising discards value, so black and silver both arrive at the same near-white and orange and brown at the same orange. `TintEmission` puts it back, scaling the emission's **brightness** by the tint's own luminance (compressed by a square root, floored at `TintEmissionFloor` so the 8-ball still has seams to be seen by). It is a scalar over the **whole** emission and not a factor on the hue, because the lava carries a seam's hottest core to a fixed near-white and a factor on the hue alone would leave those cores identical on all thirteen. The **gem**'s fault is different: nothing there normalises anything, the stone simply drowns in an uncoloured environment mirror, so `SaturateTint` lifts a tint's **chroma at constant luminance** (the identity on a neutral tint by construction — neutrals cannot be separated by hue and inflating them would push them together) and the mirror itself is scaled by the stone's own luminance. After: gem **8.2**, lava **8.2**, plasma **11.3**, and all three read as the same material by eye. ⚠ **The lava figures here are whole-disc and #395 found that the wrong question for this style** — see "#395 is the third pass" below, where the same palette is measured over the seam cores instead and reads very differently. The gem and plasma numbers stand; nobody has asked the core question of them.

**What every ball technique owes, whatever the ball is made of.** A ball technique is not the pattern's with different lighting: most of what it does is *not* about the material, it is written out twice already, and a new one that drops a line of it looks right in a still screenshot and is wrong in play. In full, and carried as a header over the ball techniques in `InstancedModel.fx`: **(1)** the dissolve clip on **both** signs of `Dissolve`, over `DissolvePixelSize` cells; **(2)** the heartbeat with its `dot(worldPosition, PulseDirection) / PulseWavelength` phase offset, which is what makes it a wave through the cluster rather than a strobe; **(3)** the ripple in **both** of its meanings, the sign of `Ripple` choosing between the landing wave and the ceiling's alarm — implement only the positive branch and that style alone silently loses the alarm; **(4)** `SurfaceOcclusion` from the instance's occlusion vector; **(5)** `ApplySeaSubmerge` then `ApplyKillPlaneFade` on the way out; and **(6)** a cue **in object space** that the ball is rolling — the gores exist for this, the film marbles for it, and a material whose whole figure is world- or view-space (a mirror is the obvious trap) draws a spinning ball as a still one. The first five are mechanical; the sixth is a constraint on the *look* and has to be answered before a style is worth building.

**Polished marble is the third style (#305), and it is the heavy one.** `InstancedModelMarble` beside the other two: the type colour as the body of a piece of cut stone, veined through with a pale mineral, under a hard tight polish. Three things make it read as stone and the first is the loudest — the **polish**: the vinyl's broad sheen is cut to `MarbleBroadHighlight` (0.3), the environment is raised to `MarbleEnvironment` (1.6) so the surface picks the dome up the way the vinyl deliberately does not, and one very tight lobe (`MarbleGloss` 190) is added off the key light alone, because three pinpoints on one sphere read as three suns. Then the **veining**: turbulence-warped bands in **object space**, on the same octave sum the vinyl uses for its moulding, which is also this style's whole answer to the contract's rolling cue — the veins turn with the ball and, being aperiodic, read as rotation better than the gores. And **no relief and no seams at all**: polished marble is smooth, there is no `PerturbNormalFromHeight` call in the technique, and that is why it is the one ball style *cheaper* than the vinyl it stands beside rather than dearer.

**#335 made the figure loud without touching the constant that governs it, and that is the whole shape of the fix.** The owner read these balls as a flat tint. One warped sine puts one line through each of its zero crossings, and at 3.5 a ball showed two or three of them on the hemisphere facing the camera — a stone with a couple of hairlines, not a figured marble. The obvious lever is the wrong one and `MARBLE_VEIN_CONTRAST`'s own note says why: over 0.6 the pale types run together, because carrying a vein further towards the mineral **lightens the whole ball** and four of the thirteen are already pale. So the figure had to get louder without the ball getting lighter, which it does two ways. **More veins**: the bold seams go to 4.2 and a **finer web** crosses them at 2.7× on its own axis and its own band limit, which is what a real figured stone has — a few bold seams with a web between them, not a set of parallel bands. And **a dark margin beside each pale core**: real veining is not a light line on stone, it is a light line with a darker selvage where the mineral has stained the rock, and that pairing is most of what makes a seam read across a room. It is also exactly what protects the palette — the margin takes back the value the core adds, so the local step across a vein roughly doubles while the mean barely moves. **Measured**: the tightest pair across the thirteen went 7.2 → 6.7 dE, still above the moulded vinyl's own worst pair of 6.4, with `MARBLE_VEIN_CONTRAST` untouched at 0.6. Cost 7.42/7.49 → 7.50/7.54 ms at ssaa 4 on the ice paragraph's pin — one more sine and two `pow`s, inside the run-to-run spread. Against the metal, which #336 fixed in the same session and which this style is meant to be told apart from: metal now carries a hard horizon and a streaked sun, marble a branching vein net under a pinpoint polish, and the two no longer read as the same softly-lit sphere.

**What the vein is made of took three attempts, and the two failures are the useful part.** The constraint is the usual one — thirteen types apart at a glance — so a fixed white or grey vein is out by construction: it is a white ball with colour between the veins, the trap the beach ball's gores set for Type4 and Type11, and over Type8's 0.045 grey it would be the whole ball. **First attempt, the tint carried a fixed fraction towards white**: a clear golden filigree on black and brown and *invisible* on cyan, red, magenta, green, yellow and orange — photographed on `Kepler`, which is one of the three levels carrying all thirteen types (with `Orrery` and `Garland`). **Second, lifting by a ratio** — the `primary / peak` normalisation the ripple uses, and the obvious correction for an ACES curve — which moved it barely at all, because a saturated tint is already near its own peak and the normalisation has nothing left to give. The reason both failed is the same and it is worth stating plainly: **what a figure competes against on a bright ball is not the body colour, it is the shading.** A lit sphere runs from a blown highlight to a nearly black underside, and a figure that changes the colour by less than the light does across that same ball cannot be seen. **What works is a second mineral**: a pale grey following the stone's own luminance (`MarbleVeinPale` 2.2) with a floor under it (`MarbleVeinFloor` 0.40) so the dark types keep their figure, blended at `MARBLE_VEIN_CONTRAST` (0.6). It reads because it moves the colour along the one axis the shading does not — a vein is *desaturated* where a highlight is merely bright, and no amount of light on a cyan ball makes part of it grey. The body keeps most of its hue inside the vein, so it is still not a white overlay. `MarbleMottle` (0.30) varies the stone between the veins on the same turbulence, for free, and matters most on exactly the saturated tints where the veins read least. **Verified on `Kepler` under the space dome with the post effects off** (film grain and chromatic aberration both disguise a shader change): all thirteen stay nameable, and the figure is strongest on black, cyan, brown, olive and green and weakest on red, orange and yellow — which is where the remaining headroom is if anyone wants more. **Measured cost: marble is cheaper than vinyl, by about 2.5 %.** Paired repeats on `Eleven` under the cavern (959 balls, the same instrument the bubble's figures were taken with), windowed at 1600×900, ssaa 2×, quality pinned `high`, vsync off: vinyl 638.0 / 638.0 FPS against marble 654.0 / 654.0 — about **0.038 ms a frame saved**, against the bubble's 8–10 % *spent*. It is the only ball style that costs less than the one it replaces, and the reason is entirely in what the technique does not do: no gore or cap thresholds, no seam field, no `SurfaceRelief` sum, no `PerturbNormalFromHeight`, and no translucency term. Two earlier paired runs on the same level agreed on the direction and the size (vinyl 683.5 / 680, marble 697.1) at a different absolute level, so the ratio is the part to trust.

**Wound wool is the fourth style (#311), and the only soft one.** `InstancedModelWool`: a ball of yarn dyed the type colour. Three things carry it. **The winding, and specifically that the strand direction changes across the ball** — a single wrap direction is a spool of thread, not a ball of yarn, so three winding axes are blended by a low-frequency mask (`exp2` of a slow wave, normalised — a cheap soft maximum) that mostly picks *one* per region, leaving broad patches of parallel strands with visible crossings in the seams between them. `WoolWindSelect` is how decisively it picks, and it is the whole design: too low and every region shows all three at once, which is a crosshatched net. **The softness of the light** — a wrapped diffuse term over the key light, added only as the *difference* from the Lambert response `ShadePixel` already applied, so it cannot brighten the lit side and only fills the terminator and the far side where a hard ball goes flatly black. And **the fuzz**: a broad Fresnel halo in the **ball's own colour**, never the sky's — this is the slot every other style spends on a mirror, and a wool ball that picks the dome up is a wool ball made of plastic. There is almost no specular, and resisting the highlight is most of the work of making it convincing.

**It is the safest style in the set on the thirteen colours, and — unlike the marble — that needed no measuring, because it falls out of the construction.** The tint is dyed wool: a full-saturation diffuse body with nothing reflecting, transmitting or radiating enough to dilute it. **The general lesson the two styles make together is worth stating on its own: a figure built out of COLOUR competes with the shading and can lose, and a figure built out of the NORMAL is shading and cannot.** The marble's veining went invisible on six of thirteen tints and had to be rebuilt twice; the wool's strands read on all thirteen at the first attempt, black included, because a strand ridge changes the light itself. Prefer a normal-space figure for any style added after these two, and treat a colour-space one as something that has to be photographed against the full palette before it is believed.

Two figures were tuned against the render rather than against a photograph of yarn. `WOOL_STRAND_FREQUENCY` (24, about eight crossings across a diameter) is set by the ball's size **on screen**: a strand has to be several pixels wide at the stand-off a level is played from, or the winding averages into a flat wash and takes the rotation cue with it. And `WOOL_EMISSION` is **0.24**, well under the vinyl's 0.5 — emission is added flat and is not shaded at all, so on a material with no specular to balance it the balls stopped reading as spheres and became discs with a pattern on them; that was visible on the space dome and obvious once the figure was right. **Measured cost**, same instrument as the other two (`Eleven` under the cavern, 959 balls, 1600×900, ssaa 2×, `high`, vsync off): vinyl 639.7 / 639.3 FPS against wool 646.9 / 643.3 — **parity with vinyl, about 0.9 % on the cheap side**. The vinyl control read 639.x here against 638.0 in the marble session, a 0.3 % drift, which is the instrument's own repeatability.

**Anodised metal is the fifth style (#306), and it is thirteen alloys rather than thirteen mirrors.** #272 proposed chrome and named its own fatal objection in the same breath — a near-mirror dilutes its tint by construction — and that objection is right *as stated*: a mirror has no albedo. But it is only true of a **white** metal. A metal's colour is its reflectance at normal incidence (gold reflects gold), so `InstancedModelMetal` makes the tint **F0** and has **no diffuse term at all**. Every photon leaving it bounced off it, which is the single biggest cue that it is metal and the first thing that will look wrong if anyone softens it.

**The sky becomes the albedo, which is the film's fault by another road, and it takes the same departure.** A red metal under a blue sky is `tint × sky` ≈ black — exactly what #258 found for a red film over the meadow — so the environment is taken as a **brightness and not as a colour** (Rec. 709 luminance, then multiplied by the alloy). What is *not* given up is the grazing rim: Fresnel rises to a full mirror there whatever the metal, so the sky's real colour comes back at the silhouette, where it is physically right and cannot be mistaken for the ball's hue. The blend between the two is Schlick's own curve, so nothing is bolted on — the body is tinted-by-luminance and the rim is the honest mirror. `MetalF0Floor` (0.16, taken along the tint's own hue at full brightness) is what keeps Type8 a gunmetal instead of a hole in the picture: a mirror reflecting 4.5 % of a dim sky is not a ball.

**The brush is the rotation cue and it is not decoration.** A perfect mirror sphere spinning looks *identical* frame to frame — the reflection is view- and world-dependent and nothing on the surface turns — so the metal carries fine parallel ridges in **object space**. Its frequency was set by what survives the band-limit and nothing else: at 70 it was arithmetically present and **completely invisible** at play distance (`1 − footprint × 70 / π` clamps to zero on a ball a few dozen pixels across), and 26 with a slightly deeper ridge is what actually streaks the highlight. This is the same lesson the wool's strand frequency records, arrived at from the other side. (#336 moved the base to 34 over four collinear octaves and cut the depth to match; the ceiling that argument establishes still stands — it is *why* the octave stack band-limits per octave rather than being pushed finer as a whole.)

**It used to go flat on a bright featureless dome, and #336 says that was a bug after all.** The paragraph here read "a real limit rather than a bug": `SkyRadiance` is a two-colour vertical gradient, so a mirror ball had nothing to reflect but a gradient, the meadow cluster came out as coloured discs with bright rims, and the conclusion drawn was that the style is *scene-bound* and should ship where there is something worth mirroring. The owner's playtest — "doesn't look like metal, it should be glossier" — is that same observation arriving as a complaint, and the diagnosis was one step short. **A polished surface does not integrate its environment, it shows it**, and an outdoor scene has exactly two hard things in it: the **horizon**, which is an edge and not a ramp, and the **sun**, which is a small object thousands of times brighter than the sky. A gradient contains neither, so no reflectance was ever going to make one appear — which is why "make it glossier" is answered at the environment and not at `MetalReflectance`. `MetalSky` is `SkyRadiance` with those two put back: a `smoothstep` horizon and a tight sun disc inside a broader lobe, against the key light's own specular colour so a dusk dome reflects a dusk sun. It is **local to this style deliberately** — `SkyRadiance` is the ambient every other surface integrates, and sharpening it there would draw a horizon line across the ground, the island, the cannon and all nine other styles. One correction fell out of it: `GroundColor` is an ambient *bounce* colour, much brighter than the ground's own face, so used raw as a reflection it put the lower half of every ball brighter than the sky above it, which is upside down; it is halved.

**And the brush read as bumps because it was two waves a few degrees apart.** Near-parallel waves of different frequency **beat**, and a beat is a row of blobs — the "small bumps on the ball" of the owner's second sentence, and no change of frequency could have fixed it because the fault was the interference rather than the scale. Brushing is *parallel lines*, and lines come from octaves along **one** direction: collinear sines sum into a one-dimensional profile, which is a set of grooves of unequal depth and spacing. Four of them now, along `MetalBrushA`, with `MetalBrushB` moved to genuinely perpendicular and demoted to the one job a second direction is good for — varying how deep the brush bit *along* a scratch, so a line fades in, runs and dies instead of circling the ball at one depth. **Measured**: metal's tightest pair loosened from 15.6 to 12.6 dE (black/silver), still the loosest palette of any style, since the tint *is* the reflectance; cost 7.26/7.28 → 7.39/7.42 ms at ssaa 4 on the ice paragraph's pin, about +2 %. It uses `AddLight` and `AddSceneLights` exactly as `ShadePixel` does, so a campfire or the city's neon lights a metal ball like everything else; only the **specular** half of that accumulation is kept. **Measured cost** (same instrument): vinyl 621.9 / 621.9 FPS against metal 654.4 / 650.2 — **about 4.9 % cheaper**, the cheapest ball style there is, since it has no diffuse, no pattern thresholds, no seam field and only two relief octaves. The vinyl control read 621.9 here against 638–640 in the two sessions before it, a 2.6 % drift, so the **ratio** is the part to trust and not the absolute numbers.

**Frosted ice is the sixth style (#307), and the decision that shaped it is that it is opaque.** #272 framed it as "a cold cousin of the glass bubble", and building it that way would have cost the whole two-pass opposite-cull machinery in `BallRenderSet.Draw`, doubled the ball pass, and re-opened everything `BUBBLE_BODY_OPACITY` settled. It is also not the right physics: **frosted** ice is not **clear** ice, frosting *is* short-range subsurface scattering, and you cannot see a level through a frosted marble. So `InstancedModelIce` is a lit solid with a strong translucency term through it — `ICE_TRANSLUCENCY` is 1.1 against the vinyl skin's 0.35, which is the difference between a thin shell letting a little light past and a solid that scatters all the way through — and the result is a genuinely different *material* from the film rather than a recolour of one. **That term is the style's whole read and a screenshot from the sun's own side cannot show it**: what says "solid but not opaque to light" is a ball with the key behind it glowing through in its own colour instead of going flatly black. The figure is a crack net built **in the normal first and the colour second** — a groove that catches light, then a cool thread along it — which is #305 and #311's shared lesson applied on purpose.

**#337 replaced the net itself, and the fault it fixed is a kind worth naming: the figure was regular.** Until it, the cracks were three `SeamLine` fields crossing — three families of straight, evenly spaced bands around great circles — and the owner's playtest read the ball not as ice but as **quilted leather**, which is exactly what three regular line families over a surface stitch. No width and no frequency could have fixed that; a finer quilting is still quilting. Ice breaks into irregular polygonal plates meeting three at a point along edges of unequal length that open, taper and die out, which is a Voronoi diagram — so the net is one now (`VoronoiEdgeCell3`, added to `Noise.fxh` for it, evaluated on the object-space direction because a sphere has no seamless 2D parameterisation to look one up in). Three things came with it. Each stretch of crack **wanders its own width** (0.18× to 1.55× of `ICE_CRACK_WIDTH`), so one edge runs from a gap to a hairline and stops — a net drawn at one width all over a ball is a drawn net. Each **plate carries its own value** from the cell's id, and that is the half of the figure which survives to play distance, where the hairline between two plates has long gone sub-pixel: it does it from **both ends at once** (`IcePlateShade` takes value out of the body, `ICE_PLATE_CONTRAST` adds cool light back) because a plate that is only ever lightened is invisible on white and one only ever darkened is invisible on the 8-ball. And the frost grain under them became **four waves along mixed directions** from one — a single wave is not grain but stripes, and once the plates were large and smooth the hatching came straight out of the same shot.

Two traps are worth keeping, and they are the same trap from opposite sides. The old crack test was written against a **raw sine** and not through `ReliefOctave`, because `ReliefOctave` fades its *amplitude* toward zero as the pixel grows, so a test on `abs(v) < width` would read the **whole ball as one crack** the moment the wave stopped being resolvable; what has to fade is the line, not the field. The new one fades **each half against its own size**: the crack against its own *width* (a border is `width / frequency` in direction units, so `footprint · frequency / width` is that line measured in pixels) and the plate against the *cell*, which is ten times coarser. Written as a plain ramp from zero footprint — the form every other limit in this file takes, because every other one rides a whole wavelength — the crack came out **43 % faded on a ball ninety-nine pixels across**, where the line was still four and a half pixels wide and perfectly sharp. A limit on a thin line has to start late; a limit on a wave can start at once.

**Measured cost** (6900 XT, 1600×900, `Full.json`'s thousand balls frozen at `campos=0,3,14`, medians of paired repeats): the fracture is **not free**. At ssaa 2 the ball pass went **2.04 → 2.44 ms** with this change and at ssaa 4 **7.63 → 8.72 ms** — about **+20 %** and **+14 %** of the whole frame on that pin, which is what 27 hashes a pixel cost. Against the vinyl on the same pin, ice moved from *marginally cheaper* (2.04 against 2.08 ms, which is the figure this paragraph used to carry) to **about 19 % dearer** (2.44 against 2.05). The bubble is still the expensive one at 8–10 % over vinyl for its second pass, but the opaque decision no longer pays for the style outright — it pays for the *pass*, and the fracture spends part of that back. **Unmeasured on the APU**, where attribution does not travel (see the note under the cavern in `docs/scenes.md`).

**And #307 found the hole #304 left, the expensive way.** `BallRenderSet.ShadingOf` ended in `_ => BallShading.Vinyl`. The ice was added to the enum, the parser, the render set, the renderer's parameter table and the shader — and never to that switch — so **it drew as a beach ball**. Nothing failed: the technique compiled, the uniforms were pushed at a program that does not declare them, and the only symptom was that changing the ice's figures changed nothing on screen, which reads as a shader that does not work rather than as one that is never selected. Two rounds of tuning were spent on the wrong thing before the gores were recognised for what they were. #304's load-time check asks that every **shading** has a technique; this is the same question one level up, and the map is now **exhaustive and throws** instead of falling back. That is safe where a fallback is not: only `ApplyStyle` calls it, on a style *change* rather than per frame, and every value reaching it is a real enum member, so an unmapped one is a programming error and not bad input.

**The cut gem is the seventh style (#308), and its facets are shaded and never built.** That is #271's ruling rather than a preference: #231 cut the crystal trophy's mesh to 24 segments with flat per-facet normals, and the owner's word was that the cup should be *plynulý bez ostrých hran* — "the intent of 'crystal sharp' was never to see the sharp edges". At a coarse enough segment count the **outline** goes polygonal, and no shading fixes a faceted rim. #271 named two ways out; this takes the second, so `SphereMesh` is untouched and the sharpness lives entirely in the shading.

**And the faceting has to be a height field, which is a constraint and not a style choice.** A pixel shader here **cannot rotate a vector from object space into world space** — the instance streams carry no tangents and the object-to-world rotation never reaches this stage, which is the whole reason `PerturbNormalFromHeight` exists. So "snap the object-space normal to the nearest facet and shade with it" *cannot be written*: the snapped vector has no way home. What can be written is a **scalar** that depends on the object-space direction, whose gradient `PerturbNormalFromHeight` takes in **screen** space — which does the object-to-world mapping for free. The scalar here is the distance from the ball's surface to the plane of the facet it belongs to: smooth within a face, stepping between faces, and its gradient points along the face's own normal. **This is worth knowing before designing any further style**, because it rules out a whole family of otherwise obvious constructions.

On a sphere the result is also geometrically honest — a cut sphere really would have flat faces at those normals; the trophy's problem was that its profile was not a sphere. Two figures carry the rest: `GemBodyFloor` under the absorbed body, because absorption tuned for the bright hues takes Types 8, 10, 12 and 13 to the same near-black (four of thirteen lost at once), and a **fade back to the smooth sphere** as the footprint grows, since quantized normals put hard edges in *screen* space where no geometric edge exists and hard shading edges alias. `GEM_FACET_COUNT` is 2 for the same band-limit reason the metal's brush and the wool's strands record: at 4 the faces are below what a ball a few dozen pixels across can resolve, so they cost arithmetic and buy nothing. **Measured cost**: vinyl 621.4 / 621.4 FPS against gem 635.3 / 632.0 — **about 2.0 % cheaper**.

**The plasma orb is the eighth style (#309), and it is the only one whose read is motion.** `InstancedModelPlasma`: a dark, nearly empty globe with thin bright filaments crawling across the inside of it, reaching out of a bright core. Every other style is a still material photographed under a moving camera; this one is alive whether anything is happening or not, which is a fact about how it must be **judged** — a screenshot says almost nothing about it, and it was verified by shooting two frames five seconds apart and confirming the arcs had re-formed. The filaments are a **domain warp** — one field displaces the second's sample point, so an arc writhes rather than merely rippling — animated by advancing the phase, which is why they use a local `PlasmaWave` with a phase argument instead of `ReliefOctave`. The heartbeat **rides on the filaments** rather than standing beside them (`PLASMA_EMISSION` is 0), because a second independent brightness over an already-moving surface reads as two effects fighting.

**It is the strongest of all eight on hue and the weakest on value**, and both halves are structural: an emissive line is read directly, diluted by nothing, but the colour lives in thin lines over a dark shell, so a cluster reads dark and a bright dome washes it out. Like the metal, it is scene-bound. **Type 8 needs no special case here** — unusually, since it needed one in three other styles: the filament colour is the tint normalised to its peak channel (the ripple's own trick), so a saturated red gives red arcs and the 8-ball's 0.045 grey gives **white** ones, which is what that toy actually looks like.

**And its measured cost contradicts what #272 and #309 both predicted, in a way worth keeping.** Both called it the dearest of the eight, reasoning that a domain warp is two noise evaluations where every other style has one. Measured: vinyl 637.6 / 638.4 FPS against plasma 685.2 / 685.3 — **about 7.4 % *cheaper*, the cheapest of all eight**. The prediction counted the work *added* and ignored the work *removed*: this technique never calls `ShadePixel` at all — no three-light rig, no scene lights, no hemisphere ambient, no specular, no relief octaves, no seam field, no translucency — and that absence is worth far more than a second sine field costs. **The general form is the thing to carry: a ball style's cost is dominated by what it does not do, so estimate against the vinyl's whole lighting model and not against the figure being added.** Every one of the six new styles came out cheaper than the vinyl it replaces, and only the transparent bubble is dearer.

**Molten crust is the ninth style (#310), and it is the plasma's inverse.** `InstancedModelLava`: a near-black basalt shell broken into plates, with the molten interior glowing through the seams in the type colour and breathing on the cluster's heartbeat. Plasma is thin bright lines over an *empty* dark shell that **writhe**; this is a solid heavy crust whose seams **breathe** — one is electricity, the other heat. They share the emissive-colour trick and nothing else: this is the one new style that **keeps** the vinyl's `SurfaceRelief` machinery, because plates have to read as broken stone. The heartbeat is **routed into the seams** rather than added beside them (`LAVA_EMISSION` is 0, like the plasma's), which is what avoids the double-application this style was most at risk of — a flat emission next to a breathing seam is a ball that pulses twice as hard as its neighbours.

**#338 gave the crust a heat halo, and the reasoning is worth more than the constant.** The owner's complaint was about **area**: the type colour lived only in the seams, which are a fifteenth of the surface, so twelve pixels in thirteen were black whatever colour the ball was. Both obvious fixes are wrong. **Lightening the crust** throws away the argument the style exists for — an emissive seam is the one colour in this game that arrives undiluted, and a crust carrying real tint is a diffuse surface again, which under a dusk dome is thirteen dark grey balls. **Widening the seam** until it covers the ball turns a cracked shell into a coloured ball with black spots, and the cracked shell *is* the read. So the colour comes from **heat** instead, which is still emission: crust within a plate-width of a gap is thin and hot and glows dully, in the ball's own hue and never carried towards `LavaIncandescent` — only the seam's core is white-hot. Two details decided it. The halo is the **max** of the three seam fields and not their sum, because three wide fields added together saturate over most of the ball and the plates stop being plates, where the nearest of them is a distance to the nearest crack, which is what heat follows. And it is shaped by a **power into a gradient**: written as `wide − seam` it is a *plateau*, and the first pass came back a fat-banded **neon cage** with the plates reduced to black islands between the bars — which is the plasma, the one style this one's header says at length to stay away from. **Measured** (same pin as the ice above, `-Whole` on a `Thirteen_Colors` capture): the share of a ball's disc carrying real chroma went **48.7 % → 57.8 %** and mean chroma **51.9 → 59.7**, at unchanged mean luminance — more colour, not more light. #315's tightest pair, orange/brown, read **8.0 dE before against 7.6 and 8.3 on two captures after**, i.e. unchanged inside the ±0.4 dE the emissive heartbeat puts on any single capture; nothing else got tighter. Cost is three more sine lookups and a `pow`: **7.59/7.65 ms before against 7.67/7.69 after** at ssaa 4, which is inside the run-to-run spread.

**#395 is the third pass at this style's colour and the first measured with an instrument that matches the complaint.** The owner reported the Eruption's ten levels as colours he could not tell apart — and, on Volley, as a suspected bug in the *match rule*, a cluster that would not release when what had actually happened was a shot at a colour that only looked similar. ⚠ **The palette measured fine and that was the trap.** `palette.ps1 -Whole` under the volcano and dome 9 — the scene these ten levels are actually played in, which #315 never measured, having read Lava under the Reveal's — puts the tightest pair at **orange/brown 7.4 dE**, against the vinyl control's own 7.9. Nothing to see.

**The disc average is the wrong instrument here, for the same reason the gore median was.** `-Whole` exists because on the emissive styles the bright pixels *are* the colour; but it averages them with a crust that is the same near-black on all thirteen, and reports a colour the eye never isolates. What a glance on this style actually reads is the **net**. Measured over the **brightest tenth of each disc**, four captures per build, the shipped style came back at a mean core saturation of **0.283** across the block's eight inks — silver **0.00**, black 0.06, blue 0.07, white 0.11 — i.e. eight of thirteen balls wearing a *neutral* net, and the tightest core pair was **yellow/white at 7.6 dE** on all four captures. That is `LavaIncandescent`: the carry to near-white was **uncapped**, so every tint bright enough to reach it arrived at the same colour exactly where the ball is brightest.

**Three levers, and the first is the owner's own words** — *thicker lines, and not carried all the way to white; the hottest point should still read as the ball's colour, just brighter*:

- **`LavaCoreCarry` 0.30** caps how far the core desaturates, and **`LavaCoreLift` 1.4** puts back, as brightness, the "hotter" the desaturation used to say. Not zero carry: a core at exactly its own hue reads as a coloured line painted in a groove, which is what the carry was introduced to avoid.
- **`LavaHuePower` 1.7** cuts the normalised hue deeper — the peak channel holds and the other two fall, so a warm ink gets warmer. ⚠ **Not `SaturateTint`**, which is the obvious call and does nothing: its first step is `primary/peak`, which is what this style already had.
- **`LavaValuePower` 1.0** opens up `TintEmission`'s square root, which compressed the one axis this style has left for the warm family (the crust is the same darkness on all thirteen by design). It is lava's **own** copy of that curve, deliberately not a change to the shared one, which the plasma also calls and which #395 did not measure.

**Measured, four captures each, same pin** (`Thirteen_Colors`, volcano, dome 9, `nopost nooverc ssaa=2`, F5): mean core saturation **0.283 → 0.510, +80 %**, with every ink up (brown 0.58 → 0.76, red 0.40 → 0.58, orange 0.36 → 0.52, yellow 0.24 → 0.36). The seam width went 0.36 → **0.46** and `LavaHeatWidth` 1.9 → **1.55** in the same breath, which is the pair to read together: the halo's width is a *multiple* of the seam's and `SeamLine` tests |sin| against it, so widening the seam alone would have carried the halo from 0.68 to 0.87 of the ceiling and flooded the plates — the "coloured ball with black spots" the paragraph above warns about, arriving through the back door. Held at 0.684 → 0.713, it did not.

⚠ **What it did NOT fix, and the next agent should know exactly where the line is.** The tightest core pair moved only 7.6 → **8.0 dE** — but it **changed identity**, from yellow/white (a neutral convergence, which is what the white carry was doing and is now gone) to **brown/orange/red**, the warm family. Those three are one hue family and cannot be separated by hue at all; value is the only axis, `LavaValuePower` has already spent it, and what is left is the **block's inks** — this issue's own step 3, in `LevelGen`, where the Eruption's law is three cold inks and two warm and Meander, Plume and Paroxysm carry more.

**The inks were the rest of it, and the finding is about the style rather than about one chapter: on the molten crust a palette has as many colours as it has HUE FAMILIES, not as many as it has tints.** The seam's colour is the tint over its own peak channel, so brown (0.42, 0.24, 0.11) arrives as a darker orange, and black, silver and white all arrive as a pale neutral net that differs only in brightness — and brightness is the axis a hanging cluster takes away, through occlusion and distance, where hue survives. The Eruption shipped its theme's colours, red, orange, yellow and white hot over black, brown, olive and silver cold, and on this style those eight inks were five colours. Measured on the `Thirteen_Colors` row under the volcano and dome 9 (`balls=lava nopost nooverc ssaa=2`, seven captures across the heartbeat in one run, `palette.ps1 -Cores` over the brightest quarter and half of each disc, `-LightnessWeight 2`, the smaller of the two readings), **the five tightest pairs in the palette were all inside the block's own eight inks**: orange/brown **6.1**, red/orange **6.8**, red/brown **7.7**, black/silver **8.4**, white/yellow **12.0**. Sill — the level the playtest called unfinishable, "the player can only shoot at random" — carried five inks from three families.

So the block's palette is now the style's (`Tools/LevelGen/Designs/Block08_Eruption.cs`, the `ERUPTION_*` inks): **red and yellow hot, black, cyan and navy cold, magenta a sixth ink only where a member needs a colour none of its neighbours carries, and never more than six in a level.** One ink from each family the style can tell apart — the tightest pair of the five is **25.2** (black/navy) and with the sixth **21.4** (navy/magenta), against the shipped palette's 6.1 — and it reads as the volcano still: the lava, the sulphur it leaves, and the blue flame sulphur burns with over black rock. A warm-only palette was not possible and was checked before the cold hues were reached for: the crust has three warm-to-neutral families (the red one, the yellow one, the neutral one) and the block's own law needs five inks. The owner chose between this and a green in place of the cyan (20.6, yellow/green the tightest) from a side-by-side of Sill in the Game.

**Nine of the ten levels kept every group they had.** Where a level's parts never touch, two old inks could share one new one without welding anything (Volley's ash and bomb bodies, Plume's hoops and fallout), and LevelGen's standing groups, anchor loads and reachability read identically before and after. So did the sag probe's verdicts: where the inks mapped one for one the probe played the same game, Breach and Causeway to the hundredth and Caldera shot for shot within the solver's own noise; and it gave the same verdict where two inks folded into one and the probe, which loads its rounds by colour, played a different game on the same groups (Volley). **Meander** is the exception, and knowingly: its weirs touch both hot inks and all three cold ones, so they take the sixth ink whole and each bar is one slab instead of two (36 → 33 groups). **Fume**'s pipes were not free to reorder either — three hot inks over five pipes, two pairs of which touch — and the first two orders welded pipes into a 35-ball group; the shipped order was chosen by counting groups over the level's real ball contacts. The #395 cannon half (`StillEmission`) is independent of all of this.

Plasma shares the normalise-to-peak trick and nobody has asked it this question: a plasma block's inks should be read with `-Cores` before it is assumed to be free of the same families.

**The seams have to wander or the style is a wire cage, and it was built as one first.** Three sine fields on a sphere cut **great circles**, and three great circles read as wire wrapped round a ball rather than as rock that has cracked — which is exactly what the first build looked like. The fix is the plasma's domain warp at a fraction of the strength and without the animation: displace the coordinate the seams are read at, enough that a seam wanders and forks, not enough that it writhes. **This is worth knowing for any future style built out of `SeamLine`** — that helper is now shared by the ice's cracks and these seams, which want the same line and opposite things from it (the ice brightens along it because a crack is an internal face catching light; the lava glows through it because there is molten rock behind it), and both cut a groove.

Type 8 takes the plasma's answer again — the seam colour is the tint normalised to its peak, so the 8-ball glows **white-hot**, which for lava is not even a departure since the hottest part of a real flow is the whitest. What the style gives up knowingly is **value** separation: the crust is the same darkness on all thirteen, so hue does all the work — which is why a lava level has to be inked by hue family, not by tint (#395, above). **Measured cost**: vinyl 638.7 / 638.0 FPS against lava 643.8 / 639.8 — **parity, about 0.5 % on the cheap side**, and it is the dearest of the six new opaque styles for exactly the reason the plasma is the cheapest: it is the only one that gives nothing up.

**Crackled porcelain is the tenth style (#312) and the last of #272's eight.** `InstancedModelPorcelain`: a deep, wet-looking coloured glaze over a ceramic body, crazed with the fine hairline net an old glaze develops. What makes it porcelain rather than a shiny ball is **glaze depth** — the colour sits slightly *under* the surface, because a tight bright lobe for the glaze's own face is laid over a body that is already shaded, and that lobe is taken off the **smooth** normal rather than the crazed one, deliberately: the glaze is continuous over a hairline crack, and a highlight that broke at every crack would say *chipped* rather than *crazed*. Like the marble it carries no relief, since a fired glaze is glass.

**#339 rebuilt it, and the finding was in the comment rather than the code.** The header above had always said "a coloured glaze over a **white ceramic body**", and the shader had no white body in it: `color` was the tint, straight. A coloured diffuse sphere under a tight highlight is *painted plastic*, and "it isn't clear what this style is supposed to be at all" is exactly what painted plastic looks like — the owner could not name the material because the shader was not drawing one. So the body is white clay now and the tint is a layer lying on it: where the coat thins the clay reads through, a craze line is the glaze parted **down to the clay** and stained the brown an old piece takes up, and the clay passes light (`PORCELAIN_TRANSLUCENCY`, a third of the ice's — a held-up teacup glows and no plastic does, and it goes through the *clay's* colour because a red cup lit from behind glows warm white). The stained clay also **replaces the luminance inversion** the crack tone used to run on, and gets the same crossover for a reason instead of a rule: against a pale glaze the stain is the darker of the two, against a dark one the clay is the lighter, with no test anywhere saying so.

**And the thing that actually names the style is the ornament — the owner's idea, and it is the right one.** China is known by its *decoration*: a material read can be argued about, a painted band cannot be mistaken for anything but tableware. It is a **Hilbert meander in white enamel** round the ball's own equator, and a Hilbert curve is the ornament of this kind that can be *evaluated* rather than stored — no texture, no UVs, nothing in the content pipeline — which also happens to **tile**: the standard curve enters its square at one corner of the bottom edge and leaves at the other, so tiles laid side by side round the equator weld into one continuous meander that closes on itself, which is the construction of a border on a plate and falls out of the curve's own definition. A pixel finds it from its own cell alone: take the cell's index along the curve, ask where the curve was one step before and after, and the curve inside the cell is the polyline from the entry edge's midpoint through the centre to the exit's. Two segment distances. Neighbouring cells cannot be nearer than half a cell unless the curve crosses in — in which case they *are* the entry or exit — so it is exact for any ribbon under half a cell wide. The enamel is a colour step **and** a shallow raised bead, because on the white ball a white band has no colour step at all (#305/#311 again). Antialiased off the **footprint** and not `fwidth` of the distance, twice deliberately: the distance jumps outside the band, and it is read through an `atan2` whose cut would otherwise put one bad column down every ball.

Two measured things decided the tuning. The craze was **band-limited on its wavelength and not on its line**, twenty times too generous, and what that looked like was not a faint net but **white sparkle** — the groove tilts a normal inside a pixel and the glaze's mirror-smooth Fresnel fires on whichever pixels catch it, so the ball came out speckled as if dirty. It is limited on the line now, which makes the craze a close-range figure and gone by play distance, which is precisely why this style could not go on resting its whole read on it. And a **pure white** band over a fifth of the disc lifts every ball towards white by the same amount, which costs the dark end of the palette: black against brown fell from 9.3 dE to 6.0, under the moulded vinyl's own worst pair of 6.4 — the line #315 says every style has to clear. Letting a fifth of the glaze show through the enamel (a slip laid that thin genuinely does) puts it back at **7.1**, with red/orange at 8.1 against the vinyl's 8.1. **Cost**: 7.75/7.78 ms before against 8.01/8.03 after at ssaa 4 on the ice paragraph's pin — about +3 %, which is the curve's two short integer loops plus an `atan2`.

**Its answer to the dark types is an inversion rather than a floor, and it is the last of the three answers this set has needed.** Dark cracks on a black glaze are no cracks, and the normalise-to-peak trick the plasma and lava use does not apply because a crack is not an emission. So the crack's tone **follows the glaze's own luminance and crosses over**: a bright glaze is crazed with *darker* lines, a dark one with *lighter* ones. One rule instead of thirteen constants. The cracks also cut a shallow groove, so the net is a figure in the normal as well as the colour — #305 and #311's lesson applied as insurance on the tints where the tone step is smallest.

**Two things keep it apart from the styles it neighbours**, which is a real risk once there are ten: against the **marble**, its figure is a fine dark hairline net where marble's is broad soft veins in a lighter tint, and its specular is a coat over a body where marble's is a hard surface highlight. Against the **ice**, which shares `SeamLine`: ice cracks are *bright*, being internal faces catching light inside a translucent solid, and these are gaps in an opaque glaze. It inherits the lava's great-circle warning and takes the same wander. **Measured cost**: vinyl 637.0 / 637.5 FPS against porcelain 645.6 / 644.4 — **about 1.2 % cheaper**.

**The whole set, measured against the same vinyl control on the same level.** Bubble **+8–10 %** (the only style dearer than vinyl, and the price of transparency); lava −0.5 %; wool −0.9 %; porcelain −1.2 %; ice −1.5 %; gem −2.0 %; marble −2.5 %; metal −4.9 %; plasma −7.4 %. The ordering is not about how elaborate a style looks — it tracks **how much of the vinyl's lighting model each one keeps**. Lava keeps all of it plus relief and lands at parity; plasma keeps none of it and is the cheapest thing here.

**And the cost of a second technique is not what "each style is its own line item" sounds like.** That is true of compiling and of maintenance — every style is another program in `InstancedModel.fx`, which all three executables build, and another look to keep working across the eighteen domes. It is **false of the frame**: a level names one style, so a frame draws one ball technique. The bubble's measured ~8–10 % below is what a *bubble level* pays against a vinyl one, not a tax the vinyl levels carry, and N styles are not N times anything per frame.

`InstancedModelBubble` is a technique of its own beside `InstancedModelPattern` — the same `PatternVS`, the same instances, the same once-per-frame walk — and not a branch inside it, for the reason measured on this project's other big shaders: an alternative shading model behind a runtime branch costs the union of both register allocations in every wavefront. The film is a dielectric like everything else here, so it reflects `DielectricF0` head-on and rises to a mirror along the rim; on top of that it carries **thin-film interference**, computed rather than textured — the phase goes as `thickness / cos θ` at the three wavelength ratios `1 : 1.236 : 1.511` (680 nm over 680/550/450), so the fringes stretch towards the silhouette, the film drains downward under gravity (measured against the **world** normal, because gravity does not turn with the ball) and marbles with the same octave sum the vinyl skin uses for its moulding (in **object** space, so it turns with the ball and replaces the gores as the cue that a bubble is rolling). Two figures are worth keeping: the band-limit is against `fwidth(path)`, how much of a fringe **one pixel** spans, and the first attempt used `footprint × path` instead — the pixel's reach across the *ball* — which was already fully closed at the stand-off a level is played from, so the whole rainbow existed only in the arithmetic. And a **tinted edge glow** is added beside the Fresnel mirror, because that mirror shows the *sky*: under space, the cavern or a night dome there is no sky to show and every ball came out a flat coloured circle with no edge on it.

**What a ball is dyed does not get to depend on what is behind it, and what it hides decides whether the dye is legible at all.** Both were found by looking at the shipped opening block rather than at a test rig, and both are departures from the physics made for the one constraint the game cannot trade away — thirteen types apart at a glance under eighteen domes. First, the transmitted light takes the backdrop as a **brightness and not as a colour** (Rec. 709 luminance, then multiplied by the dye): as a colour, a dye can only pass what the backdrop happens to contain, so a **red** film over the meadow's **blue** sky passes almost nothing and level 1's red pyramid came out pink whatever `BUBBLE_TINT` was set to. Second, `BUBBLE_BODY_OPACITY` is what a film hides face-on, and what it does not hide is the backdrop arriving *untinted*: the two walls together cover `1 − (1 − f/2)(1 − f)`, so the 0.26 the style shipped with left **64 % of a bright sky standing behind every ball**. It is 0.90 now — a 5 % residue, where 0.84 left 9 % — and it costs the style nothing, which is the thing to re-check before anyone lowers it: a bubble reads as glass through its rim, its pinpoint, its iridescence, the second rim of its own far wall inside the first, and its brightness following whatever is behind it. Photographed at 0.26, 0.55, 0.62, 0.72, 0.84 and 0.90 on the meadow (the brightest sky in the campaign) and on space (the darkest).

**And a shell fades by how much of the pile stands in front of it** (`BubbleScreenFade`), which is the other half of the same complaint and the half that was not about opacity at all. Nothing was fading a buried ball, so four layers of shells showed through one another with **equal clarity** where the eye expects each layer behind the last to be fainter — and the cause is in `DrawShell`'s own note: the far walls are drawn with no depth write, so every ball in the pile puts its inner rim into the picture whether or not four others stand in front of it, and bucket order decides which lands on top. The proper cure is a back-to-front sort, which this renderer structurally cannot do. The order-independent stand-in costs one dot product: the occlusion vector already carries the *direction* a ball's occupied neighbours lie in, so dotting it against the eye asks exactly "are my neighbours between me and the camera?" — positive fades the shell, and a ball on the cluster's near face has its neighbours behind it, so the dot goes negative and it is left alone. A shot in flight and a loaded round carry a zero vector and are untouched by construction. It is a per-*ball* figure, so a shell fades as a whole rather than pixel by pixel, which is right: what is modelled is the depth of the pile in front of it, not the shape of its own surface. `BubbleScreenFade` stops short of 1 deliberately — a deep ball should go faint, not vanish, or the holes a played field is full of stop reading as holes.

**Two things the film does that the skin deliberately does not.** Its **emission is occluded**: "a light buried in the pile is the one that should still show" is an argument about a ball whose neighbours you cannot see past, and it is exactly false of a pile of films — every ball reaches the eye and a pixel shows the *sum* over four or five of them, which at the skin's falloff turned the middle of a 438-ball cluster into a flat pastel wash with no ball in it (photographed on the space scene, where the balls' own light is all there is). The occlusion is **squared** for the same reason, so a fully surrounded bubble keeps about a fifth of its light rather than half. And the **drawing states are `BallRenderSet.Draw`'s** rather than the caller's, which is the one asymmetry in that method: the shell is put out as two passes with opposite cull modes — the far wall first under `DepthRead`, then the near wall under `Default` — and the states are put back as they were found, so the glass drawn after the balls still stands on the caller's baseline. The near wall **writes depth**, which costs the bubbles showing one another through but is not negotiable: the aim beam is the overview's only word on where the gun points and a beam that runs through the cluster is a guide that lies, the muzzle round's halo is carved into a ring by that same buffer (#236), and the ceiling's glass and the floor's laser net both composite against it. The balls are **not sorted**, and cannot be: a ball's colour is a per-draw uniform, so a bucket is one colour and one mesh and a global order over three thousand balls is not expressible as fifty-two instanced calls — what is drawn is bucket order, deterministic within a frame and moving only when a ball changes LOD. **Measured cost**, paired repeats on 959 balls (`Eleven` under the cavern, ssaa 2×, vsync off): windowed at 1600×900, vinyl 576.7 / 593.9 FPS against bubble 537.1 / 536.3 — about 0.15 ms a frame, ~8 %; at 3840×1600, vinyl 166.1 / 166.3 against bubble 149.5 / 149.9 — about 0.66 ms, ~10 %. That is the price of a doubled ball pass with a heavier pixel shader, and it does not move with the opacity or the screening fade: the passes and the instructions are the same whatever the film hides.

## Resolution and aspect (Testbed)

The game targets desktop and Xbox on large screens — 3840×2160 down to 3840×1600, i.e. **16:9 to 21:9**. The windowed default is 1600×900 so the development case is the narrowest targeted aspect rather than a wider-than-target one.

- **`width=<n>` / `height=<n>` set the windowed back buffer directly**, overriding the 1600×900 default so a screenshot can be captured at the desktop's play resolution (e.g. `width=3840 height=2160`) from any machine, regardless of its own panel (#141). This is the same `PreferredBackBufferWidth/Height` path the windowed default takes; it does nothing in fullscreen, where F11 fills the physical monitor. The screenshot skill's `CopyFromScreen` still grabs whatever the window occupies on screen, so a capture wider than the monitor is clipped to it — a true 4K capture off a sub-4K laptop display needs the off-screen `RenderTarget2D` path this argument does not provide.

- `CreatePerspectiveFieldOfView` takes the **vertical** FOV, so a wider display adds width and leaves the vertical framing untouched (`Hor+`). That is the behaviour wanted here — the map keeps its size on an ultrawide and the extra width goes to city — and it means **vertical is the binding constraint at every targeted aspect**, so the frame is already as tight on the map as it can be at 21:9. `GameCameraFit.Solve` still checks both axes, because a narrow display (or a tall window, which `AllowUserResizing` permits) flips which one binds.
- `InfoRenderer` scales its text and icon by `Viewport.Height / 2160`, i.e. it is authored for 2160p and scales down — it does not need changing for 4K.
- **Supersampling is the dominant frame cost at 4K, which it is not at 720p.** Measured on `Full.json` at 3840×1600 uncapped: `ssaa=2` gives ~118 FPS, `ssaa=1` ~362 — 8.5 ms against 2.8, so SSAA costs ~5.7 ms/frame. (At 1280×800 it costs nothing measurable, because CPU physics binds long first.) Comfortable for 60 Hz on a desktop GPU; worth knowing before assuming it on a console.
  - **That "costs nothing at 720p" is a statement about *this desktop*, not about the setting.** On an integrated GPU (Ryzen 7 2700U / Vega 10) SSAA is the biggest thing in the frame at any resolution: `ssaa=2` → `ssaa=1` on `Full.json` went ~8 → ~22 FPS, while *tripling* the ball count 1000 → 3000 moved it only ~8 → ~6-7. There the fixed per-frame scene cost dominates and the ball count barely registers — the exact opposite of the story here, and why the game measures rather than assumes (see "Adaptive quality" in `docs/game-shell.md`). Issues #62/#63/#64. Visually at native 4K, `ssaa=1` shows harder stair-stepping on the city's edges and slightly coarser shading on the balls, but the gap is much smaller than at 720p — the native pixel footprint is already ~3× finer, which is what the relief's band-limiting reads.

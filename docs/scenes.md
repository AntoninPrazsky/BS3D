# The scenes

The seventeen backdrops the game, the Testbed and the map editor all share, plus the island the gun stands on and the glass drain in the middle of it.

Part of the BS3D documentation. CLAUDE.md holds the project overview, the build commands, the ball grid and the repo-wide conventions, and says which of these documents covers what.

## The setting, in one copy

Every executable used to build the setting for itself, and #75 ended that the way #74 ended three copies of the frame's exit (see "Color management" in `docs/rendering.md`). The island's stone cap and concrete drum, the glass drain with its two gold beads and the dark pit shaft behind it (`Prazsky.Core.Render.ArenaIsland`, which owns every mesh, texture, renderer, world matrix and figure of them); the glass plate a cluster hangs from (`CeilingPlate`); the dome-derived light rig, its hemisphere scales, its tint strength and its overcast palette (`SkyLightRig`); a scene's own point lights (`SceneLights`); the drain's collision mesh (`Prazsky.BS3D.Physics.FunnelPhysics`); the ten look values of the cloud deck and the two applies that push them (`CloudField`, which already owned the field's shape); the forest's wood — the fifteen tree, boulder and stump meshes, the twenty-five renderers that dress them, the three matte materials, the encoded tints and the six instanced draws (`ForestScatterRenderer`, the last piece of #75, and the one whose absence had left two of the three executables drawing a bare clearing — see "The forest"); and every question anybody asks about a `SceneKind` — `IsSolidTerrainScene`, `OpenBelow`, `SceneCount`, `CycleLength`, `SceneName`, `TryParseScene`, all statics on `SceneRenderer` now — are one copy each in the libraries. They had been identical value for value, which is not something a *shared* setting should ever have been able to be, and the duplication had already cost twice: the forest was missing from **both** hand-kept `IsSolidTerrainScene` lists (below), and the seven-of-eleven scene cycle was a bare `% 7` in two files.

What the hoist deliberately did **not** move is the frame sequence. `ArenaIsland` hands its draws over in three slices — `DrawIsland`, `DrawPit`, `DrawGlass` — precisely so each executable goes on placing them itself: the Game puts the gun, the cluster and the shot trails between the pit and the glass, and its front end runs the same frame with nothing between them at all. Sky-lit enrolment stays the caller's for the same reason, each having its own list and its own reasons (the editor lights the balls, the city and the forest's scatter, there being no island, drain or ceiling of its own to light) — and all of them leave the pit shaft out, so no dome bleaches the inside of a hole in the ground. The ceiling glass sits outside both lists since #156: it stands against the sky itself, so the Testbed and the Game hand it to `SkyLightRig.ApplyToGlass` instead, which pushes the same rig with the three directional lights scaled to the sky's own brightness — the dome scenes always hid the plate by lighting it from the very palette that shows through it, and the sky-replacing scenes' full-strength sun (kept for the balls and the drain's beads) had turned the plate into a bright slab over their near-black backdrops. The Game's plate renderer may be null mid-load; the push tolerates it. Every scatter renderer takes part wherever the wood stands, walked as the one flat array `ForestScatterRenderer.Renderers` hands back, or a spruce of the variant the list missed would stand under the rig of whatever dome was up when it was made — and since a re-planted wood is twenty-five brand-new renderers, whoever re-plants one has to re-light it too, exactly as a refitted `CeilingPlate` must be.

Three things came out of reconciling the copies:

- **One deliberate visible change, and it is an improvement to the Testbed rather than a no-op — the old number must not be "restored".** The two copies disagreed on exactly one figure: the ground-proximity darkening anchor the balls' and the gun's renderers are handed (`InstancedModelRenderer.GroundHeight`). The Testbed was passing **−9.49**, a value its own comment admitted was kept from the old ground-block layout whose recessed centre block's top sat at −9.5 — geometry deleted long since, so the referent no longer existed. It is the island's own top (`ArenaIsland.TOP_Y`, −8.5) in both executables now. Measured against the shader's ground-occlusion range of 2.0: a ball resting on the stone was getting a proximity of `1 − 0.99/2 = 0.505` instead of `1.0`, i.e. about half the contact darkening it should have, and the effect faded out at y = −7.49 instead of at −6.5. The Game was already correct. (The anchor stays `TOP_Y` now that the walkable top dishes below it — the shader's term saturates under the anchor, so a ball rolling down the dish keeps full contact darkening rather than an overdriven one.)
- **`OpenBelow` is defined as the complement of `IsSolidTerrainScene`, not as a second list.** It was two hand-kept sets in different files, and the negation is a consequence rather than a shortcut: the opaque near-black pit shaft those scenes need is exactly what closes the view from below, so a scene that wants the shaft cannot also give the drop cinematic a vantage under the stone. A twelfth scene is one decision now instead of two that can quietly disagree — and when the twelfth actually arrived (the Moon, #125) the complement held: it wants the shaft and not the dive. What the Moon did split is the *other* pair — it is the first scene in `IsSolidTerrainScene` **and** `ReplacesSky` at once; see "The Moon (Game)" for why that is one deliberate decision and not a drift.
- **`SceneFrame.SunColor` and the clouds' lit side are one number** (`SkyLightRig.SunRadianceTinted`): the sun's own radiance carried towards the dome's horizon colour by `SkyLightRig.SKY_TINT_STRENGTH`, which is what makes the deck lit by literally the same light as everything under it. They were two separately-computed expressions that happened to agree, written out twice per executable, and unifying them took a per-frame `Lerp` **and** a per-frame delegate allocation off the draw path in all three: the cloud hook a `SceneFrame` carries is set once at load on the rig, where a method group written at the call site built a fresh delegate every frame it was evaluated. It is taken off the **dome** and never off `KeyTint`, even though the two are the same expression wherever no scene overrides the rig — a sky-replacing scene states its own key tint precisely because a dome-derived one would be a lie, and folding that override into the sun would change what the terrain shaders are told the sun is.

Verified at runtime in all three executables, and the figures reconciled: every numeric literal of the old island code survived onto `ArenaIsland` at the hoist (the dish has since added `DISH_DEPTH` and moved the drain family that far below `TOP_Y` — see "The city and the glass arena"), and the Game still reports 315 balls / 1088 constraints on `One.json`.

## The city and the glass arena

The default of the seven settings (NumPad2 cycles through the sea, the savanna, the desert, the mountains, the meadow and the neon city, below) — no flag: a **small round stone island** (the drain funnel ringed by a moulded stone platform, below) in a clearing between the towers of a procedural city (`Prazsky.Core.Render.City`, shared with the map editor; see "The shared scene backdrops"). The arena used to be a big square marble-and-glass plaza sized to the ground blocks, but it ate the whole lower frame and hid the scene it stood in; it is now the round island in **every** scene, so the sea, the dunes or the city fill the frame rather than a plaza. The castle backdrop and the shadow overlay are gone, and so are the marble ground blocks — the island's only physics floor is the funnel's own mesh (see "The drain funnel").

The city stands **over** the arena; it no longer falls away underneath it. The first version cleared the blocks under the glass, which left nothing to look through the glass at; a second version filled them back in, cut off far below so a look down the drain funnel would read as a drop. **#275 took the under-arena blocks back out again, for good**: no play or drop-cinematic camera angle ever looks down the funnel's narrow throat far enough to see that shaft, so it was cost with no payoff — the clearing above reads exactly the same either way, since the roofline never reached down to it. The second correction, unrelated, put the roofline below the floor, so no tower was ever seen against the sky — only bright roofs with dark canyons beside them, which the eye reads as holes punched in a white plain rather than as buildings (it was reported as inverted normals, and it was not). `ROOFLINE_Y` therefore sits well above the play surface.

The island is a **cast-concrete drum with a dressed stone top and a moulded coping**, built by `Prazsky.Core.Render.IslandMesh` and assembled by `ArenaIsland`, which the Testbed and the Game both draw and the map editor deliberately does not (above). Its top is `ArenaIsland.TOP_Y` (−8.5) — a plain figure of the island's own now rather than something derived from the ground blocks that are long deleted, and the plane of the stone top's **outer arris**, from which the walkable top falls `ArenaIsland.DISH_DEPTH` (1.2) to the drain: the drain's rim, its gold bead and the pit's mouth all share the lower plane `TOP_Y − DISH_DEPTH`, and the collision floor runs between the two by construction (before the dish all four were flush at `TOP_Y`, which is what the Testbed's old `FUNNEL_TOP_Y`/`PIT_TOP_Y` aliases were saying). The top runs from the funnel's rim (`ArenaIsland.FUNNEL_TOP_RADIUS`) out to `ArenaIsland.RADIUS` and the platform drops `ArenaIsland.EDGE_HEIGHT`. The old marble-and-glass panel mosaic, its four marble bands, its mullions and the recessed centre "bath" are all gone, and with them the whole square-plaza machinery (`ARENA_HALF_EXTENT`/`ARENA_PANEL_SIZE`/the panel loop). What replaced *that* was a plain extruded washer (`DiscMesh`, now deleted), and it read as exactly what it was — a cylinder with a hole in it, every edge a raw 90° cut.

- **The shaping is all downward, and that is forced.** The physics floor mirrors the walkable top exactly (its outer arris at the top plane), so anything raised above it would be a lip balls pass straight through; every moulding is therefore cut *below* the top face — the walkable top itself included, which is the dish falling `DISH_DEPTH` from its arris to the drain's mouth so a landed ball rolls in. Outward from the arris: a chamfer, the coping's wash, a bullnose turning down to the platform's widest point, the coping's vertical face, then a **drip** where it oversails the concrete. The drum is battered slightly inward, flares into a string course a third of the way down, and **splays into a plinth** whose foot returns to very nearly the coping's radius.
- **What makes an edge read here is the hemisphere ambient, not a shadow** — there are no drawn shadows in this scene. An upward-facing chamfer takes the sky and draws a bright ring; the underside of the coping's overhang takes the ground bounce and draws a dark one. That pair of lines around the rim is most of the difference between a platform and an extruded circle, and it costs nothing.
- **The plinth flares rather than tapering, for two reasons.** A base that widens carries weight; one that narrows reads as balanced on a point. And the foot's radius is what covers the hole the five solid-terrain scenes cut out of the ground (`TerrainHoleRadius`): the island floats half a unit over that ground, so a foot drawn in much narrower than the platform opens a slot at every grazing angle and the sky shows through it as a pale ring. An earlier profile chamfered in to `r - 1.15` and did exactly that.
- **The physics ring stops at `ArenaIsland.FLOOR_RADIUS`, not at `ArenaIsland.RADIUS`** — `IslandMesh.FloorRadius` of it, the dish's own outer arris rather than the edge of the platform. Carried out to the widest point it would hold a ball up on air over the coping's wash.
- **Two meshes because it is two materials**, split at the coping's drip and built from one polyline so they cannot drift apart: the stone cap (coursed slab joints, the sky reflection turned down so it is matte) and the concrete drum (no joints — it is cast, not laid — a coarser relief and barely any reflection). `Prazsky.Core.Render.LatheMesh` is the general machinery: a surface of revolution from a cross-section polyline, per-point creases, and an optional radial irregularity (a function of angle and *world height*, so two lathes sharing an edge are displaced identically along it) that keeps the drum from reading as a machined part. Its triangles are wound clockwise from outside, so unlike `DiscMesh` the island now takes ordinary back-face culling — which is what would *show* a winding mistake rather than hide one.

Three things it cost to get right, all worth knowing before touching any other stone surface:

- **`Ground_8.png` does not tile, and never did.** The stone covers only the left ~1073 columns of its 2048² canvas; the rest is pure black. The triplanar projection therefore multiplied roughly half of every surface it was mapped onto by zero — which is what left the island's wall reading as a black band, at *any* detail scale, since no scale avoids the black. Both members now use `Prazsky.Core.Render.SurfaceTexture` instead: procedural concrete and stone generated at load on a noise lattice that wraps at the texture edge, so they tile by construction, with a linear-space mip chain. No content-project change in either executable, and no asset to get wrong.
- **That broken texture was silently acting as an exposure control.** Same vantage, same nominal albedo, only the texture swapped: the top face measured 151 grey with the photograph and 181 with a texture whose mean is 1 by construction — half a stop brighter for a number nobody changed. **Any albedo carried over from a surface that used it arrives overexposed**; the island's came down accordingly. `SurfaceTexture.LinearMean` exists so `DetailBoost = 1 / LinearMean` makes the texture a pure modulation about 1, leaving the material colour the honest albedo and `DetailStrength` a pure contrast dial.
- **A neutral vertical surface in this world reads distinctly warm, and that is the light rig, not the material.** The key light is carried halfway to the horizon colour (`SkyLightRig.SKY_TINT_STRENGTH`) and half of the hemisphere ambient a vertical face sees is the ground bounce, which is that horizon again. Measured in one frame: the cannon's steel 89,57,35 and the island's concrete 82,51,31, with the city's facades in the same family. A first pass read this as a fault in the concrete and pushed its albedo blue to cancel it — which would have made the platform the one object in the scene not taking the light everything else does. The albedo is a plain concrete grey, within a hair of `CannonRig.STEEL_COLOR`. Two more dials that misled on the way: the relief is a **sum of sines**, and past about 0.02 it stops reading as a rough surface and starts reading as the waves it is made of (a regular diagonal weave across the whole drum, at 0.045); and because the **specular ambient is not multiplied by albedo**, it and not the material colour is the dial that decides how *polished* a face reads — halving the stone's colour barely dimmed a top face whose brightness was mostly that term.

**The arena is on screen for every second of every level, in all fifteen scenes, and until #151 nobody had measured it.** That is the whole reason the issue exists, and it holds whatever the number turns out to be: the island is the one piece of geometry the game is never without, and it had never been in a quality tier for the same reason it was never measured — it is not a scene. **Two method notes from that first round, both paid for and both still standing**: a camera must be screenshotted before a number taken through it is trusted (#151 was filed against `Sea.fx` on a view that turned out to be mostly island), and the sea's four-octave chop and 2000 spray billboards both measure at **zero**, the same not-ALU-bound answer #104 and the tier sweeps got.

**The figure this issue was opened on — roughly 27 ms of a 42 ms frame on the weakest machine — has now been measured there, and it does not hold: the arena is 10–14 % of that frame, not 64 %.** The original was a **residual, not a measurement**: 42.4 ms in the sea view, less the sea's own pixel shader (9.2, measured) and a 6.9 ms control frame, with everything left over called "the arena". Two things were wrong with the subtraction. The remainder also holds the gun, the ceiling plate, the crosshair, every draw call's own overhead and the terrain the island is standing on — and the 6.9 ms control was **not a sky-only frame at all**. `campos=0,0,0 camtarget=0,80,0` looks straight along the up vector, so `Matrix.CreateLookAt` degenerates and *nothing is drawn*: two different scenes through it produce byte-identical flat frames of the clear colour, while a camera tilted 14° off vertical from the same point draws the dome and its clouds normally and costs 30.2 ms against the degenerate frame's 7.8. What that control bounded is the post chain over an empty frame — which is a legitimate bound on the post chain, since it is full-screen and scene-independent, but it is not what the label says and it is not a bound on the frame's fixed costs.

**What it actually is, measured member by member on the machine the ratio was opened about** (Ryzen 7 5700U + integrated Radeon), Testbed, meadow, dome 13, `nocap`, windowed **1600×900 at ssaa 2**, the arena drawn nine ways *inside one process* (`alt=`, below), two runs with the cycle order reversed, median of the per-cycle differences:

| *the frame itself* | *24.69 / 24.45 ms* | *26.53 / 26.01 ms* |
| **the whole arena** | **2.664 / 2.544 — 10.8 % / 10.4 %** | **3.776 / 3.685 — 14.2 % / 14.2 %** |
| the stone cap, shaded flat | 1.479 / 1.197 | 3.365 / 2.754 |
| the stone cap, not drawn | 1.514 / 1.352 | 2.949 / 2.555 |
| the glass | 0.470 / 0.324 | 0.362 / −0.447 |
| the pit | 0.354 / 0.353 | 0.362 / 0.114 |
| the concrete drum | 0.119 / 0.030 | 0.499 / 0.214 |
| the gold rims | 0.060 / 0.000 | 0.000 / −0.377 |

- **The cap is the arena here too** — about 80 % of it at the play camera — and every other member is at or under the noise, which is what says the class of machine does not change the *attribution* even though it changes every absolute. That is worth stating explicitly, because the benchmark skill's standing rule is that attribution does **not** travel between the desktop and the APU (#102 measured a cavern cut at nothing on the 6900 XT that #250 then measured at 20.7 → 17.9 ms here). This is a case where it happens to; it was checked rather than assumed.
- **The sign, not the size, is what separates signal from noise on this machine.** Each cycle gives one reading of every variant a second apart, so the per-cycle *differences* are what get medianed. Taking the whole arena out is cheaper in **96–100 %** of cycles; the drum and the rims land at **42–67 %**, i.e. a coin toss, which is what "measures at nothing" looks like when it is written down honestly.
- **Shading the cap flat and not drawing it at all come out within noise of each other, and at the play camera the flat one is *dearer*** — the same inversion the reference desktop measured, for the same reason: the cap occludes the terrain behind it, so removing it hands those pixels to the scene. The flat-colour probe stays the honest bound.
- **In the sea, at the top-down camera, the arena did not separate from noise at all** (0.193 ms, cheaper in 62 % of cycles, in a run whose own interquartile spread was ±2 ms). Removing the island there uncovers the sea, and the sea costs about what the island cost. So "what those three expensive views have in common is the arena" is not what the isolation says either — in the one scene the issue was originally filed against, the arena is nearly free *at the margin*.

**The instrument that made any of this measurable is `alt=`, and the machine is why it had to exist.** On the reference desktop #151 compared whole runs and its paired baselines landed within 0.15 ms. This laptop cannot do that: an integrated Radeon shares one 15 W package budget with the CPU, so its sustained clock moves with whatever else the box is doing, and two 125-second runs of *one unchanged variant* came back **33.6 and 25.7 ms** apart — a spread wider than the arena being measured. Worse, the drift is not a scale factor: while a stray process was hammering the machine, every variant compressed towards the same number, so a sweep taken then reads as "nothing costs anything". `alt=<members>[/<probe>];…` (`TestOptions.Alternation`) redraws the arena a different way on **every `[fps]` window**, so the variants share one process, one clock and the same neighbours, and the line already names the members and the probe so each reading labels its own variant. It is the same step `capprobe=` took — one build drawn several ways rather than several builds measured against each other — carried one further to one *run* drawn several ways. **Two operational notes that came with it:** the contention was real and named (`find / -iname …` had been pinning a core for three and a half hours; killing it moved the same pin from 26.7 to 24.5 ms), and the `[fps]` line's arena field prints a flags enum, so it is **itself comma-separated** — a harness regex that stops at the first comma silently drops every multi-member run, which cost one whole sweep before the logs were re-read rather than re-run.

**The isolation is done now, and it names the stone cap.** Measured on the reference desktop (Ryzen 9 5900X + RX 6900 XT), Testbed, meadow, dome 13, `nocap`, windowed 1600×900 at **ssaa 4** — 23.0 Mpix, within 7 % of what fullscreen 3840×1600 at ssaa 2 shades, so it is that machine's real `High` load in a small window — two passes a variant, each member taken out of an otherwise complete frame in turn (`arena=all,-cap` and so on, below):

| taken out | top-down, `campos=0,30,0 camtarget=0,-13,6` | play view, `campos=0,-4,30 camtarget=0,-8,0` |
|---|---|---|
| the stone cap | **0.698 ms** | **1.091 ms** |
| the glass | 0.129 | −0.140 |
| the pit | 0.099 | 0.005 |
| the concrete drum | 0.038 | 0.004 |
| the gold rims | −0.007 | −0.016 |
| *the whole arena* | *0.989 of 8.25* | *1.240 of 7.74* |

**The cap is the arena** — 71 % of it from above, 88 % from the view a player actually has — and every other member lands at or under the run-to-run noise, the glass coming out slightly *negative* in one camera. So #151's own first suspect is refuted: the translucent `CullNone` drain over the opaque `CullNone` pit, right where the camera looks, costs nothing worth naming. The overdraw is real and is not what the frame is spent on. The drum's zero is a **coverage** result and not a shader one — neither camera the game ever uses sees much of a wall that faces outwards under the coping — so nothing here says the drum's shading is cheap, only that it is never asked for. What was left of the suspect list is the cap's own per-pixel work: `SurfaceReliefWorld`'s seven octaves feeding `PerturbNormalFromHeight`'s `ddx`/`ddy`, the triplanar detail's three taps and the procedural masonry joints, all on the one surface that fills the lower half of a play frame — and the last of those turned out not to be work the game had any use for.

**A quarter of the cap was drawing the castle that was deleted years ago.** `TriplanarPS` carried a construction-pattern block — coursed stone joints, sawn-timber board seams, and `WoodGrain`, a *second* seven-octave `SurfaceReliefWorld` — selected between by `lerp`s on `isPatterned` and `isWood`. Both were **always zero**: nothing in the repository ever set `MasonryStrength` off its `0` default and nothing ever called `SetMeshSurfaceStyle`, so every term was computed per pixel and multiplied away, on every surface of every executable. It is the remains of the castle backdrop that went with the square plaza; `ForestScatterRenderer`'s own comment had been declining to declare a timber style for bark, against an API that could no longer do anything. The shader's comment claimed a branch "would save nothing anyway", and the measurement disagrees. All of it is deleted — the block, `MasonryStrength`, `SurfaceStyle`, `SetMeshSurfaceStyle` and `PixelFootprint`, which went with its only caller. `MortarDepth` survives as `CavityHeadroom`, at the same 0.055: the cavity range is the one place the patterns reached that outlived them, and every surface in the game has been shaded through that number since the joints stopped being drawn.

**Measured as a paired A/B of two whole builds run alternately**, six pairs, order swapped within each pair (see below on why that mattered): **−0.260 ms**, sd 0.036, 95 % CI −0.29 to −0.23, the new build faster in 6 pairs of 6. With `arena=none` — the same two builds, the arena simply out of the frame — the same comparison gives **+0.006 ms, CI −0.036 to +0.048, and the new build faster in only 2 pairs of 4**, i.e. nothing: **the whole saving is the stone cap**, which falls from 1.09 ms to about 0.83. The look is unchanged and that is not an argument from the code: two captures under `nopost`, diffed pixel by pixel over the 386 224 px of cap, differ in 76 — 60 of them the overlay's own frame counters, and **not one pixel outside it by more than a single LSB of one channel**, which is float reassociation and not shading.

**Two measurement notes, both paid for here.** Unpaired runs of the two builds in separate sessions put the unchanged side at 7.744 and 7.517 ms — a spread as large as the effect — because a session an hour into hammering the GPU drifts; alternating the builds inside one run is what made a 0.26 ms difference legible at all. And one pair had to be thrown out and *said so*: its reading of 21.3 ms began at 127 FPS, matching its neighbour exactly, then collapsed to 35 and stayed there — the benchmark skill's own trap 6, a stray process, visible only because the per-second lines were kept rather than the average.

It is **fill-bound**, confirmed rather than assumed: the whole arena costs 0.242 ms at ssaa 2 against 0.989 at ssaa 4, a 4.09× for 4× the pixels. And the 27-of-42-ms figure above stays the weak machine's own — it has deliberately **not** been extrapolated onto this one, where the arena is 1.24 ms of a 7.74 ms frame, i.e. 16 %. What makes re-running it there a few minutes' work rather than a rebuild is `ArenaIsland.Members` and the Testbed's `arena=` argument, which is the third method note: a suspect nothing can take out of the frame is not a suspect that can be tested, and every candidate in this list had stood unfalsifiable for exactly that reason.

**And the cap's own pixel shader is 13.6 % of a play frame, of which only the relief can be cut.** Following the isolation above — the cap is the arena, and a quarter of it was the deleted castle — the last suspect list was `SurfaceReliefWorld`'s seven octaves into `PerturbNormalFromHeight`'s `ddx`/`ddy` and the triplanar detail's three taps. Both are testable now for the reason `arena=` made the members testable: the cap draws through one of five cut-down copies of its own pixel shader on demand (`capprobe=N`, `InstancedModelRenderer.TriplanarProbe`), so this is **one build measured several ways** rather than several builds measured against each other. Reference desktop, Testbed, meadow, dome 13, `nopost`, the play camera `campos=0,-4,30 camtarget=0,-8,0`, windowed **1920×1080 at ssaa 4** — 33.2 Mpix, more than this machine's own fullscreen 3840×1600 at ssaa 2 — `fpscap=150`, four interleaved rounds with the order reversed on alternate rounds:

|---|---|---|
| the shipped `TriplanarPS` | 10.971 | — |
| three relief octaves instead of seven | 10.635 | **0.336** |
| the height field, without `PerturbNormalFromHeight` | 10.672 | 0.299 |

- **The three taps are the fourth suspect this issue has named and measured at nothing.** 0.029 ms for two of the three, 0.137 for all three — against 0.660 for the height field. The texture units are not what this pass is waiting on.
- **The whole pixel shader is 1.490 ms of a 10.971 ms frame**, and the relief is 0.660 of it. The remaining ~0.83 is `ShadePixel` — the light rig every surface in the game is shaded through — which is not this issue's to cut.
- **Taking the cap out of the frame is *cheaper* for the frame than shading it flat, and that is not a contradiction**: 1.204 against 1.490, because the cap occludes the terrain behind it and removing it hands those pixels to the scene instead. So `arena=all,-cap` **understates** the cap, and the flat-colour probe is the honest bound on it. The member sweep above is a lower bound on every member for the same reason.
- **The saving follows the shaded pixel count**, which is what says it survives down to the tier that will use it. The same variants at **2560×1440 ssaa 4** — 59.0 Mpix, 1.78× the pixels — read 19.841 ms shipped, 19.317 coarse and 17.534 flat: the coarse saving grows 0.336 → **0.524** and the flat bound 1.490 → **2.307**, i.e. 1.56× and 1.55× for 1.78× the pixels. `Medium` and `Low` shade at 1× rather than 4×, so what they collect was put at about a quarter of the figures above — **derived from that scaling and not measured**, because `fpscap=150` (this desktop's own condition, see the benchmark skill) puts every ssaa 1 and ssaa 2 frame of this camera against the ceiling, where nothing can be read.
- **Measured since, on the machine the tier exists for, that entry buys nothing anyone can detect.** Weak machine, meadow, the play camera, windowed 1600×900 at **ssaa 1** — which is what `Medium` and `Low` actually shade — the coarse field against the shipped one reads **0.00 to 0.05 ms of a 13 ms frame, cheaper in 45–60 % of cycles**: a coin toss. At ssaa 2 it is the same answer (−0.12 / −0.06 top-down at 54–71 %, and *dearer* by 0.2–0.3 at the play camera). This is not a contradiction of the desktop's 0.336 ms — it is that figure's own scaling carried honestly to the end: 1600×900 at 1× shades 1.44 Mpix against the desktop pin's 33.2, and 0.336 ms scaled by that is hundredths of a millisecond. **What is wrong is the "about a quarter", which was the one step taken by arithmetic rather than by measurement**, and the tier's own cap is smaller still: the cap's *entire* pixel shader is 0.21–0.46 ms of that 13 ms frame, and the relief is a fraction of that. The entry costs a shipped frame nothing and it does pay at higher pixel counts, so it stays — but nothing here should be quoted as a saving this class of machine collects.
- **Dropping the height field entirely cannot ship, and the picture says so rather than taste.** `SlabGroove` is part of the same field, so the cap loses its coursed slab joints with it — the only structure the dressed stone has at a scale the eye can see. Three octaves keep the joints and give up the finest grain: diffed over 106 600 px of stone under `nopost`, the coarse field moves a pixel by **2.9 of 255 on average** (ssaa 1; 3.4 at ssaa 2), against **10.5** for no field at all.

**So the arena finally has a quality-tier entry, and it is the first thing in the tier that is not a scene.** `ArenaIsland.SurfaceDetail` — 1 the authored look, below it the coarse field — wired from the Game's tier exactly as `SceneRenderer.SceneDetail` is, and reduced at **`Low` alone** since #298 (it was everything below `High`, which is what left nothing between the two lower rungs; handing it back to `Medium` is safe because it measures 0.00-0.05 ms there on the weak machine). The cap alone and deliberately not the concrete drum: the drum is triplanar too and its relief is most of what makes the two read as different materials at a distance, but it measures 0.004 ms because neither camera the game uses sees much of a wall that faces outwards under the coping — reducing it would trade a look for nothing. A second compiled technique (`InstancedModelTriplanarCoarse`) rather than a uniform, for the reason #155 measured and the glass bubble restates: a runtime branch over an alternative shading path costs the union of both register allocations in every wavefront of an occupancy-bound pass.

**Two things this round cost, both worth keeping.** A run whose window is **maximized** renders at the panel's size instead of the pinned one and reads as a 3× frame cost, with nothing on the `[fps]` line to say so except the size it names for exactly this purpose — it happened twice and it is why the harness now throws away any run whose back buffer is not the one asked for. And the same harness's per-second lines caught a run that opened at 91.8 FPS, matching its neighbours exactly, then collapsed to 31.5 and stayed there; a **median** of the per-second readings rather than a mean is what stops one of those from moving an answer, and the collapse is still visible in the low reading beside it.

Note `ReliefShadowStrength` and `ParallaxScale` were dropped from both renderers rather than carried over: the triplanar path builds its own height field and never runs the self-shadow or parallax marches, so both were dead where they were being set. Measured on `Dense20x10x15` in the city, uncapped: 188.2 FPS before the rework and 189.8 after — one extra draw call and a couple of thousand triangles against a 3000-ball scene.

## The drain funnel (Testbed)

Where the recessed square used to be, the centre of the arena is a **glass funnel** the shot balls fall into: they run down the cone to a hole at the bottom and drop through it, below the platform, into the drop — where the kill plane removes them. It is what replaced the flat bath that just let balls pile up.

- **Rendering** is `Prazsky.Core.Render.FunnelMesh` — a single-sided truncated-cone wall (a round rim flush with the stone dish's low inner lip, `ArenaIsland.TOP_Y − DISH_DEPTH`, down to a small hole at `ArenaIsland.FUNNEL_BOTTOM_Y`, which the dish takes nothing off) with inward-and-up normals, and **no collar** now (`squareHalf` 0): the round stone dish meets the round rim directly, so there are no square corners to fill. The rim's height below the stone's arris is `ArenaIsland.DISH_DEPTH`, the one figure the dish added — the drain's mouth, the pit's mouth and the dish's lip stay one plane by construction, carried by the drain family's own world matrix (`_drainWorld`), while the island's mesh keeps the arris plane (`TOP_Y`). It is drawn translucent through its own `InstancedModelRenderer` at `ArenaIsland.FUNNEL_GLASS_ALPHA` (~0.55, more opaque than plain glass, so the funnel reads clearly as a frosted-glass drain). Because it is one open surface it is drawn with `RasterizerState.CullNone` so it reads both looking down into it and up through the hole, and it is one of the four renderers `ArenaIsland` hands the caller's sky-lighting pass (`SkyLitRenderers`/`AppendSkyLitTo`) so its sheen takes the dome. **Its back faces flip their shading normal** (`TwoSidedNormals`, an `InstancedModelRenderer` property mirrored as a `MainPS` uniform, #291): a one-sided wall drawn `CullNone` used to shade its *outside* with the inside's normal, and under the grazing-angle sky sheen the cone read from below — the open-below scenes, and the very shot the drop cinematic's dive films — as a bright, nearly opaque milky sheet instead of the frosted glass it is from above. The uniform is 0 for every other renderer (a closed mesh never shows a back face worth shading), set unconditionally per draw like `Metalness`, and the pit sets it too.
- The rim (radius `ArenaIsland.FUNNEL_TOP_RADIUS`, 14) meets the stone dish's inner lip exactly, and the gold top-rim bead rings that stone-meets-glass junction — `DISH_DEPTH` below the stone's arris now, sunk at the centre of the dish — so the drain reads at a glance where the two materials meet. (The old square-collar machinery that filled the corner gaps between the round funnel and the square pit is gone with the square plaza.)
- **Gold rims, and they lie flat on the surface rather than standing on it (#94).** A polished-gold band runs around **both circles** — the wide top rim and the small bottom hole — which is what makes the glass drain read at a glance (`FunnelRimsMesh`: two bands in one mesh, at the funnel's own local y = 0 and y = -height, so they share the drain family's world matrix — `TOP_Y − DISH_DEPTH`, the glass and the pit under the same one; one gold `InstancedModelRenderer` draws them). Each was a **torus** until #94, a round bead revolved around its own tube as well as around the ring, and the shape was wrong for where it sits: it stood a half-unit proud at the exact junction every released ball is *guaranteed* to cross on its way into the drain, and nothing underneath it agreed — `FunnelPhysics.Build` collides the smooth cone and the smooth dished stone and says so itself, so the bead was a lip a ball rolled straight through. Each band now lies **on the surface that actually meets it there**: the top one on the stone dish, tilted to `ArenaIsland.DISH_GRADE` (derived from `DISH_DEPTH` over the dish's run, so it cannot drift from `FloorHeightAt`), the bottom one on the glass cone at the cone's own grade, which `FunnelRimsMesh` works out from the three figures that already describe it — which is why only the dish's grade is a constructor argument. Each is a **shallow crown with buried edges** since #109: its two edges sink `EDGE_SINK` (0.04) *into* the surfaces they meet and its mid-width rises `SURFACE_LIFT` (0.05, a tenth of a ball radius) above them. The lift exists because coplanar geometry z-fights across the ring's whole width rather than at a fringe — but a whole band *floating* at that lift, which is what #94 first shipped, showed a sliver of the surface under its inner edge from the shallow angle the play camera actually has: a stone/shadow strip between the gold and the glass, at the very junction the gold exists to mark (#109's report). A buried edge cannot be seen under from any angle, the crown keeps the lift so nothing is coplanar, and each ring's normal is taken from the cross-section's own polyline (so a band's shading cannot disagree with the shape it was given, which matters now that the top band turns a crease mid-run) — which incidentally gives back a little of the glint the tori had, the inner and outer halves catching the light differently. **⚠ At the MOUTH that fix bought the very strip it was buying off (#237), and the arithmetic says why.** Burying an edge means the surface is drawn in FRONT of the gold there, so the gold's visible boundary moves inward from the edge to `halfWidth × EDGE_SINK / (EDGE_SINK + SURFACE_LIFT)` — **0.22 units of bare stone** ringing the mouth between the glass and the gold. #109 saw a stone strip under a floating edge and buried the edge; burying it drew a stone strip *over* the edge instead. The two states look the same and neither is the answer: **the stone has to be covered, not hidden behind.** So the top band now **wraps the lip** — past its mouth ring it turns the crease and runs `COLLAR_DROP` (0.35) on down the glass cone, ending sunk into the *glass* rather than into the stone. There is no longer a radius at the mouth where stone is the front-most thing, and the collar's own buried edge sits inside the throat under translucent glass, which shows it as a soft gold under-lip instead of hiding it — the trim piece #237 asked for in as many words. The mouth ring itself is neither buried nor flush but stands `LIP_LIFT` (0.012) off the stone, because the lip is the crease where the dish meets the cone and a ring sitting exactly on it is coplanar with both. **The mechanism was measured before anything was built:** zeroing `EDGE_SINK` made the strip vanish outright, which is what named it. Widths carry the tori's read across (`FUNNEL_RIM_TOP_WIDTH` 1.0 = the old top bead's diameter, `FUNNEL_RIM_HOLE_WIDTH` 0.6), and it is still far less geometry — a few rings of quads against two double-revolved tori. The bead's full roundness stays given up, and rightly — the colour and the metalness are what make the drain legible, not the cross-section. They are **opaque**, so they draw with the opaque scene *before* the glass, and `CullNone` — which is now load-bearing rather than merely convenient: a zero-thickness annulus has one face, and culling off is what lets the top band read from under the island as well as from above it. `ArenaIsland.DrawGlass` puts both draws inside **one** `CullNone` sandwich, restored to `CullCounterClockwise` after, which is image-identical to the Testbed's two separate pairs because the two draws want culling off for different reasons and one setting satisfies both. They are drawn **metallic**, which needed a shader addition: `InstancedModel.fx` now has a `Metalness` uniform (a renderer property, set unconditionally every `Draw` like `SpecularAmbientStrength`, **0 for every other surface** so nothing else changes) that makes the environment-reflection F0 the material's specular color itself rather than the 4 % dielectric `DielectricF0 * tint` — so gold reflects the sky *in gold*. The gold specular (`ArenaIsland.FUNNEL_RIM_SPECULAR`) is passed as a per-draw `BasicEffectParams` override; the gold diffuse (`ArenaIsland.FUNNEL_RIM_COLOR`) keeps the bead visible even under a dark dome. The rims are visual only — no physics; the stone dish and the cone are the floor, which since #94 is what their geometry says too.
- **Physics** is a Bepu triangle-`Mesh` (`Prazsky.BS3D.Physics.FunnelPhysics.Build`, one copy for both executables since #75) — the island's whole physics floor now that the wide grid of ground blocks is gone: the sloped cone wall plus the **dished stone ring** from the rim up to `ArenaIsland.FLOOR_RADIUS`, all **double-sided** — eight triangles a segment, every quad's two added in both windings — deliberately, so a ball collides with the surface regardless of Bepu's one-sided-triangle normal convention rather than depending on getting the winding right. It is handed the very figures `ArenaIsland` draws from, `DISH_DEPTH` included, so the drawn surface and the collided one cannot drift. A ball that lands on the ring **rolls down its dish into the glass** — Bepu spheres carry no rolling resistance (friction resists sliding, not rolling), so the ~6.4° grade is plenty — then within the rim it runs down the cone to the hole (wider than a ball) and falls through; past the ring it drops off the island's edge into the drop and the kill plane takes it. The dish is what made the drain work for big maps at all: on the flat ring a ball parked where it stopped, and the wider the field the larger the share of released balls that never reached the mouth's radius 14. `KILL_PLANE_Y` sits below `ArenaIsland.FUNNEL_BOTTOM_Y` so a ball that drops through falls a visible distance into the drop before `RemoveFallenBalls` culls it. Steep walls (~55°) keep balls running down to the hole rather than resting (a ball that did come to rest would be sleep-culled, i.e. vanish where it sits). Dumping a very large cluster all at once (the `End` debug release of a full map) briefly floods the funnel and spikes the physics until it drains; ordinary matched clusters are small and drain smoothly.
- **The terrain hole and the dark pit shaft (solid-ground scenes).** In the city there is nothing built below the arena to drain into any more (#275 — see "The city and the glass arena"), and in the sea the funnel drains into the water; but the **solid-terrain** scenes (mountains, meadow, savanna, desert, forest) are a flat clearing at the island's foot, and the flat ground plane sliced across the glass funnel just below its rim — the drain read as a shallow bowl and balls falling through vanished into the ground. The fix is in two halves. First, the terrain shaders `clip()` the island's footprint out of the ground (`IslandHoleRadius`, set from `SceneRenderer.TerrainHoleRadius` = `ArenaIsland.TERRAIN_HOLE_RADIUS`, a couple of units inside the disc's rim so the terrain tucks under the stone edge with no gap; **0 in the map editor**, which draws no island, so its terrain stays whole under the field AABB — and making an island available to a host must not hand the editor one as a side effect). But a hole alone made it worse — the ~55 %-opaque glass then showed the bright sky-haze straight through it, reading as a glass ring on the ground. So second, a **dark pit shaft** (`ArenaIsland`'s own mesh and renderer, drawn by `DrawPit` in the five `SceneRenderer.IsSolidTerrainScene`s only, opaque and `CullNone` before the glass, which composites over it) backs the glass: a near-black `FunnelMesh` that **hugs** the glass funnel — since #291 *literally*: a two-band polyline sharing the funnel's mouth (`ArenaIsland.FUNNEL_TOP_RADIUS`), running `PIT_SHEATH_CLEARANCE` (0.35) outside the glass down to just past its hole, then a narrow throat on down past the kill plane — so a ball runs down the glass into darkness and is culled inside the throat rather than against the bright sky. A wide cone would hide behind the disc's stone ring and leave the bright hole showing through the narrow (radius 14) aperture — the pit **must** stay just outside the funnel at every depth, and the sheath's gap grows linearly 0 → 0.35 from mouth to hole, outside by construction. **⚠ It was one straight cone from the mouth to `PIT_BOTTOM_Y` (−46) until #291**, which — being 18.5 units deeper than the glass on the same mouth — sat *wider* than the glass at every depth (at 18 down the glass has closed to ~1.6 and that cone was still ~7.7) and read, from any vantage under a solid scene's terrain (the free camera, the editor, a terrain dip), as a long dark spike nothing inside the drain accounts for: the owner's "outer shell doesn't match the inside", reproduced and photographed in every solid scene before the fix. Sheathed, the outside reads as the bowl's own underside with a tidy drain pipe below it — the same object as the view down into it, in every map. It is near-matte (`SpecularAmbientStrength` 0.03) so no dome bleaches the well, and the gold beads hide the mouth ring and the knee it shares with the funnel. Purely visual — no physics; the funnel mesh is still the only floor, and `FunnelPhysics.Build` takes the glass's own figures, which did not move.

Windows are not lit once and for all: each keeps its own rhythm off `CityWindowTime` (wall clock, like the balls' pulse — a city's lamps do not stop when the simulation is paused), holding a state for an interval of its own length and then deciding again, with a short fade rather than a cut. `CITY_WINDOW_BRIGHTNESS` stays **under** `GLARE_THRESHOLD`: a window that glares veils the tower behind it, and a thousand of them turn the skyline into one white haze.

Everything about the buildings is procedural and there are no assets. One 1×1×1 `BoxMesh` serves every building — each is that cube under a different instance matrix, so the whole city is a single instanced draw call (the arena floor is no longer part of it; that is the separate `IslandMesh` platform, above) — one call, but fed a frustum-culled array ordered near to far rather than the generator's own, for the reasons in "Drawing the city near to far" in `docs/rendering.md`. Non-uniform scale is safe here because the boxes are axis-aligned in object space: a diagonal scale maps each face normal onto itself, so `normalize` in the pixel shader recovers it without an inverse transpose. The `InstancedCity` technique lays the window grid out **relative to each building**, not on a raw world grid. A raw world grid (what it used to do) put the pattern at a random phase against every tower's edges, so windows landed jammed into corners or clipped — which reads as unreal. Now a dedicated `CityVS` hands the pixel shader the pixel's offset from the building's centre and the building's world size (the box is the 1×1×1 unit cube, so a transformed unit axis is the world size and the transform of the object origin is the centre; all Bone-agnostic), and `CityPS` holds a fixed wall margin (`WindowMargin`, world units) clear of glass at **every** edge and spreads the windows evenly across the interior — so no window touches a corner and every tower carries the same border. The floor/column **count** is still solved from the building's world size against the target pitch (`WindowPitchX/Y`), so a hundred-storey tower still gets more floors than a low one and a wide one more columns; only the phase is now the building's rather than the world's. The per-building cell pitch is the target pitch nudged so a whole number of windows fills the interior exactly (kept near the target, so it still reads natural), and the `buildingId` — for the neon per-tower hue and the lit-window pattern — is taken from the tower's own centre so it is one value across the whole facade. Windows band-limit to their own average coverage against that per-building pitch, so a distant tower is a dim glowing block rather than a moire of floors.

Two things learned tuning it, both worth keeping in mind elsewhere. The **specular ambient is not multiplied by albedo** (a reflection does not care how dark the surface under it is), which is physically right but washes a whole skyline in sky color at grazing angles. Almost every facade of a city seen from inside it, and almost every square metre of a floor beyond your own feet, is at a grazing angle where Fresnel is near 1 — so the city runs the term at 0.07 and the marble floor at 0.4, and both were bleached white before that. And a translucent sheet over a lit city reads as *no floor at all*; the mullions dividing the glass into panels are what make the eye accept there is glass there.

## The shared scene backdrops

### The wind, in one copy (#276)

Three ground scenes show the wind — the meadow, the savanna and the forest — and until #276 each carried its own copy of the same two lines and the same two faults, character for character. The owner reported it from the Testbed as ground that "visibly crawls and does not read as anything in particular".

- **The shading was an infinite plane wave.** `sin(dot(xz, WindDirection) * 0.15 + t * 1.4)`: dead straight, perfectly regular, and at frequency 0.15 its wavelength is 2π/0.15 ≈ **42 world units — wider than the visible field**, so the whole ground brightened and darkened under one straight edge sweeping across it. Nothing outdoors is that regular, which is why it read as an artefact rather than as weather. It is `Noise.fxh`'s **`WindGust`** now: one octave of combed noise, advected downwind, giving irregular patches drawn out along the wind.
- **And the ground's own fine texture translated.** All three sampled their relief at `xz + WindDirection * time * 0.7` — a flat 0.7 world units a second for ever, which at `GrassReliefFrequency` 2 slides the blades' texture across the ground they are rooted in at 1.4 features a second. Measured on two frames 0.6 s apart from a fixed camera, the near field's **mean absolute change was 10.3 of 255 on the meadow and 16.0 on the desert** — essentially every pixel. Grass **leans** by the gust field now (bounded to about an eighth of a feature, so it rocks and springs back); the forest floor, which is litter rather than blades, simply does not move at all. After: meadow **2.7** — the gust and the clouds, which is motion that belongs — and desert **0.26**.
- **⚠ One octave, and that is a measured ceiling.** At two the gust fell off an occupancy cliff in the two dearest shaders: on the reference desktop (6900XT, Testbed, fixed camera, 1600×900 at ssaa 4, `fpscap=400`, median of 17 readings) the savanna went **8.496 → 27.933 ms** and the forest **12.788 → 28.011**, while the meadow — the same addition in a cheaper shader — went 7.746 → 8.097 and merely began dipping to 28 now and then. Back at one octave all three come home: meadow **7.949**, savanna **8.547**, forest **12.953**, i.e. **0.05–0.20 ms** for the whole effect. It is the wall `Forest.fx` documents for its own two extras ("cutting either one ALONE saves NOTHING") read from the other side — these passes are occupancy-bound, so cost steps when the shader crosses a register threshold rather than scaling with work. **Anything added to `WindGust` has to be re-measured on the savanna and the forest, never on the meadow.**

The settings are **shared between the game and the map editor** so a map looks in the editor the way it will play. Fifteen `SceneKind`s exist, but only the first seven are on the cycle the Testbed's NumPad2 and the editor's V walk (`SceneRenderer.CycleLength`, deliberately **not** `SceneCount` — the cycle stays on the seven scenes a map is authored against, and both executables wrote the 7 as a bare literal until #75): the forest, space, the dream, the cavern, the Moon, the outback, the tropical beach and the volcano are past its end and only the Game selects them (plus `scene=forest`/`scene=space`/`scene=dream`/`scene=cavern`/`scene=moon`/`scene=outback`/`scene=tropical`/`scene=volcano` on either command line). The split:

- The self-lit backdrops — sea, savanna (with its acacias and birds), desert and the outback (with the same flock of birds), mountains (with its snow), meadow, the forest's floor and hills (its scatter of trees, rocks and stumps is `ForestScatterRenderer`'s beside them since #75, so all three draw the wood too), the tropical beach (with its palms, its waterline's mossy rocks and the same flock again), the volcano (with its lava fountains, its smoke plume and its ash) and **space** — live in `Prazsky.Core.Render.SceneRenderer`, which owns their effects, meshes, particle buffers and every tuning constant. Each is a dedicated shader that computes its own lighting from a `SceneFrame` handed over per frame (camera, sun direction, the sky palette in linear radiance, the sun's tinted radiance, the wall-clock time, and an optional cloud-apply hook). `DrawEnvironment` draws the terrain/water (plus the savanna's acacias, the savanna's, desert's, outback's and tropical's birds); `DrawOverlays` draws the foreground weather last — the mountain's snow, the sea's spray, the savanna's campfire flame, the volcano's ash. The Testbed passes `_clouds.ApplyTo` as the hook; the editor passes null, so its scenes get full sun with no cloud shadow (the cloud uniforms stay zero and `CloudSunlight` returns 1). Both build the scene shaders out of the one Testbed content directory. **Space is the odd one in this list** — it replaces the sky rather than the ground (`SceneRenderer.ReplacesSky`, which the dream, the cavern and the Moon share), so it is one full-screen pass with no grid, no cloud hook and no dome behind it; the four sky-replacing draws are the ones here that touch the depth state (the Moon its own way — it writes depth with its terrain and reads it under its sky, where the other three draw depth-off), and each states its own light rig (`TryGetLightRig`) instead of taking the dome's. See "Space (Game)" and "The Moon (Game)".
- The **city** (and its neon relight) is `Prazsky.Core.Render.City` — a seeded generator of one instanced box per building — drawn through the shared `InstancedModel` city technique by an `InstancedModelRenderer` each executable owns and sky-lights like the balls (`CityWindowBrightness > 0` selects the technique; `CityNeon` flips it to neon). The arena floor (marble/glass panels) is **not** shared: it is tied to the physics ground blocks and stays in `Testbed.cs`, which is why the editor draws no platform and marks the field with the AABB instead.

So a change to any scene reaches both executables at once, exactly as the shared shaders do. The per-scene sections below describe what each one is doing; the tuning dials named in them now live on `SceneRenderer` (or `City`) rather than at the top of `Testbed.cs`.

### The scenes' observation points (#289)

Each `SceneKind` names **somewhere in itself worth looking at, and how a camera should stand to frame it** — `SceneRenderer.TryGetViewpoint`, which joins `ReplacesSky` / `IsSolidTerrainScene` / `OpenBelow` as the per-scene questions answered in one place rather than in each caller. The one asker today is the Game's chapter intro (`docs/game-feedback.md`, "The chapter intro"), whose opening legs used to look across the island's far rim on a rolled bearing whatever the backdrop was: a fair shot of a sea, and a poor one of a volcano, whose cone was as likely to be behind the camera as in front of it.

**A `SceneViewpoint` is a point plus a recipe, and the split is the whole design.** *Where* the interesting thing is, is the scene's own business and nothing else can know it — the volcano's crater moves with its cone config, the savanna's fire with its own, and the map editor's live panel moves both while somebody watches, so every figure below is read off the scene's **live config** rather than written down twice. *How far out and how high* a camera should stand is the camera's business and is the half that has to scale with the **level**: a tall cluster is played from further back and its establishing shot has to stand back with it, so the distance is a multiple of `GameCameraFit.Solve`'s own stand-off and never a world distance. The bearing offset is measured from the subject's own bearing: **0** puts the camera between the arena and the subject looking outward (pure scenery, the island out of frame), **180** puts the subject beyond the arena so the island and its cluster stand in front of it.

**Half the scenes have a landmark and half do not, and "no landmark" is not "no viewpoint".** A meadow is the same in every direction, but it still wants a *low, close* look at the flowers where the mountains want a *far, raised* one at the peaks — and getting that wrong is most of what made the old shot generic. Those scenes build their point out of the caller's own rolled bearing, so a meadow still comes in from anywhere while a savanna names its fire.

**Two of them were changed by the photograph rather than by the argument that chose them.** The cavern's first draft looked *up* at the roof, on the reasoning that a cavern's defining feature is overhead and the gameplay camera never looks there — and it came out very nearly black, because the ceiling is unlit rock a hundred units off and everything this scene has to show glows from below. The dream's first draft sat at 26 up and tilted the lens off the island entirely, so the frame was marbling and two orbs: a wallpaper rather than a place.

**Eleven of the seventeen are photographed; six are not, and cannot be yet.** The shipped level set names only eleven scenes (`grep '"scene"' Game/Levels/*.json`: space, savanna, neon, mountain, moon, meadow, dream, desert, city and cavern ten times each, volcano five), and a chapter intro only ever opens on a block's first level — so sea, forest, outback, tropical, Mars and storm have viewpoints stated from their own configs that no run of the game can currently show. Photograph them the moment a level names one.

## The sea (Testbed)

The second setting, reached with NumPad2 the way NumPad1 cycles the sky domes (or `scene=sea` on the command line, since a synthetic NumPad press never reaches the SDL window). Only the environment swaps: the round stone island stays as it is, now floating a little above open water instead of standing among city towers. It is `Testbed/Content/Shaders/Sea.fx` with its switch and draw plumbing in `Testbed.cs` (`SceneKind`, `SwitchScene`, `DrawSea`), and it borrows the scene's whole toolkit rather than inventing its own.

**One uniform in `Sea.fx` belongs to the other scene that draws through it**: `ShallowBias`, added by #268, lifts the floor of the body-colour mix so a *lagoon* — shallow across its whole basin — takes the pale colour wherever you look at it rather than only on the up-facing faces of its swell. The open sea sets it to **0** and `lerp(x, 1, 0)` is bit-exactly `x`, so nothing here moved; that was checked and not asserted, by regional means over 62 400 water pixels before and after (R 69.55 / G 88.77 / B 123.29 against R 69.30 / G 88.49 / B 123.08 — a third of a level, i.e. wave phase). A per-pixel diff cannot answer this question at all: the swell and the cloud deck both run off the wall clock, so two launches of the *same* build differ in 45 % of their pixels.

The water is **real geometry** now, not a flat quad: a camera-centred grid (the shared `CreateGridMesh`, the same machinery the desert, mountains and meadow use), snapped to a cell each frame so it does not swim, displaced by a sum of **Gerstner (trochoidal) waves** in the vertex shader — sharp crests, rounded troughs, the horizontal pinch that sells a wave over a plain height field. The analytic normal and the horizontal **Jacobian fold** (which drops below 1 as a crest pinches and negative where it overhangs — the whitecap generator) come free from the same per-vertex evaluation. The wavelengths are kept **short relative to the scene** (a ball is ~1 unit) so several crests fall inside the visible water and it reads as waves rather than one flat tilt — a 100-unit swell only ever shows a corner of one wave at this scale, which is why the first flat-quad version, whose whole "wave" was a normal-only slope, read as a low-res mirror. The mean level (`SEA_LEVEL_Y`) is dropped well below the island so the crests have headroom without poking up through it, and the waves **fade to flat** between `WAVE_FADE_START`/`WAVE_FADE_END` so the far sea settles into a clean hazed horizon rather than a jagged fringe. Everything runs off the wall clock, so the sea keeps moving while the simulation is paused, like the clouds and the balls' pulse.

On the wave geometry the pixel shader piles the rest. Fine **wind chop** — a few band-limited octaves tilting the normal through the ground relief's `PerturbNormalFromHeight`, scrolled downwind — breaks the surface so it is rough rather than a mirror between the crests. **Whitecap foam** takes its *permission* from two per-vertex signals — the Jacobian fold (the wave genuinely breaking) and a crest gate past `FoamCrestStart` — but its *shape* from neither, since #128: both are smooth interpolated scalars, and thresholding the crest height painted large round white blobs (where the six fanned waves constructively interfere the sum is a localized round bump, and a height threshold on a bump is a disc drifting at phase speed — exactly how it was reported). The gates now set only the foam **density**, and the visible silhouette comes from a streak field — band-limited fbm combed along the dominant swell's crest line (`Fbm2Combed`, the grass relief's idiom; the crest line is the perpendicular of `WAVE_DIR[0]`) — so foam reads as wind-torn lanes riding the crests, with more energy widening the lanes toward a connected cap rather than brightening a disc. `FoamCrestStart`'s default came down 0.72 → 0.6 with the same change: at 0.72 the gate only opened on the rare interference bumps (which is *why* the blobs were isolated), while the streak shaping makes a wider gate safe — more frequent lanes, never bigger discs. Lit as a matte cap by sun and sky, as ever. **Subsurface scattering** glows the crests when the sun is low behind them and the eye looks into the water. A Fresnel **sky reflection** samples the dome's two-color gradient in closed form (`SkyRadiance`'s trick, so the water takes the mood of whichever of the twenty skies is up), a sharp **sun glint** sparks where the reflected ray points at the sun, and the **cloud shadow** is the same one line the rest of the scene gets (`CloudSunlight` off `Clouds.fxh`), so the water darkens under the very cloud overhead. A horizon haze fades the finite grid into the skyline. Because the water mirrors the sky, its whole mood follows the dome — a bright sunny sky gives a bright, breezy sea, not a stormy one — so **the sea scene defaults to a darker dome** (`SEA_DEFAULT_SKY_DOME`, a violet/teal dusk) on entry, whether from `scene=sea` at startup or a NumPad2 switch into it, unless a command-line `sky=` overrode the startup default; NumPad1 still cycles domes freely from there (`SetSkyDome` is the shared runtime dome-change, used by both). The dials (`WAVE_*`, `CHOP_*`, `FOAM_*`, `SSS_*`, `WATER_COLOR_*`, `SEA_LEVEL_Y`) live on the shared `SceneRenderer`, so the map editor draws the same water. It is drawn opaque, first in the scene block where the city would be, `CullNone` (one open surface, read from above and through the crests), so the arena glass composites over it unchanged — but **depth-read rather than depth-write** since #131, for the reason the next paragraph gives. Shader Model 5.0, like every scene shader.

**A missed ball sinks into the water instead of being cut off by it** (#131). A ball that misses the island falls past its edge into the sea, and while the water both wrote depth and drew opaque it was gone in the single frame it crossed the surface — the surface plane depth-killed every pixel behind it, so the ball did not sink so much as get switched off. The fix is in two halves, and needs both. First the open water **gives up its depth write** (`DepthStencilState.DepthRead` in `DrawSea`, restored to `Default` after it) so the ball's own pixels run below the waterline at all; the island is drawn after the sea and is opaque, so it still writes and owns its own depth, and only the water gives it up. Second the ball shader fades what now survives: `InstancedModel.fx`'s `PatternPS` lerps the lit colour towards the deep-water tint and drops the alpha to nothing over `SEA_SUBMERGE_FADE` (**3** world units) below `SeaLevelY`, so it dims *into* dark water rather than lingering under it as a clear sphere. The tint is `SeaSceneConfig.WaterDeep` — the very colour the water itself shades with, so a ball converges on what is in front of it whichever dome is up, and a retuned sea takes the fade with it. The band is short on purpose: the mean level sits at −13 and the drain's `KILL_PLANE_Y` at −42, so the ball is off the screen from about −16 and the simulation drops it 26 units further down, long out of sight.

`SceneRenderer.ApplySeaSubmerge` pushes the three uniforms onto the shared instancing effect — the mirror of `CloudField.ApplyTo` for a sea-specific set the *ball* shader needs — and **all three hosts** call it once a frame in the scene block. Off the sea it pushes `SeaFadeDepth = 0` and returns, and the shader gates the whole block on that being positive: a **uniform** branch, so the frame takes one path with no divergence, per the per-frame render hygiene. (This page said the map editor never calls it; `MapEditor.cs:803` does, and has as long as the editor has drawn the sea — so the editor's flying camera meets the same fade, and the same fix below, as the two hosts.)

**The fade is RELEASED as the lens goes under, since #159 — and until it was, it took the drop cinematic's own subject off the screen.** What the effect is for is a ball seen from *above*, through the surface: the water column between the eye and the ball is what dims it. The sea is one of the scenes the cinematic can film from **below** (`OpenBelow`), and that shot deliberately swings the eye well under the mean level to follow a released cluster down the drain — where the murk owns the frame and dissolving individual objects inside it as well is one water effect too many. So the fade is multiplied by `1 − SeaLensSubmerged`, and that uniform is **the very number the tonemap gets for its murk**: `SceneRenderer.LensSubmergedAmount`, 0 above the water and 1 seven units under.

- **The handover is the whole of its correctness, and the first attempt got it wrong in a way worth recording.** That one released the fade over a 1.5-unit band measured from `EyePosition`, which put the release *above* the surface: a tenth of a unit over the water and the fade was already 93 % gone — and because `DrawSea` gives up its depth write, the whole submerged drain shaft then drew straight **through** the standing pool, which is the exact composite `MainPS`'s copy of this block was added to prevent (#132). The band also claimed the murk stood behind it when the murk is **7 %** in at that height (its ramp is 7 units, not 1.5), and it read nothing at all in the map editor, which pins the murk to zero. An adversarial verification pass found all three; the capture that settled the first was taken from `campos=0,-12.9,6`, one tenth of a unit above the mean level.
- **So one number answers "is the lens in the water", for both effects.** It lived twice before — once in each host, with a second copy of the 7-unit band — which was harmless while only the murk read it and became a hazard the moment the fade did too. It is `SceneRenderer`'s now (it owns `SeaLevelY`), both hosts call it for the murk *and* pass it to `ApplySeaSubmerge`, and the **map editor passes 0** — correct there precisely because it has no murk to hand over to, so its flying camera keeps the fade it always had.
- **Above the water nothing changed**, and the bound is exact rather than hoped: the release is **precisely zero for any lens more than half a unit over the mean level**, so `#131`'s sinking ball and `#132`'s pool compute what they always did. That covers every camera the game ships — the Game's play lens is pinned at **Y = −7.9** (`GameCameraFit`'s `LENS_FLOOR_Y`, which the dished stone can only lower the trunnions under), 5.1 units over the mean, whatever the level or the walk. Inside that last half unit the release follows the murk instead of stopping dead, and it should: the murk's gate opens there *by design* because the crests reach ±0.76, so a lens sitting at the mean level is as likely to be inside water as above it. At a tenth of a unit over the mean the fade is 6 % released — against the 93 % the refuted band gave at the same height, which is the difference between an allowance and a defect.
- **Both copies of the block are edited identically**, `MainPS`'s and `PatternPS`'s, and the release is folded into `submerge` rather than applied after it — the colour and the alpha have to keep taking the *same* figure, because this output is premultiplied and two fades that could disagree is exactly how a vanished surface starts *adding* its colour instead of leaving (the #132 mush, from the other direction).
- **Verified on static geometry, which is what made it cheap**: the drain's glass cone and its bottom gold band run through `MainPS`'s copy of the same fade, so no falling ball and no keystroke are needed. From `campos=0,-18,24 camtarget=0,-8,0` — five units under the surface — the funnel **before** the fix is cut off dead at the waterline, its whole cone down to −27.5 simply absent; after it, the cone is there. Which also says the defect was worse than #159 reported: from under water the cinematic lost not only the balls it was following but the drain it was following them into.
- **Two things it does not fix, both filed rather than smuggled in.** A ball watched from under water now stays drawn all the way to the kill plane at −42 and is culled in a single frame when it arrives, so the pop the surface fade used to hide is back — five other `OpenBelow` scenes have always shown it, so it wants fixing there rather than here. And for roughly the top quarter of the cinematic's elevation roll the eye is still *above* the water while the subject is metres under, where the fade correctly still hides it: that is the cinematic's own framing to answer, not the fade's.

**The drain holds a standing pool of the same water (#132).** The open-ocean wave field used to run straight through the funnel's solid geometry: from outside the island's silhouette the depth test hid it like anything else, but the funnel is a shape the camera sees *into*, and the animated swell showed through the glass. The first cut clipped the island's whole footprint out of the sea, which fixed the pass-through by leaving the drain bone dry — and the issue's own point is that the water must stay: the sea stands *in* the drain wherever the glass cone crosses the mean level. So the cut is an **annulus, not a disc**: `Sea.fx` keeps both the open sea past `IslandHoleRadius` and the pool inside `FunnelPoolRadius` (one `clip` on the better of the two signed distances), discarding only the ring hidden inside the island's stone. The pool radius is the cone's own at `SeaLevelY`, derived in `DrawSea` from `ArenaIsland`'s figures — the same straight span `FunnelMesh` is built from, so the water and the glass cannot drift — buried `POOL_WALL_BIAS` (0.15) into the glass so the funnel's 64-segment faceting can never open a sliver of sky between water and wall (the gold bands' buried-edge lesson, #109). Three things shape the water in there:

- **The swell dies before the glass, keyed on the rest position.** `CALM_BAND` (4, about one sea-grid cell) under the island's rim takes the Gerstner amplitude to zero, so the pool is *exactly* the flat rest grid at `SeaLevelY` and its clipped rim cannot breathe with the swell — every vertex a pool-edge triangle can touch is fully calm by construction. The last visible water at the island's foot inherits the tail of the ramp and laps rather than breaks, which reads as the island's shelter. Inside, a `POOL_CHOP` fraction (0.18) of the wind chop survives as fine capillary ripple; foam and the crest SSS are zeroed outright (the damped crest signal parks at 0.5, which a low `FoamCrestStart` config would otherwise read as foam over dead-still water), and the body colour is biased to the deep tint — a still column of water standing in a drain reads dark, and the pool's straight-up normal would otherwise pick the palest mix the shader has.
- **The water meets the glass in a capillary rim, not a razor cut.** Over the last `MENISCUS_BAND` (0.9) before the pool's edge the normal curls up the wall (`MENISCUS_TILT`), so the rim catches the sky at its own angle and draws the soft bright ring a meniscus actually shows, with the supersampling softening the cut edge itself.
- **What continues below the pool sinks into it.** The drain's glass cone and its bottom gold band draw *after* the water, which writes no depth (#131 above), so unfaded they would composite over the pool's surface from underneath it across the whole disc. `MainPS` therefore gives the plain-material path the balls' #131 submerge fade. The pool also exposed a blending fault in that fade as #131 shipped it: the output rides premultiplied alpha, so lowering the alpha while leaving the colour standing turns every faded pixel *additive*. One sinking ball hides it — the residue is the near-black tint, once — but the `End` release of a big map piles hundreds of half-sunk balls into the drain, and their residues stacked into a pale glowing mush over the dark water. Both paths now scale the faded colour to zero with the alpha, which is invisible for the single ball #131 was tuned on and removes the stack.

The map editor keeps `TerrainHoleRadius` at 0, so its sea has neither the cut nor the pool — `FunnelPoolRadius` is pushed but ignored there, every formula in the shader saturating to the open-sea path.

The free camera can dive **below the surface** (in the game, the drop cinematic's lens does), and there the sea alone gets an **underwater murk** so being submerged reads as underwater rather than showing the world unchanged with a water plane cutting through it. It is a post-resolve tint in `Tonemap.fx`, applied in **linear light before the ACES curve** (so the submerged scene rolls through the same highlight response as everything above it): the frame is absorbed towards a blue-green (`UNDERWATER_ABSORB`, a multiplier under 1 — red goes first, so it blues and dims) and the water's own in-scattered glow is added (`UNDERWATER_INSCATTER`), both by `UnderwaterAmount`. That amount is set on the CPU only in the sea scene and ramps `0→1` over `UNDERWATER_FADE_DEPTH` world units below the mean surface (`SceneRenderer.SeaLevelY`, a touch above it so partial submersion already tints), and is **0 (a shader no-op) in every other scene** — the terrain scenes have solid ground, not water, to get under. The tint itself is uniform (no per-pixel depth in that pass, so no distance fog — good enough, and it fades in smoothly as you dive; the spray billboards double as suspended bubbles down there). On top of it the same pass adds a **peripheral blur** — a diver's vision goes soft at the edges: a cheap 8-tap golden-angle spiral disc whose radius and blend both grow from **0 at the frame centre** (which stays crisp) out to the corners, as `edge² · UnderwaterAmount`, so it is stronger towards the edges and off entirely above water. It is inside a branch on the `UnderwaterAmount` **uniform**, so the whole frame takes the same path (no cost above water, no divergence). The overlay and the crosshair draw after the resolve, so they stay sharp. Above water the whole block is byte-for-byte the old resolve.

Blown spray and spindrift ride over the waves (`Spray.fx`, drawn by `SceneRenderer.DrawOverlays` in the sea scene exactly as the mountain's snow is): a static buffer of billboards animated entirely in the vertex shader, like the snow, but in a **thin slab that follows the camera in XZ while clinging to the sea surface in Y** (`SPRAY_LEVEL_Y`, just above the mean), whipped fast downwind (`SPRAY_WIND`, aligned with the waves' own wind) with a turbulent per-particle sway. One buffer carries two reads split per particle — fine **droplets** and larger, fainter **mist wisps** (the spindrift). Each billboard is **stretched along the wind as it projects onto the screen** and thinned across it, the area preserved so the particle stacking below is unchanged, so both classes read as blown streaks rather than round discs — a round camera-facing billboard reads as a white "snowball" (the defect #169 fixed; its large faint mist class was the worst of it, so that class was shrunk as well). The stretch scales with how side-on the wind is to the lens — the wind dotted onto the camera's screen axes has length sin(angle to view) — so a head-on wind foreshortens to a disc on its own (a streak seen end-on *is* a dot) and the streaks follow the orbiting camera for free; a small per-particle turn keeps them out of one comb. It feathers towards the slab's top and edges so it has no hard boundary, is depth-read (the waves and platform occlude it) and runs off the wall clock, so the storm keeps blowing while the simulation is paused. The one trap, worth remembering for any water/light particle here: `GLARE_THRESHOLD` is only **0.55** (on linear luminance), and at a grazing angle the view ray threads hundreds of particles through the thin slab which **stack to full opacity** — so any spray colour above the threshold blooms into a starfield once it accumulates, *however low the per-particle alpha*. The spray colour (`SPRAY_COLOR`) is therefore kept a cool grey-blue **well under** it: it reads as cold storm haze/spindrift rather than white foam-spray, but it never glares. Making it a brighter, whiter spray would need a raised glare threshold (globally, or per-scene) or drawing it in a post-resolve pass — which would forgo the depth occlusion that keeps it behind the platform and cluster. The spray dials (`SPRAY_*`) sit with the sea's on the shared `SceneRenderer`, so the map editor draws the same spray.

## The savanna (Testbed)

The third setting, next in the NumPad2 cycle after the sea (or `scene=savanna`; the enum slot is `SceneKind.Savanna`). It **grew out of the old Sahara dunes** — which are now back as their own [Desert](#the-desert-testbed) scene, below — into open golden grassland rolling gently to a wide horizon, dotted with acacia trees, the island standing in a flat clearing. The rework was prompted by the dunes showing a **grid** — the coarse displaced mesh's per-vertex normal left a faint facet / Mach-band pattern across the sand — and the per-pixel-normal fix is the one thing the savanna terrain does differently from its siblings (and the fix the reborn desert now shares).

`Savanna.fx` displaces a camera-centred grid (`SAVANNA_GRID_N` a side over `SAVANNA_EXTENT`, snapped to its cell so it does not swim) by a smooth rolling field — flat in a clearing around the origin and rising into low rises with distance, flatter than the meadow's hills since a savanna is open. Its **normal is taken per pixel** from the height field's own gradient (three cheap `TerrainHeight` taps in the pixel shader), not interpolated from per-vertex normals: interpolating a coarse mesh's per-vertex normal is exactly what made the grid show (a normal discontinuity, i.e. a Mach band, at every cell edge), and evaluating the gradient per pixel makes the shading smooth regardless of tessellation, so the grid is gone. The grid is also finer than the old dunes (400 vs 200) so the silhouette is smooth too. The meadow and mountains still take their normal per vertex; they get away with it because their per-pixel relief and detail hide the seams, but the per-pixel approach here is the clean fix and the one to reach for if either of them ever shows the same grid.

The grass is dry gold-green, varied in patches at two noise scales so the field is patchy gold and green rather than one flat tone, combed by travelling **gusts** (`Noise.fxh`'s `WindGust`, shared with the meadow and the forest — see "The wind, in one copy" below) and tilted by a fine footprint-band-limited relief that leans by the same gust. It is matte — sun plus the sky hemisphere, dimmed by the shared cloud shadow — and melts into the skyline over `SAVANNA_HORIZON_HAZE_DISTANCE`, so it takes the mood of every dome. Like the sea's dusk default, the savanna **enters under a warm golden dome** (`SAVANNA_DEFAULT_SKY_DOME`, the set's warmest gold horizon) on `scene=savanna` or a NumPad2 switch into it, unless `sky=` overrode it; NumPad1 still cycles freely. Drawn opaque, first in the scene block, CullNone; the grass, wind, relief and terrain dials (`SAVANNA_*`, `GRASS_COLOR_*`) sit on the shared `SceneRenderer`. Shader Model 5.0, drawn in both executables.

**The fine relief drew a diamond lattice across the middle distance for a long time (#117)**, and it is the same mistake as the crown's checkerboard below at a different scale and by a different mechanism. `GrassRelief` was **two crossed plane-wave sines**, and two plane waves crossing *are* a lattice — that is what their interference is. These crossed at **93.4°**, so the lattice was very nearly square and read in perspective as a field of diamonds; at the shipped `GrassReliefFrequency` 2 the two periods are **3.14 and 1.75 world units**, which is the scale the diamonds appeared at. It showed as strongly as it did because the field feeds `PerturbNormalFromHeight`, so it tilts the *normal* and lands in the shading rather than merely in the colour — the strongest way to make a pattern visible. This is exactly the failure `Noise.fxh`'s own opening documents ("a sum of plane-wave sines keeps its planes however many terms it has") and the one #86 removed from the mountain's peaks; two terms is simply its smallest case, and being only two they never get the chance to hide each other. Note this is **not** the savanna's older grid artifact, which was the coarse mesh's per-vertex normal and is genuinely fixed — this sat on top of that fix.
- Three octaves of gradient noise now, through `Noise.fxh`'s `Fbm2Combed`. Two things had to come with the swap. **The domain is stretched along the wind** (`GRASS_COMB_STRETCH` 2.6): one of the two sines always dominated and supplied a *grain* for free, and isotropic noise has none by construction — a relief without one reads as gravel rather than as grass lying over. And **the amplitude had to be re-derived rather than carried over** (`GRASS_FBM_GAIN` 5.0): two crossed sines have an RMS near 0.51 (0.6 and 0.4 in quadrature) where three octaves of `Fbm2` sum to about 0.10, so keeping `GrassReliefStrength` 0.05 unchanged would have left a fifth of the relief, which on a normal-tilting field is the effect gone rather than softened. That is the trap #97 hit on the acacia crowns.
- **The meadow carried a line-for-line copy of the same function**, lattice included; #117 was filed against the savanna alone and the copy was found while fixing it. The mechanism is one copy in `Noise.fxh` now and what stays per scene is the tuning, which is the split that should have existed from the start.
- Measured (front end, 1600×900, `quality=High`, dome 13, `nocap`, 16 s runs): savanna **639.9 → 597.6 FPS** (1.56 → 1.67 ms), meadow **672.0 → 625.2** (1.49 → 1.60). **+0.11 ms and about −7 % on each** — three noise octaves per pixel against two sines, on a full-screen terrain shader. Cheap for an artifact this visible, and an order below what the forest floor's second pass costs (see "The forest").

**The campfire is a RING of them now** — `CampfireConfig.Count`, eight by default, evenly spaced around the island. One fire lit a single flank of the stone and left the rest of the walk the gun makes in flat dome light; eight light it all the way round, and the island's drum takes a warm rim wherever the camera orbits to.
- **The ring is derived from the one config point, not from new fields.** `GroundXZ` is fire zero, and the radius and the ring's rotation come out of it, so the fire the single-fire era authored stands exactly where it stood and simply gains seven more on its circle. Everything else — range, flame size, colour, height above the terrain — is shared: they are the same kind of fire, and a range or a colour per fire would be a config nobody could tune.
- **Each fire burns on its own clock, and that is the whole of what makes it read as fire rather than as a decoration.** Both the light's flicker (`SceneRenderer.CampfireColor`) and the flame's shape (`Flame.fx`'s `FlameSeed`) take the fire's index: it offsets the time *and* stretches every rate by a few per cent. An offset alone would leave eight flames licking the identical pattern a moment apart, which the eye reads as one wave travelling round the island the instant two of them are in shot together — and the vertical frequencies are stretched as well, so the tongues differ in **shape** and not only in timing.
- **Eight is the cap and it is the shader's, not a taste figure**: each fire is a real point light, and `SceneLights.MaxLights` is 8 to match `MAX_SCENE_LIGHTS` in `Savanna.fx` and `InstancedModel.fx`. The savanna can spend the whole budget because it is the only thing in the scene that lights.
- Measured (Testbed, savanna, dome 13, `nocap`, fixed low camera): **1.75 → 1.85 ms**, one fire against eight — +0.10 ms for eight point lights in the grass's per-pixel loop and eight two-triangle billboard draws.
- **Each fire now stands in a hearth rather than out of untouched grass (#282).** A fire billboard with a point light under it and nothing else on the ground read exactly as reported: fire burning out of a lawn. Two parts, and they are deliberately in different places. **The stones** are a ring of `RockMesh` boulders (the forest's own boulder, re-proportioned) set into the ground around each fire, drawn on the **acacia's own instanced path** — same shader, same sun-and-dome lighting as everything else planted on this terrain — with one draw per *fire* rather than per mesh variant. **The scorched ground** is `Savanna.fx`'s own: a burn field that ashes the grass over the ring and chars it nearly black at the fire's foot, with the grass relief and the wind's own combing fading out with it, because what that relief is a texture *of* is blades and a hearth has none. Everything is sized off `FlameSize` rather than in world units, so a hearth belongs to the fire standing in it — these flames are 14 units tall, and a hearth measured once by hand would be a kerb of pebbles the day somebody widened them.
  - **The firelight on the stones is a per-draw additive, not a ninth point light** (`Acacia.fx`'s `AddedLight`, zero for every plant). Every stone of a ring stands at one distance from one fire, so the attenuation a point light solves per pixel is a constant here; it is worked out against the same quadratic falloff the ground uses, multiplied by the stone's own albedo (what reaches the eye is firelight reflected off basalt), and it rides `CampfireColor` at this frame's time so a ring breathes with its own fire. What it does not do is favour the side of a stone facing the flame, which at this size is not what tells the eye it is a fire pit.
  - **The hearth positions are pushed at config time, not per frame**, and the per-pixel loop is bounded by an early-out on the ring's own extent — `HearthNear`/`HearthFar`, measured in C# **from the fires' own positions** rather than re-deriving the config's ring rule inside the shader. A second copy of a placement rule is how a scene grows a fault nobody can see (#297).
  - **From the play camera you will not see it.** The fires are 33 units out and below the island's rim from where the gun stands, so what the player sees of a fire in play is still its flame against the sky. The hearth reads from the front end's orbiting camera, from a chapter intro and from any elevated vantage — which is where the bare-grass fire looked wrong in the first place.
  - Measured (Testbed, savanna, dome 1, `nocap`, `nopost`, windowed 1600×900 at ssaa 4, fixed elevated camera with **all eight** hearths in frame, 18 s runs, first four readings dropped, alternated with the build without them): **7.37 / 7.33 ms without against 7.44 / 7.54 with** — about **+0.14 ms** for eight stone rings (56 instances in 8 draws) and the ground's burn term together. The same order as the ring of fires itself cost (+0.10 ms above), and measured from the one camera that has every hearth on screen at once.
- **The flame's height is a config value and not the shader's business, and it is the game's camera that decided the number.** `Flame.fx` used to size the billboard `FlameSize × 2.4`, which is 5.5 units tall — and the fires stand on grass some **five units below the island's stone**, while the game's play camera sits about level with that stone. So from the one camera that matters a fire cleared the rim by half a unit and all that showed was the tip of one. `FlameHeightScale` separates height from width because the obvious fix — growing `FlameSize` — raises the tip by widening the fire into a bonfire. Six was chosen from the play camera against 4.5 and 8: 4.5 is present but slight, 8 stops reading as a fire and becomes a beam standing in the sky. It does make a flame ~14 units tall, taller than the acacias' 9, which is accepted rather than overlooked — these are 33 units out and the eye judges them against the island beside them.

Scattered over it are **acacia trees** — the flat-topped umbrella tree that reads as savanna at a glance (`Acacia.fx`, `AcaciaMesh`). They were flat camera-facing billboards until #202, the pixel shader drawing the tree's silhouette with `clip()` — which read as a paper cutout, one flat shape however the camera moved. They are **real 3D geometry** now: a bark trunk under a wide, flat-topped umbrella canopy, both `LatheMesh`es (the canopy is exactly what a lathe is for — a flat top run out to a thin rim, then a domed underside curving back in to a thick middle, so it is fat in the centre and thins to the sides, the rim wobbled so it is not a machined disc), planted as instances (`ModelInstance`, the shared instancing path) and shaded from the scene's own sun and dome exactly as the terrain is — `dot(N, SunDirection)` and the zenith-to-horizon ambient off the normal — so a tree sits in the savanna's light rather than being pasted over it. A **few variants of each kind** — trees at rolled proportions and structural seeds, plus a low domed **bush** with no trunk — with per-plant size, a small lean off vertical (a leaning tree reads as a tree, a tilted one as a felled one, the forest's own `TREE_LEAN`) and a free yaw, and a per-variant greenness, so a grove is a mix rather than one shape stamped out. The canopy's leaf mottle is a 3D field of **world** position (`Fbm3`), so it has no seam and does not swim as the canopy turns with its tree, and the trunk takes none of it. **120 of them** (`AcaciaConfig.Count` — 8 until #168, 64 until #202's denser savanna, over the same-sized ring the forest's 240 scatter across; the count is a look decision, not a budget) scatter in a ring clear of the island (`MinRadius`/`MaxRadius` 42/340), **82 % around twenty cluster centres** (`Clusters`, raised with the count — a cluster centre no plant reaches is not a grove), planted on the terrain (`SavannaTerrainHeight` mirrors the shader's own field) and kept out of each other by `ScatterSpacing`'s rule, one copy with the forest's. Drawn opaque and depth-writing after the terrain, before the birds.

A flock of birds circles overhead — the only moving creature in the whole world, and still no sprite sheet; the savanna, the desert and the outback **share the one flock** (the mesh, the shader and the orbits live once in `SceneRenderer`, drawn in all three scenes). They were **camera-facing billboards until #235**, the pixel shader drawing the whole bird with a `clip()`ed silhouette and a `sin` flap — and that failed the same way #202's acacia billboards did, plus once more on top: a flat quad shows one silhouette however the camera moves, so a bird could never **bank into the turn it was flying**, and its wings could only ever be the straight V two line segments make. They are **real 3D geometry** now (`BirdMesh` — a spindle body with a head on a neck, a fanned tail, and wings that end in separated fingered primaries), shaded from the scene's own sun and dome exactly as `Acacia.fx` and the terrain under it are, so a bird sits in the scene's light rather than being pasted over it. `BirdsConfig.Color` is therefore an **albedo** now and not a finished radiance: from below against a bright sky the ambient term alone brings it out near black, which is what a backlit bird is, and the sun is what puts a flash on a bird's back as it banks over. Drawn **opaque and depth-writing** where the billboard was alpha-blended and depth-read — a bird is a solid, and its own breast has to hide the far wing behind it — under `CullNone`, since the wings, the primaries and the tail are single sheets whose normal the pixel shader turns towards the camera instead.

**The shape is the rest pose and the whole flap is in the vertex shader**, off two per-bird uniforms; each vertex carries the pair it is animated from (its signed spanwise station, and its distance forward of the wing's mean line — the twist is a rotation about that line, and that distance is all such a rotation needs). Four things in it are what stopped the motion reading as a machine, and each answers a named half of #235:

- **the wing is an ARC, not a V.** Every spanwise station turns by its own angle — a curve that grows faster than the span does — where the billboard applied one angle along the whole wing and got two straight segments.
- **the beat travels OUT along the wing**, so the tip lags the shoulder and the stroke is a wave rather than a hinge; and the stroke is **skewed** (`θ − K·sin θ`, monotonic for `K < 1`, so it retimes the beat without ever running it backwards) into a fast downstroke and a slow recovery.
- **the wrist flexes on the way up** — the hand pulls in and sweeps back outboard of the wrist — and **the hand twists nose-down as it comes down**, which is what a wing does to make thrust. The body rises and falls against its own wings; a body held dead still while the wings work is the giveaway of a puppet.
- **a bird GLIDES most of the time.** The old flock beat at a fixed rate for ever. Each bird now flies a long cycle of one short burst and a long glide — measured at **13 % of the time beating**, at **2.1–3.0 beats a second**, the rest gliding with the wings only breathing. ⚠ The burst's *length* is **derived** from how many beats it is and how fast that bird beats; rolled independently it let a two-beat burst spread across a long window and come out at **0.8 Hz**, which reads as slow motion rather than as a bird. Both edges of a burst are continuous **by construction, not by smoothing**: a burst runs a whole number of beats so it ends on the neutral point it started on, the glide then sweeps exactly one slow turn of the same phase, and the envelope is floored at the value the glide holds. Measured across both edges, the wing's tip angle steps in exact proportion to the sample interval (0.083° at 0.5 ms, 0.0083° at 0.05 ms, 0.00083° at 0.005 ms) — the signature of no discontinuity at all. Nothing is tracked between frames: a bird's whole flap is a function of the wall clock.

The C# side places each bird: `BirdsConfig.Count` of them orbit `FlockCenter` over the field, each at its own radius, altitude, phase and unhurried speed, all off the wall clock, so the kettle wheels slowly and never beats in time. **The nose goes where the bird is actually going** — the circle's horizontal tangent plus the climb its own drift is doing at that instant, so a bird tips up as it rises and down as it sinks off a derivative rather than off a dial. **The bank leans the bird's up vector towards the inside of its circle** (`Matrix.CreateWorld` squares the basis up from there, so there is no hand-rolled cross product to get the handedness wrong in), and it is taken from the **radius** — 30° on the tightest orbit to 15° on the widest, wandering slowly — rather than from the honest `atan(v²/g·r)`: this flock deliberately circles far slower than a real kettle of vultures does, and at these speeds that formula asks for about four degrees on the wide orbits and seventy on the tight ones. One draw per bird of the one shared mesh, for `DrawFlame`'s reason — three uniforms vary, there are nine birds at most and only in three scenes, and the alternative is an instance buffer and a vertex format for something that has neither. **It costs nothing measurable.** Against the billboard it replaced, on a 6900XT at 1600×900 with vsync off, savanna went 1228 → 1232 FPS and desert 1220 → 1246 — while **city, which has no birds in it at all, moved 1449 → 1489 across the same pair of runs**. That control is what makes the pair readable: every difference here sits inside the run-to-run noise it establishes. The flock's count, size, place and colour sit in `BirdsConfig`; its `Aspect` (the billboard's height as a fraction of its width) went with the billboard.

## The desert (Testbed)

The fourth setting, next in the NumPad2 cycle after the savanna (or `scene=desert`; the enum slot is `SceneKind.Desert`). A Sahara: rolling sand dunes stretching to a hazed horizon, the round island standing in the sand exactly as it floats on the sea and stands in the grass. It was the third scene originally, was **turned into the savanna** to fix a grid artefact, and is now **back as its own scene alongside the savanna** — the two arid siblings adjacent in the cycle — because the fix that killed it (the per-pixel terrain normal) means it can come back clean. `Desert.fx` with its dials, mesh and `DrawDesert` on the shared `SceneRenderer`, so the map editor draws the same dunes.

The dunes are **real geometry**, not a flat plane: the shared `CreateGridMesh` grid (`DESERT_GRID_N` a side over `DESERT_EXTENT`), snapped to its cell each frame so it does not swim, displaced in the vertex shader by a low-frequency **direction-mixed sine sum** (`DuneSum`) — a dune with no silhouette against the sky is just a lit patch of floor. `DuneSum` is two long low waves for the shape of the field plus a third term **waveshaped into crest lines** (`1 − |x|`, smoothstepped), because a dune has a sharp crest and a long windward face where a sine has a rounded hump; how far that crest may be sharpened is set by the mesh and not by taste — the cell is ~2.8 units and the ridge terms' own wavelengths are ~57 and ~30, so the crest stays several cells wide and the geometry can still hold it. Sharpened past that it falls between vertices and the per-pixel normal shades a ridge the silhouette does not have. The trailing constant takes the field's mean back to zero: it is multiplied by the clearing ramp, so a field with a mean would rise with the ramp and the desert would read as a shallow bowl with the island at the bottom. Like every terrain scene since the arena became the small round island, it is **flat in a clearing** around the origin (the island's foot, `DESERT_LEVEL_Y`, the same level the savanna's grass sits at) and **rises into dunes with distance** (`DESERT_CLEARING_RADIUS`/`_TRANSITION`, `DUNE_AMPLITUDE` the peak height out in the far field), so the sand fills the frame and the dunes read against the horizon rather than crowding the platform.

The one thing it does deliberately, and the whole reason it can return: the base **normal is taken per pixel** from the height field's gradient (three cheap `DesertHeight` taps in the pixel shader), *not* interpolated from a per-vertex normal. Interpolating a coarse displaced mesh's per-vertex normal is exactly what left the old dunes' faint facet / Mach-band grid; evaluating the gradient per pixel makes the shading smooth regardless of tessellation — the same fix the savanna taught, applied here (the grid is finer too, 360 vs the old 200, for a smooth crest silhouette). On top of the smooth dune normal, fine **wind ripples** (`SandPattern`, band-limited against the footprint and fed through `PerturbNormalFromHeight`) lie in the sand — **static, since #276**: a ripple is a ridge carved into the dune, and the domain used to be scrolled downwind at 1.4 world units a second, which slid every ridge across the surface it is cut into and is what the owner reported as the sand looking wrong. Nor was a slower speed the answer, since real ripples migrate centimetres in an hour. What moves here is the air: a veil of **blown dust** (the shared `Clouds.fxh` noise) drifts across it and thickens towards the horizon into a windblown murk. It melts into the skyline over `DESERT_HORIZON_HAZE_DISTANCE`, so it takes the mood of every dome.

**The sand's own surface is the part that was rebuilt, and the flat version is what argued for it**: one uniform tan, ripples that read as a regular diagonal crosshatch — which is what a sum of plane-wave sines always reads as (see `Noise.fxh`'s opening) — and dunes whose tops were the rounded crest of a sine. Real wind-rippled sand is **braided**: sets of wavy lines running across the wind, each set wandering, one handing over to another in patches. `SandLayer` draws exactly that, and all three of its parts are load-bearing — two sets of repeat rounded lines at **+10° and −9°** (enough to cross visibly, little enough to stay one family), each made wavy by perturbing its across-line **phase** with `Noise.fxh` gradient noise running at roughly the line frequency itself (a slow perturbation bends the set like a bent comb; one at the line spacing lets each line wander independently of its neighbours), and the two **screen-blended** with a weight that wanders over the ground. One set alone is corduroy; a straight average of two is a lattice, the same failure as multiplying sines instead of summing them; and the screen blend rather than a mix because averaging darkens exactly the crossings the eye reads the weave from. The technique's lineage is Shane's *Desert Sand* on Shadertoy, rewritten rather than ported: its hashes are sine-based (which band differently across drivers, and `Noise.fxh` exists to end that) and it band-limits by distance alone, which would flatten near sand along with far.
  - **The colour does two steps from one noise evaluation**: a broad field mixes `SAND_COLOR`'s warm ochre against the new `SAND_COLOR_PALE`, and the *same* field offset varies the brightness of what that gave — patches with no tone read as stains, tone with no patches reads as vignetting. On top of it the ripple field itself darkens its own troughs (the cheapest ambient occlusion there is, and what makes the ripples read on the faces the sun is *not* raking — with the normal tilt alone a dune's shadowed flank goes back to being a flat patch of colour), and a per-pixel hash over a 60-cells-per-unit lattice puts **grains** in the near sand. The grain's fade must finish *before* its cells reach pixel size, not at it, or its last few metres are a crawling speckle. The sand is no longer quite matte either: `SHEEN_STRENGTH` adds a wide Blinn lobe (quartz grains are little mirrors, so it is a broad sheen off a million grains facing every way rather than a highlight), gated on `ndotl` or the lee faces would glint in light that never reaches them.
  - **One pattern layer, and that is measured.** The original stacks a second layer turned 15° and a quarter finer, mixed in by a further noise field — four more gradient-noise evaluations per pixel on a shader that covers the whole frame. Measured from a vantage low over the sand at `ssaa=2`, where the frame *is* terrain: flat sand 47.6 FPS, two layers **30.5** (+56 % frame time), one layer **36.1** (+32 %). What the second layer bought was large-scale variety, and most of that comes free instead — `SandLayer` already computes a wandering field for its blend, so the same field is fed back as a slow **bend** of the line family. It must be a bend of the *position* and never of the frequency: scaling the frequency by a field puts `coordinate × field` in the phase, and the coordinate here is a world position running to ±500, so the phase gradient goes as hundreds of cycles per unit while the band-limit is still computed from the nominal frequency and has no idea. The near sand came out as a violent rainbow moiré across the entire frame.
  - And `SandPattern` has **no early exit** once the ripples are unresolvable, tempting as one is: the value feeds `ddx`/`ddy` through `PerturbNormalFromHeight` and the resolvable factor varies per pixel, so a branch on it would diverge inside a quad along the very line where the ripples fade — and a quad whose lanes took different paths has undefined derivatives. A `lerp` does the same job for a handful of ALU. The sharpening of the line crests fades **quadratically faster** than their amplitude, because the shaping is a nonlinearity and generates harmonics well above the fundamental: fading the amplitude alone would leave the crests as the last thing to go and the first thing to crawl. Drawn opaque, first in the scene block where the city would be, `CullNone` (the winding is moot on a heightfield); the dune, ripple, dust and sand dials (`DUNE_AMPLITUDE`, `DESERT_LEVEL_Y`, `DESERT_RIPPLE_*`, `DESERT_DUST_*`, `SAND_COLOR`, `SAND_COLOR_PALE`, `SHEEN_STRENGTH`) sit on the shared `SceneRenderer`. Shader Model 5.0, drawn in both executables. It carries **no point lights** (no campfire), so unlike the savanna its draw sets `SceneLightCount` to nothing, and it forces **no default dome** — like the mountains and the meadow, it keeps whatever dome is up. It shares the savanna's [flock of birds](#the-savanna-testbed) circling overhead (drawn from the one mesh in all three scenes), the one moving creature over the empty sand.

## The mountains (Testbed)

The fifth setting in the NumPad2 cycle (or `scene=mountain`). A snow basin the arena sits in, ringed by tall snow-capped peaks that rise with distance and fade into an alpine haze — the city's clearing-among-towers rebuilt in rock and snow, the platform standing in the basin as it stands anywhere.

The terrain is the desert's machinery with a different field: a camera-centred grid (they share `CreateGridMesh`), snapped to its cell on the CPU so it does not swim, displaced by a *ridged fractal* heightfield — `Noise.fxh`'s `RidgedFbm2`, five octaves at a 130-unit first spacing — rather than the dunes' smooth sines. It is low and gentle around the arena (world origin) and rises into peaks with distance (`ClearingRadius`/`ClearingTransition`), so the play surface sits in a clearing the way it does in the city, and the basin stays below the platform glass. Its base normal is taken by finite differences — three field taps per vertex — because the ridge folds and the distance ramp would make a closed-form gradient more trouble than it saves; a fine `RockRelief` (band-limited against the footprint) then perturbs it per pixel so the near rock reads as rough stone. Snow lies where the surface faces up (a `smoothstep` on `normal.y`) *and* above an irregular, noise-broken altitude snowline, so the range reads as rock with snow on it rather than one white blanket; rock varies between a dark and a lighter grey-brown in patches; the blue zenith fills the shadows so the snow reads cold where the sun misses it, and the range dissolves into the skyline over `MOUNTAIN_HORIZON_HAZE_DISTANCE` — the strong aerial perspective that says a lot of cold air. **And since #208 the snow carries its own two surfaces**, which it had none of: a wind-streaked drift relief (`SnowRelief` — combed fbm on its own grain, crossed to the rock's, stretched harder and half its strength, tilting the normal only where the snow mask says snow lies) and a sparse sun-facing **sparkle** — a glint in the top 0.75 % of a third-of-a-metre cell lattice, added as a specular-weighted glint after lighting and gated on the snow mask. **The cell says only WHERE a glint is; its size comes from the footprint** (about a pixel and a half, jittered off its cell's centre by whatever the radius leaves over so it can never be cut off flat against the boundary). That was #278: a cell is fixed in *world* units, so the same third of a metre that is the intended 3–4 pixels at the mid slopes' ~0.08 world/pixel is **thirty** pixels on the basin floor beside the arena — and lighting the whole of it there scattered hard white parallelograms over the ground the player stands closest to, on rock rather than snow, since the glint's own comment said "on the lit snow" and the line did not multiply by the mask. (The threshold takes 0.75 % rather than the ~1.5 % this said until #278, because `NoiseHash22` answers in [-1, 1]; the sparser reading is the one that was tuned by eye, so the number stayed and the arithmetic is written down beside it.) Both band-limited against the footprint like everything else in this shader; measured, the pair costs nothing visible at the frame rate (326-372 FPS at 1600×900 ssaa2 uncapped, against the scene's own noise). ****|⚠  THE DRIFT RELIEF WAS THEN CUT OUTRIGHT (#296), at `High` and everywhere else.** The owner asked for something to be given up on the top tier, and this was the menu, measured on the reference desktop at 3840x1600, `ssaa` 2, against a 10.99 ms frame: the **sparkle 0.20 ms**, the **rock's fourth octave 0.31**, the **drift relief 1.08** - close to a tenth of the mountain's whole frame, and photographed on and off from a mid-range camera over the snowfields the two frames cannot be told apart. Best saving, least seen; the mountain's `High` is **10.99 -> 9.85 ms** for it. The cut is all-or-nothing on purpose: three octaves to two saved only **0.27** and to one only **0.63** - the occupancy signature again, partial cuts buying proportionally less and only removing the call crossing back over the threshold. One octave is the 0.63 ms option if the character is ever wanted back. **What #208 was actually complaining about survives, and this is lighter than the state it complained about rather than a return to it**: the snowfields keep the **sparkle** - the half a photograph of snow is recognised by - and they still take their 40 % share of the rock's relief. **`MountainReduced` (#298) then needed a new partner for the sparkle**, half its old pair having been promoted into the authored scene; the rock's fourth octave is it, so `Low` drops the glint and shades the rock on three octaves. Weaker than the pair it replaces - **0.18 ms at `ssaa` 1 and 0.58 at `ssaa` 2**, against 0.40 / 1.36 - which is the honest price of the good half having gone to everyone.

**The peaks read as low-poly for a long time, and the tessellation was never the reason (#86).** The field was four octaves of `1 - |sin(dot(p, direction))|`, and both of that construction's failures were on the screen at once. Plane waves keep their planes: every face carried the same long straight corrugations, which read as wind-rippled sand rather than rock. And its finest octave's ridges ran about **thirty units** apart, so an 82-unit peak had three features from base to summit and its outline against the sky was a smooth simple cone — the "1998" read is a field with no high frequencies in it, not a mesh with visible facets. Measured against the grid, the case closes: the cell is **3.34 units**, so the vertex grid was oversampling the field about ninefold and a denser one would have changed nothing. `Noise.fxh`'s opening calls this exact construction out — "a sum of plane-wave sines keeps its planes however many terms it has, and the eye reads it instantly as the plasma effect of a 1994 demo" — and the library exists to replace it; the mountain was simply the scene that never got converted.

Converting it needed one non-obvious step. `RidgedFbm2` **cannot be dropped in raw**: sampled over the annulus the peaks occupy it means 0.70 with a standard deviation of 0.13, against the plane-wave field's 0.36 and 0.17 — it sits high and narrow, so it lifts the whole basin and leaves shallow dips in it, which draws a white blanket rather than a range (it did, first try). Dropping a 0.24 floor out and **cubing** what is left puts the terrain back on the height distribution the rest of the scene was tuned against — median 0.30 and p95 0.67 against the old field's 0.35 and 0.66, so `Height` 82 and the snowline both stand unchanged — and the curve is itself the ridged look: valleys flatten, crests keep their height. Measured slope says the same, the median falling from 0.77 to 0.67 while the p99 rises from 2.48 to 3.72. That steepening is why `SnowSlope` moved 0.95 → 0.78: the old band wanted a face within 18° of flat, which on the new massing left the snow only in the gullies and a bare rock range in a scene it snows in. Cost, measured on the integrated Radeon at `High`: **~25.0 ms against ~24.2**, about 1 ms for five gradient-noise octaves evaluated three times per vertex (the finite-difference normal) over 129 600 vertices.

**And the same construction was still in the scene's other field until #170 — the fine `RockRelief`, which laid a plaid over the snow.** #86 converted the *massing* and left the per-pixel relief alone; #140 then added a **fourth sine onto it** rather than replacing it, which is how the defect outlived its own diagnosis twice. Confirmed by capture before anything was touched: a regular cross-hatch, plus broad diagonal bands, in the **interior** of the snowfields — which is also what identified the culprit, since the report's other candidate (`snowNoise`, one un-fractalled octave on the snowline altitude) would wobble the snow's *edge* and cannot lay a lattice across its middle. At `RockReliefFrequency` 0.6 the four periods are 2π/f and its halves — about 10.5, 5.5, 2.9 and 1.5 world units — and they interfere by construction. It reaches the snow at all because the slope term that scales the relief floors at **0.4 rather than 0**, so flat, up-facing ground — exactly where the snow lies — still takes 40 % of a field that feeds the *normal*.

- **Four octaves of `Fbm2Combed`**, so there is no shared axis to read as a lattice and the relief still has a **grain** (`ROCK_GRAIN` is the dominant sine's own direction, `ROCK_STRETCH` 2.4) — the savanna's #117 lesson, that isotropic noise reads as gravel. Four and not the two or three the report suggested: #140 added the finest sine to reach crag scale, and the fourth octave is what still reaches it (10.5 world units down to ~1.3, the old spectrum's own span).
- **The frequency is converted, not reinterpreted.** The config's figure is a sine's *angular* frequency (period 2π/f) where a noise field wants a domain scale (a feature is ~1/scale), so it goes in as `f/2π` and the coarsest feature stays where it was authored; passing it straight in would make every feature 6.3× finer and take the broad undulation out of the range. The comment on that property said "features per world unit", which was wrong for the sines too, and now says what it is.
- **The amplitude is bought back by a shader gain (`ROCK_FBM_GAIN` 3.0), not by moving `RockReliefStrength`** — #117's choice, and this scene has the stronger reason for it: both mountain levels bake that figure into their JSON (see the snow dials below), so a new default would not reach the shipping game until `LevelGen` was re-run, and a hand-built level pinning 0.5 would quietly go flat. The three is derived and then checked: the four sines' weights put their RMS near 0.41 where four `Fbm2BandLimited` octaves come to about 0.20, and for equal amplitude and period a sine is half again as steep — which matters because it is the field's *slope* that tilts the normal. Confirmed by eye from a fixed camera against the old field: the relief reads as strongly as it did, and irregularly.
- **Measured: no cost.** Game front end, mountain, dome 13, `quality=medium`, `nocap`, 30 s runs, first six samples discarded: sines **49.9 mean / 51.8 median FPS**, noise **53.3 / 54.8**. Four rotated gradient-noise octaves against four sines came out a touch *faster* — plausibly because each old octave carried a `sin` and a `saturate` guard of its own — but the run-to-run spread is 38–61 FPS on this machine, so the honest claim is that the swap costs nothing measurable rather than that it wins anything. This is the one place the mountain differs from the savanna's own conversion, which measured +0.11 ms for going from two sines to three noise octaves.
- **`snowNoise` was left alone**, deliberately. It is the report's secondary candidate and nothing in the capture points at it; changing it would move where the snow lies, which is a look decision with no fault behind it.

`MOUNTAIN_GRID_N` was raised from 240 to 360 for craggier detail, and **that is what forced `CreateGridMesh` onto a 32-bit index buffer**: a grid over ~255 a side has more than 65 536 vertices, and the old 16-bit index silently wrapped, so far triangles referenced the wrong vertices and stretched into garbage. On the near-flat sea (380) and savanna (400) that garbage stayed down at the surface and never showed — but the mountain's tall peaks stretched it into strong, regular *dark horizontal bands across the whole sky*, which read exactly like a glare/shading artifact and cost a long hunt down the wrong path (glare threshold, snow brightness, normal band-limiting — none of it). The lesson is in the code: **any `CreateGridMesh` grid over 255 a side must use 32-bit indices**; the sea and savanna were silently over the line already and are now correct too.

And it snows. `Snow.fx` is a static buffer of billboard flakes — one quad each, its base a fixed point in a unit cube — animated entirely in the vertex shader: each flake falls, drifts on the wind, sways and tumbles, wrapping with `frac` inside a box that follows the camera, so the snowfall is endless and always around you. Following the camera rather than the world trades a little translational parallax for never popping at a box edge, the right trade for a uniform veil of small flakes. They draw last, alpha-blended and depth-read (the terrain and the cluster hide the flakes behind them) writing no depth, in the mountain scene alone. Their color's luminance (~0.76) actually sits *over* `GLARE_THRESHOLD` (0.55); what keeps a near flake from blooming into a glowing orb — which reads as a bug rather than a snowflake — is the feathered speck alpha and the quarter-resolution glare downsample diluting its few pixels. Dim the colour, not the opacity, if flakes ever glow.

**For a long time a flake was a snowball (#85)**, and the reason is worth keeping: a feathered white circle is exactly what a lit sphere looks like. The old speck was a disc feathered only over its outer half at opacity 0.9, and at `FlakeSize` 0.13 one eighteen units out still covered 23 pixels — against a ball a single unit wide, that is a snowball falling past the camera, not weather. Three things separate a flake from a ball, and the shader now carries all three. **Shape**: the silhouette is a six-armed crystal, `cos(6θ)` built from the direction cosines by the double-angle identity — a handful of ALU, and unlike `atan2` no branch cut to seam on — normalised by `1 + lobe` so the arms always reach the quad's inscribed circle and a spikier flake loses the area between them rather than growing out of the quad that carries it. The per-flake lobe depth is decorrelated from the size random and never zero: a flake with no arms is the disc the whole shape exists to escape. **Tumble**: the quad turns in its own plane while the mask stays in flake-local space, so the crystal itself rotates, and a twinkle at twice the spin rate glints it broadside-on. **Focus**: the box is centred on the camera, so `length(boxPosition)` *is* the distance from the lens — a flake nearer than a quarter of `NearFade` is dropped entirely, because real snow that close to a lens is a blur and drawing it crisply is what filled the screen with a white coin. Softening the mask alone would not have done it: wherever a flake crosses something dark the tonemap crushes the feathered rim back to white, which is why `Opacity` came down to 0.6 as well.

The dials — count, size, shape, fall speed, wind — sit in `MountainSceneConfig.Snow` (`SnowConfig`), and `SceneRenderer` rebuilds the flake buffer when the count changes, so the map editor's property grid retunes the snowfall live. They used to be baked into the mountain levels' JSON as well, so a changed default did not reach the shipping game until the generator was re-run; since the format-2 refactor a level names only its scene and **a change to these defaults reaches every mountain level directly**. Retuning for #85 halved the flake and dropped its opacity a third, which cost the veil most of its coverage — hence `FlakeCount` 1400 → 2800.

### The "38 FPS mountain" was not the mountain (#270)

**A playtest reported this scene at ~38 FPS fullscreen on a 6900 XT, an order of magnitude under the anchors above. It is not this scene, and it is not a cost at all** — the finding is kept here because here is where it will be looked for. Measured on the reporting machine itself (Ryzen 9 5900X / RX 6900 XT, **3840×1600 panel at 75 Hz**), windowed throughout, `fpscap=` rather than `nocap`:

- **The frame is cheap.** In the Game, `level=Column` (540 balls) under this scene at `quality=high ssaa=2`, windowed 1600×900: **200 FPS under `fpscap=200`** — under 5 ms. The same run under vsync presents at **37.5**. It also holds `fpscap=150`, `100`, **and 76** exactly, and 76 paces the frame the same way a 75 Hz vsync does, with the same idle. So it is not frame pacing, not a GPU clock ramp, and not a frame sitting just over the budget.
- **Paired repeats, the same build and the same level, alternating:** vsync **37.5 / 37.5**, `fpscap=75` **75.0 / 75.0**. Same work, same target rate, twice the frame rate. What costs the frames is the *presentation*.
- **It is not monotonic in supersampling** — ssaa 1 → 75, **2 → 37.5**, 3 → 31.8, 4 → 75 — and no cost curve can be V-shaped with its minimum in the middle. That alone rules out "the scene costs this much" without any further argument.
- **What it takes is a heavy cluster, not this backdrop.** The mountain *alone* never collapses: the Game's front end reads 73 and the Testbed holds a flat 75.0 at ssaa 1, 2 **and** 4, and under this scene the small `level=One` holds 75.0 while `level=Column` halves. (This bullet said until 2026-08-27 that the same level "drawn in the meadow collapses identically", and that was **wrong** — see the correction below. The three bullets above are all measured under the mountain and do not depend on it.)
- **The Testbed shows a milder form of the same thing** (a heavy map at ssaa 2: vsync **71.0** against `fpscap=75` **75.0**, the frame itself 5.03 ms), so this is the shared present path and the Game amplifies it. `PresentationInterval` is `One` in both, not `Two`; the mechanism inside DXGI/MonoGame was **not** pinned down and nothing here should be read as saying it was.

**~38 FPS is 75/2**, and this project has met that number before: `BS3DGame.Quality.cs` records #121/#209's Onion clearing the menu at High and then playing the whole level at "37.5 FPS on a 75 Hz panel, exactly half refresh".

One figure here *is* about cost, and it is the one that matters for a fullscreen player. It has now been measured **fullscreen at the reporting resolution itself** rather than extrapolated from a window: `level=Column` at **3840×1600, `quality=high`** (ssaa 2, so 24.6 Mpix shaded), `nocap` —

| mountain, `level=Column`, dome 8 | | |
|---|---|---|
| launch 1 | 81.0 FPS | **12.35 ms** |
| launch 2 | 80.8 FPS | **12.38 ms** |

**12.35 ms against the 13.3 ms a 75 Hz panel allows** — about a millisecond of headroom, so fullscreen `High` here is genuinely marginal, which is why the half-refresh present above had something to catch. On a slower machine there is no headroom at all.

>
> **So the cross-scene comparison was never made, and the issue's framing is not answered by one.** It does not need to be: the 38 FPS was measured to be the present path and not a cost at all (the four bullets above, all under the mountain), so "far under every other scene's class" is answered by there being no class difference to explain. The mountain's own fullscreen cost, 12.35 ms, stands — it is the figure the two launches agree on. The trap is now trap 11 in `.claude/skills/benchmark/SKILL.md`.

The windowed stand-in this was estimated from first held up, which is worth knowing because it is how the *other* machine can measure a 4K load: ssaa 4 at 1600×900 (23.0 Mpix, within 7 % of the above) predicted **12.0 ms** against the 12.35 measured here. The empty scene at that load is **10.5 ms** (Testbed, fixed camera at `campos=0,2,45 camtarget=0,-4,0`, dome 8), so the cluster and the gun are about 1.9 ms of it.

Measured on the cavern with `level=Onion`: windowed 1600×900 at ssaa 4 reads **4.00 ms**, fullscreen 3840×1600 at ssaa 2 reads **5.96** — the stand-in is a third light, where on the mountain's real geometry it predicted 12.0 against 12.35. Use it for the terrain scenes; measure the sky-replacing four at the size they will be played at.

The instrument this needed did not exist: the Game got the Testbed's **`fpscap=N`** for it, because a vsync-capped level can only ever report "dearer than one refresh" and the whole question was how much dearer. The answer was "not dearer at all", which no vsync-capped run could have said.

**The fix was to stop vsyncing.** The game presents immediately in every mode now and holds the rate on the CPU in `Game/Platform/FrameLimiter.cs` — which is safe precisely because the game is only ever windowed or *borderless* fullscreen (`HardwareModeSwitch = false`, #157), so DWM owns the flip and composites at the panel's rate whatever the app asks for; there is no scanout to race and nothing tears. Verified on the case that was reported, at the default settings and with nothing pinned but the level (which brings its own scene and dome with it): **37.5 → 78.0 FPS, flat** — windowed first, and then **fullscreen at 3840×1600, which is where the report came from**, also 78.0 flat with the back buffer confirmed on the run's own `[fps]` line. (78 and not 75 because the limiter aims a few percent over the refresh — a limiter never pays back an overrun, so one aimed exactly at the refresh drifts slower than the compositor and periodically leaves it nothing new to show.) The limiter sleeps the bulk of each period and spins only the last two milliseconds, unlike the Testbed's benchmark-only idle which spins the lot; see its class doc for why that distinction matters on a laptop.

**What is still open:** the 12.35 ms above is real, and a 75 Hz panel allows 13.3. Fullscreen `High` therefore has about a millisecond of headroom on this machine and none at all on a slower one, so a genuine cost reduction here — or a tier that drops supersampling at 4K — is still worth having. It is now an ordinary "this is a bit dear" question rather than the ten-fold mystery it was filed as.

## The meadow (Testbed)

The sixth setting in the NumPad2 cycle (or `scene=meadow`). The Windows XP *Bliss* hill: vivid green rolling to a crisp horizon under a blue cloudy sky, a flat flowering clearing where the arena stands rolling up into hills with distance. It is the desert's and mountains' displaced-grid machinery again (shared `CreateGridMesh`), but the field is smooth rolling sines rather than dunes or ridges, low around the arena (world origin) and rising with distance, its normal by finite differences. The grass is matte, varied in broad patches so it is not one flat green, and the whole field takes the mood of the dome like everything else.

The motion is the wind and the weather, with no particle system: bands of wind comb through the grass — bright and dark travelling downwind — while the shared cloud shadows drift across the hills, and a fine wind-animated relief tilts the grass normal so the light plays over it. And it is in bloom: wildflowers are scattered through it, little rosettes rather than dots — a ring of petals around a warm golden eye, `|cos(N * angle / 2)|` cutting N rounded lobes (even in the angle, so it survives the `atan2` seam), with a per-cell petal count, size, rotation and colour (white daisies, yellow buttercups, the odd pink one). **A rosette has a shape of its own since #127, not just an albedo** — it used to be a pure colour swap under the ground's own shading, which read as a flat decal printed on the grass. Each petal is now a dome (full mid-petal, dying at the scalloped rim, dipping in the gaps) with the eye a raised boss over them, summed **into the very height field the grass relief feeds `PerturbNormalFromHeight`** — one surface, one perturbation, so a flower catches the sun and the sky hemisphere on its own form; the height is zero by construction at the cell border, so the per-cell hashes cannot tear the field where cells meet. **And the grass's own share of that field is faded out under the rosette (#283)**, which #127 did not do: summed in flat, the flower wore the ground's fine texture and whatever the gust was doing at that point, with its dome merely added on top — so the petals came out washed and blotchy instead of reading as five domes. The colour had always isolated itself correctly (`lerp(grass, flowerColor, flowerMask)` replaces outright); this is the normal being given the same treatment, and the comment above the line had claimed it for some time. **⚠ The weight is the smooth `petalProfile` and deliberately NOT `flowerMask`**: the mask is antialiased over a pixel and a half, and this weight goes into a height field that is *differentiated*, so a mask-shaped falloff puts a pixel-wide step in the derivative and lights a hard ring around every rosette — a new artefact in place of the old one. The profile is linear in radius, so its derivative is bounded by `1/petalEdge` and the crossover is invisible; it is also the same profile the dome is built on, which is what makes the two complementary — where the flower stands tallest the grass texture is gone entirely, and they trade off at the rim. It carries `resolvable` with it, so the grass's texture comes back by itself as the flowers fade out with distance. On top of the normal, a cheap occlusion gradient shades the rim of each petal against its face and brightens the eye's centre (the ambient half a hemisphere term is too broad to give), and a narrow **contact ring** darkens the grass just outside the petals, which is what makes the rosette stand *over* the grass instead of lying in it. Each flower's centre is held within `[size, 1 - size]` of its grid cell so the whole rosette fits inside and no petal is clipped by the cell edge, and the lot — heights included — fades against the pixel footprint so the distant meadow stays clean green rather than a shimmer of dots. The rosettes' form constants (`FLOWER_PETAL_RELIEF`/`FLOWER_EYE_RELIEF`/`FLOWER_CONTACT_SHADOW`, fractions of a flower's own size) live in `Meadow.fx`; the mood dials (density, spacing, size) stay on the config. Grass, hill, wind and flower dials sit at the top of `Testbed.cs`.

## The neon city (Testbed)

The last of the seven, at the end of the NumPad2 cycle (or `scene=neon`). Not a new place but the same procedural city relit: the `CityNeon` switch on `InstancedModelRenderer` flips `InstancedModel.fx`'s city technique from warm-and-cool lamps to a neon night — near-black facades and windows glowing pink and blue, the magenta and cyan of a neon street, one dominant hue per tower with the odd off-colour accent, about one window in six sparking the opposite hue, big sign bands wrapping some towers in the contrast colour, and a fraction of it all buzzing. The window brightness is driven well over `GLARE_THRESHOLD` (`NEON_WINDOW_BRIGHTNESS`), so every lit pane blooms into a sign; the facades stay dark under any dome, so the neon carries whatever the sky, but it reads best under a dark or dusk one. The `#if !OPENGL` guard the neon path used to sit behind is gone now that both executables build the file for Shader Model 5.0 — the map editor draws the city, and its neon, through the same shared technique, so V reaches the neon scene there too. It is the heaviest scene — the whole city plus the extra sign/flicker work and the bloom it feeds — so it runs the lowest frame rate. **The campaign's eighth block, the Arcade, plays here** under dome 16, the darkest of the twenty (a near-black zenith over a warm horizon), which is the pairing the facades want: they stay dark under any dome, so a bright one only fights the neon. Measured with a level loaded, the heaviest of the five (588 balls) holds a flat 60.0 FPS at High — ssaa 2×, 1600×900, vsync on, 932 of 1788 towers drawn — on the weakest machine here; what that does *not* say is how much headroom is left, because measuring a frame's real cost needs the presentation cap lifted, and that had been reported crashing the owner's machine (the journal, #228). #250 then found it is not about `nocap` at all: the desktop hard-reset twice in one afternoon under a **capped** run - `Kernel-Power 41` with no bugcheck, no WHEA and no display-driver reset, against ten unexpected shutdowns in thirty days. The instrument for the day the machine is trusted again is `fpscap=N` (`TestOptions.FpsCap`).

## The forest

The eighth `SceneKind`, a temperate woodland glade: a mossy needle-strewn clearing whose hills dress in a dark conifer canopy, scattered with procedural trees, rocks and stumps. The terrain is `Forest.fx` on the shared `SceneRenderer` (the meadow's displaced-grid machinery, cool and floorless of blooms), and **the wood standing on it is shared too** — `ForestScatter` for where every piece stands and `Prazsky.Core.Render.ForestScatterRenderer` for everything it is drawn with, both in the library since #75, drawn through the shared instanced effect. Until then the scatter lived in the Game alone, which is exactly why the Testbed and the editor used to show a bare clearing: the terrain under it was always the shared `SceneRenderer`'s, so all three had the glade and only one of them had the trees in it. Nothing about it was ever the Game's by nature; the split was an accident of where the scene was built. Like Space the scene sits past the end of the NumPad2/V cycle (`SceneRenderer.CycleLength`, which stays on the seven scenes a map is authored against), so `scene=forest` is how the Testbed reaches it and loading a forest **level** is how the editor does; the game also has it on the scene menu and in the random launch pick. Where in the frame the wood goes stays the caller's, and so does the scene gate — `ForestScatterRenderer.Draw` touches no GPU state, assumes the opaque scene state the callers already set, and draws whenever it is called, so each keeps its own `if (scene == SceneKind.Forest)` and places the draw after the terrain the trees stand on and before the island.

Two tree species split by `ConiferFraction` — a **conifer**, a spruce built as **tiers of branch whorls** (each layer a drooping `LatheMesh` skirt with a shadowed tuck beneath it — the sawtooth silhouette is what says "spruce", where the first build's smooth wobbled cone said "toy"), and a **broadleaf**, a crown of several overlapping **leaf lobes** over a base noise field (a deciduous crown is a cluster of masses; a single noise-bulged sphere still read as a ball, because gradient noise has no structure at the one scale a crown does). `RockMesh` and `StumpMesh` are lathes with strong and modest irregularity. Each kind is built at **several sets of proportions AND its own structural seed** (six spruces, four broadleaves, three boulders, two stumps): the seed rolls what proportions cannot — the spruce's tier count, taper, droop and pinch; the broadleaf's lobe count, directions and weights; and every part's wobble phase (`LatheMesh` grew an `irregularityPhase` for exactly this — without it every variant undulated identically, and **two lathes that share an edge must share the phase** or the seam opens). `ForestScatter` splits its instances between the variants, because a uniform scale and a yaw do not change a silhouette and the silhouette is what the eye counts — proportions alone made copies of one tree at three sizes, and the eye caught it. So a kind's instances come back as `ModelInstance[][]`, one array per variant, each its own instanced draw (and two per tree, since bark and foliage are two materials and the tint is per-draw) — and each foliage variant takes its own **tint multiplier on the config's linear colour** (`ForestScatterRenderer.CONIFER_TINTS`/`BROADLEAF_TINTS`, means near 1 so the config stays the authored average, encoded to sRGB only after the multiply), because a real stand is never one green. **All twenty-five of those tints are encoded once at build time**, which is the one thing the hoist changed rather than moved: the Game re-derived every one of them on every frame the forest was on screen — a `GetSceneConfig`, six `ToVector3` and a `LinearToSrgb` per variant — for values only a config edit can change, so the draw is six indexed loops with no arithmetic in them now. The cost of that is one rule to know: a caller that mutates a config **in place** has to call `ForestScatterRenderer.Replant`, or it goes on drawing the colours the old config asked for (which is why the editor's live grid re-plants — see "The scene-config panel" in `docs/formats-and-tools.md`). And the tint tables must be at least as long as their variant arrays, which nothing checked: a seventh spruce used to throw from *inside the draw*, in the one scene where the forest is on screen at all, so it would have been a crash nobody met until they picked the forest. It fails at construction now, naming both lengths. The variety is in the **mesh** rather than in a per-instance stretch: the shader transforms normals by the world matrix with no inverse transpose, so a non-uniform scale would shade a squashed tree as the shape it was authored at. A **rotation** is safe, which is why every instance also gets a small lean (a boulder a much larger tumble). Near-tip lathe rings get their wobble **capped at a share of their own radius**, or the displacement crosses the axis and turns the ring inside out. The scatter is the savanna's clumped generator, planted on the floor through `SceneRenderer.ForestTerrainHeight` — a CPU mirror of the shader's height field, and **`MathHelper.SmoothStep` is not GLSL `smoothstep`** (value-interpolate vs clamp-then-hermite); that misuse scattered the first forest six thousand units into the sky, and the same misuse inverted the first crown's bottom tuck.

**Neither scatter kept its plants out of each other until #108**, and the forest and the savanna had the bug for the same reason: positions were drawn independently, with **82 %** of them clumped around a cluster centre at a density that *rises* inwards (`off² * ClusterSpread`), and nothing anywhere compared a candidate against what was already standing. Two landing on top of each other was therefore the expected case rather than bad luck — measured on the shipped forest config (240 trees, 20 clusters, spread 34), **67 overlapping pairs, 35 of them badly**. `ScatterSpacing` is the rule in one copy for both: a packing fraction (0.72 — crowns in a real stand interlace, and a wood whose every crown clears every other reads as an orchard), a retry budget, and a signed clearance test so a position that cannot be made good is at least made *least bad*. Retries measured against that same config: 1 → 67 pairs, 2 → 11, **4 → 0**, and 8/16/32 also 0; the shipped budget is 8 as headroom for a denser config, and it costs nothing because the loop stops at the first position that clears. Nothing is ever dropped for want of room — a forest that quietly plants 180 of the 240 trees it was asked for would be the worse bug. Each caller keeps only what genuinely differs: the forest reads its radii off the crown radii the meshes are built at, the savanna off the very width `Acacia.fx` sizes the billboard with (so a bush is held to a third of a tree's room rather than all of it, which needed the tree/bush roll moved *before* the position). Two consequences to know: the trees' two species share one occupancy list (they are scattered separately for the groves, so a per-call list would have stopped each species colliding only with itself), while rocks and stumps keep their own — a boulder at the foot of a tree is what a wood looks like. And **a scatter re-rolls at an unchanged seed**, because the number of rng draws now varies with how crowded the ground is.

**The wood did not take the dome's mood, and the lighting was not what was wrong with it (#108).** Every scatter renderer is enrolled for the sky rig in all three executables, the rig tints the key light and the hemisphere ambient, and both reach the trees — proved by making the pigment a neutral grey for one run, whereupon the very same trees took a sunset dome fully, salmon-pink, with not a line of the lighting path changed. The trouble is the **pigment**: `FoliageColor` is `(0.042, 0.115, 0.026)` in linear radiance and `ConiferColor` `(0.028, 0.075, 0.026)`, whose red and blue channels are near zero — a coloured light has almost nothing to multiply there, so the crowns stayed the same green under every sky while the ground around them turned. A saturated pigment not taking a coloured light is correct physics; it is simply not the picture this scene wants, so the pigment is what moves. `ForestScatterRenderer.ApplySkyTint` carries every tint a third of the way (`SKY_TINT_STRENGTH` 0.34) towards the dome's hue — the same idea and nearly the same figure as `SkyLightRig.SKY_TINT_STRENGTH`, which lerps the key light half of the way to the horizon rather than all of it, and for the same reason: far enough short of 1 that a spruce is still a spruce. **Hue only** — the dome's tint is rescaled to the pigment's own luminance before the blend, so a dark dome cannot darken the wood nor a bright one light it. Called from the same pass that applies the rig (all three executables have one) and guarded on the value, so re-encoding the twenty-five tints happens on a dome switch and never on the frames between, which is what the tint cache exists for.

The first build's trees rendered as **black eggs sitting on the ground**, and every cause is a trap worth keeping:

- **The crown used its height as a semi-axis**, spanning `[baseY − h, baseY + h]` — the bottom pole a unit under the ground and the whole trunk buried inside the egg. A crown spans `[trunkHeight, trunkHeight + crownHeight]`, so the trunk stays visible under it.
- **Every lathe profile was traced bottom-up**, the reverse of the direction `LatheMesh` documents (top → outside → underside), so trunk, rock and stump were all **inside out** — the sphere-winding trap in its lathe form: nothing disappears, the far side is drawn with inward normals, and a shallow inside-out dome even reads *lit* (you are looking at the interior of its bottom disc, whose normal faces the sun).
- **The config colours are linear radiance, but the material-diffuse uniform is sRGB**: the instanced shader decodes `DiffuseColor` with `SrgbToLinear` at the tap (ball tints are display values), so linear-authored greens landed near black — 0.16 decoded to 0.02. The scatter draws encode once at the boundary (`ColorSpace.LinearToSrgb`); the terrain shader's own colours stay linear like every scene shader's. **And a tinted draw is not a pass-through**: `Draw` reduces the material diffuse to its luminance and multiplies by **1.25**, a boost calibrated for models whose brightest material is 0.8 — over a white material that lands every encoded colour half again too bright, so the scatter meshes are built at 0.8 (`ForestScatterRenderer.SCATTER_MATERIAL_DIFFUSE`) and the two cancel.
- **A procedural-mesh renderer defaults to a white specular** (`(1,1,1)` power 16 — right for vinyl and glass), which on a dark crown reads as wet obsidian. The scatter passes matte per-draw `BasicEffectParams` — three of them, built once in `ForestScatterRenderer` (foliage, bark and weathered stone) out of the scene's flat ambient fill, which is the only thing about the scene that component is told — and a **zero** specular there means "no override, use the white default", so matte is a dim non-zero colour, never zero.
- **A `LatheMesh` span whose first ring sits on the axis got a `+Y` face normal for the whole span.** The ring tangent was taken from the span's first ring, and a ring at radius 0 has all its segments at one point — so the cross product collapsed and the fallback fired. That is *right* for a flat top (a disc's first ring is its centre) and wrong for anything that comes to a point: the fix takes the tangent from whichever of the span's two rings is off the axis, without which the top third of every conifer shaded as though it faced the sky.
- The forest belongs in **`SceneRenderer.IsSolidTerrainScene`** — its clearing is flat ground at the island's foot like the meadow's, so the terrain shader cuts the island's footprint out and the dark pit shaft has to be drawn behind the drain's glass, or the funnel reads as a glass ring over bright sky haze. It was omitted from the hand-kept list in the game **and** from the Testbed's own, which is the exact failure a duplicated classification invites and the reason there is one shared list now (#75, above). The five are mountains, meadow, savanna, desert and forest.

The terrain took a second pass after the first build read as **blurred, low-res mounds**, and each fix names the cause:

- **The hills' sine octaves are domain-warped** before they are summed (two long sines bending the sampling plane — exact to mirror on the CPU), because summed plane waves keep their planes however many there are, and straight wavefronts are what made the hills a *row* of smooth mounds. Five octaves rather than three, the two new ones filling the gap between hill and lump scales — the band a "smooth blob with bumps on it" is missing — with amplitudes summing to 1 so `HillHeight` keeps its meaning.
- **The floor lumps run under a broad mask** and gained a third wave: unmasked, two sines interfere into an even weave across the whole floor, which reads as a pattern rather than as ground; masked, the roughness comes in patches the way roots and hollows do.
- **The base normal is per pixel now** — the savanna's fix, three `TerrainHeight` taps in the pixel shader — because interpolating the coarse displaced grid's per-vertex normal leaves a Mach band at every cell edge, and on this scene it read as the whole floor being slightly out of focus.
- **The needle relief is four octaves, band-limited per octave** (the ball relief's rule — one global fade flattens the lot at arm's length), and a **needle-scale colour grain** joins it near the camera: the relief tilts the light, but a floor with no fine *albedo* change still looks airbrushed however it is lit. Both fade to nothing before they can shimmer.
- **The near slopes' canopy gained a third, crown-top mottle scale** under its own band limit — the two broad scales alone read soft-focus exactly where the hills fill the frame.
- Measured on the APU (front end, 1600×900, `quality=high`, dome 13, 16 s runs): **17.2 → 20.0 ms**. The +2.8 ms is mostly the per-pixel normal's three field taps; the forest stays inside the terrain-scene band (the mountain is 19.1) and above the adaptive probe's floor on this machine.
- **The floor's second pass — FBM mottle, triplanar normal variation, slope-driven colour and procedural tree shadows — is the most expensive thing on this scene, and it was not free.** Measured on the desktop GPU (front end, 1600×900, `quality=High`, dome 13, `nocap`, two 18 s runs each): **540 → 371 FPS**, i.e. 1.85 → 2.70 ms, **+0.84 ms and −31 %**. That is at windowed 1600×900; the work is per-pixel on the floor, so it scales with resolution and 4K is the stated base.
- **What that cost actually is: occupancy, not work — and it took a full attribution sweep to find out (#118).** Removing any *one* piece saves **nothing at all**: without the triplanar normal variation 2.71 ms, without the tree shadows 2.72, with the floor's FBM cut from four octaves to two 2.71, against the full 2.69. Removing *pairs* saves a great deal — normal variation and shadows together 2.09, normal variation and the mottle's FBM 1.97, all three 1.85. A cost with that shape is not per-feature work being done; it is the pass sitting below an occupancy threshold that only a pair of removals climbs back over. **So a per-feature quality dial would have bought nothing**, and neither does a runtime branch: a `float FloorDetail` uniform with two `[branch]`es on it measured **2.72 against the full look's 2.70**, because the register footprint is fixed when the shader is compiled — the branch skips the work and keeps the registers that were the actual limit.
- **Hence a second compiled program.** `Forest.fx` has two techniques over one body (`ForestFloor(input, detail)`, called with a literal from `ForestPS` and `ForestReducedPS` — MGFX cannot parse a uniform argument in a `compile` statement, so it is two entry points rather than the tidier `compile … ForestPS(true)`). `SceneRenderer.SceneDetail` picks between them and the Game sets it from the tier: **`Low` drops both extras and `High` and `Medium` keep the authored floor** (it was everything below `High` until #298 — see "The quality tier" in `docs/game-shell.md`). Measured with supersampling pinned at 2× so only the technique differs: **High 2.69 ms, Medium 2.10** — the reduced program recovers 0.59 of the 0.84 the pass added, and `High` is untouched. The Testbed and the map editor leave `SceneDetail` at its default 1, since they are where the scene is looked at and tuned.
- A trap this set on the way: `SceneDetail` began as a plain auto-property, and `ApplyForestParameters` — which runs from the constructor and on a config change — had already chosen the technique by the time any host set it, so the reduced program was compiled, shipped and **never once drawn**, at every tier, while looking perfectly correct. It measured as "the technique switch saves nothing", which is the same reading as the uniform branch above and would have been believed. Caught by making the reduced technique output flat red for one run: the floor stayed green. The property re-selects on write now.
- **The floor colour answers the form now**: `slope` (from `baseNormal.y`) washes the flats towards moss and the banks towards bare earth and litter. Without it the floor reads as one material however it is lit, because the colour never answers the shape it sits on. The four-octave `ForestFbm` replaces the single `CloudNoise` tap the patches and litter used to read off — one octave reads as smooth blobs where four sum to broken ground.
- **The tree shadows are cast by trees that do not exist.** The terrain shader does not know where the scatter's ~240 instances stand, and passing them in would be a limit and a cost; `ForestShadow` plants a *virtual* tree per hash-grid cell instead and marches the sun ray across the cells. The two never argue about who shadows whom because the shadow's density rides `canopyRamp` — 0 inside the clearing, where the real trees stand and only their own shadow should show.

The wooded hills are **colour, not geometry**: past the clearing the floor dresses in a dark canopy mottle (`TreelineColor`, grove- and crown-scale noise) with its **own ramp, nearer and tighter than the hills'** — the slopes that fill the frame are the transition band itself, and a canopy keyed to the full hill ramp only arrives where the haze already owns the colour. Its edge rides the grove noise so the woods begin on a ragged line. The horizon haze is **two-stage**: distant forested ridges turn *blue* (the air scatters skylight), so the hills recede first into a zenith-derived murk and only the last stretch melts into `HorizonColor` — reaching it exactly at `HorizonHazeDistance`, which must stay at or inside the terrain grid's half-extent or the mesh edge shows against the dome. And **`CloudNoise` is gradient noise, which clusters hard around zero**: measured over four million samples, one sigma is 0.18, the 5–95 band is ±0.30 and only the extremes approach ±0.8 — so the usual `* 0.5 + 0.5` spans about 0.35–0.65 and the ACES curve flattens that into one tone. Spread it by ~1.6 (and saturate) before using it as a mottle, here and anywhere else a noise has to *read* on screen.

## Space (Game)

The ninth `SceneKind`: the island floats in deep space under a long-exposure astrophoto sky — a dense starfield, the Milky Way with its dust lanes, three coloured emission nebulae, scattered distant galaxies, one large banded planet, and a **volume the island is inside of**. Physically an eye in orbit would see mostly black; this is deliberately the *picture* of space, which is what the eye recognises as space. `Space.fx` on the shared `SceneRenderer`, so all three executables compile it; only the Game can select it (`ScenePage`, the random pick, `scene=space`), and the Testbed reaches it with `scene=space` too — the Testbed's NumPad2 and the editor's V both still stop at `SceneRenderer.CycleLength`, as they do past the forest.

**It was the first backdrop to replace the SKY rather than the ground — the dream and the cavern followed it, then the Moon (which alone keeps a ground under its replaced sky), and `SceneRenderer.ReplacesSky` is now the four of them — and every difference below follows from that one fact.** The other eight leave the dome standing over their ground; space, the dream and the cavern have no ground, no horizon and no weather.

- **One full-screen pass, not a displaced grid.** A four-corner quad already in normalized device coordinates, the view ray recovered per pixel from an `InverseViewProjection` uniform (`Matrix.Invert(View * Projection)` — row vectors, as everywhere here), drawn `DepthStencilState.None` so it writes no depth and the island, cluster and gun simply cover it. It covers the **whole** frame rather than a hemisphere, which is why the clear colour goes to black in this scene and the wide-aspect corner band the other scenes clear the horizon for cannot happen. No `OriginXZ` (nothing on the ground to swim), no `IslandHoleRadius` (nothing drawn under the island), no cloud hook.
- **One layer of it is marched through, and every other one is not — which is what the scene was missing.** Everything above `StarNestVolume` in `Space.fx` is a function of the view **direction** alone; the camera's position enters the shader only to build the ray. So the whole sky was a painted dome: move the camera and not one pixel changed, turn it and the picture slid rigidly, near and far together. A starfield is entitled to that (real stars have no parallax worth drawing) but it left the scene with no depth of its own to be inside of, and it read as a texture rather than as a place. The volume is stepped along the ray **from the camera's own position**, so structure one step out crosses the frame faster than structure eight steps out — parallax, from real depth. Measured directly: two frames taken along the *same* view direction from positions 45 units apart differ by a mean **|ΔRGB| of 39.8** over 14 000 samples, and since every other layer is direction-only by construction that difference is entirely the volume. Before it, the same test is exactly zero.
  - The field is Pablo Roman Andrioli's **Star Nest** (MIT, so usable rather than merely admirable). Space is folded into mirrored cells by the triangle wave `2T·|frac(p/2T) − 0.5|`, which repeats the volume for free and mirrors at every wall so no seam shows — **spelled with `frac` since #147, not as the original's GLSL `abs(T − mod(p, 2T))`**: GLSL `mod` is floor-based where HLSL `fmod` truncates toward zero, so the straight port stopped mirroring below zero and **jumped by the full 2T period at every `p = −2T·k`, k ≥ 1**. The drift walks `p.z` down through those planes — the first crossing is derived to enter the marched span between 483 and 772 s at the shipped ~33-unit camera, a circle per march step sweeping the sky over its window — and it was photographed for #147 with `SpaceTime` temporarily pinned to 661 s, where all eight circles land within half a degree of one great circle (derived) and stack into one hard, frame-crossing brightness step. The fix leaves the look the layer was tuned at untouched (a pinned before/after pair at t = 30 s differs by no more than one display code anywhere in the sky) and at 900/1800 s keeps the authored statistics — the sky's darkest percentile within two codes, its mean within 9 % (0.5 % at 900 s, 8.2 % at 1800 s) — so `VOLUME_HAZE`, `Strength`, `Opacity` and the tint were re-judged against the void and deliberately kept. Cost, measured at the repro vantage (1920×1080, `ssaa 2`, `nocap`, the desktop RX 6900 XT, two interleaved runs each, 18 readings per run): **442.2/442.4 FPS with `frac` against 436.1/437.1 with `fmod`** — the correct fold is also marginally the cheaper one (the march kernel compiles to 123 instruction slots against 124, d3dcompiler ps_5_0 /O3: `fmod` pays a sign-select for its truncation). Inside a cell an iterated map `p = abs(p)/dot(p,p) − FORMULA` runs — a sphere inversion, a fold into the positive octant, a translate — whose attractor is a filigree of sheets. What is drawn is not where the point lands but **how far it moved** at each iteration, summed: points near the attractor move little, so the sum is an inside-out distance field and the filaments come out as dark tracery inside a glowing web. Colour comes from `(s, s², s⁴)` along the ray, so the near web is cool and the far one warm, which is a cheap stand-in for scattering and most of why the field reads as having a front and a back.
  - **Four things had to change for it to belong to this scene rather than replace it.** It is entered at the camera (above). It is rotated into a basis of its own, or the fold's cell walls read as a grid squared up with the island and the gun. The world is scaled down hard on the way in (`Volume.Scale`, the parallax dial) — the cells are 0.85 units wide in the field's space against an island 26 across and a camera 33 out, so raw world coordinates would fly the eye through thousands of cells and the whole thing would boil. And it carries an **extinction**, which the original needs none of because it draws nothing behind itself; here a bright web that lets every star shine through its densest knots reads as a decal over the sky. The extinction comes off the emission's own luminance rather than a second accumulator, so it cannot disagree with what is drawn, and it is a reciprocal so it can never go negative however hard the strength is turned up.
  - **The flat haze term is the one number that decides whether this is an addition or a takeover.** The original's `v += fade` adds a constant lift to every pixel — the series sums to about 3.7 — and at full weight it took the void from the 3–4 display codes this scene is authored around to a uniform grey that swallowed the Milky Way's band and both bright nebulae whole. Star Nest is free to do that because nothing else is in its frame. Here it is kept at `VOLUME_HAZE` = 0.22, and its job is the thin stuff *between* the filaments and nothing more.
  - **Cost, and why the counts are constants rather than dials.** They multiply, and a dial invites a setting that drops the frame off a cliff. The original marches 20 steps of 17 iterations — 340 evaluations of the formula for every pixel of the frame, a Shadertoy's budget and not a backdrop's. Measured from one vantage at `ssaa=2` against the painted sky this was added to (**60.4 FPS**): 11×12 gives **29.5** (+105 % frame time, a backdrop taking the frame over); the shipped **8×10** gives **36.5** (+65 %), and the two are not distinguishable side by side. Steps are the cheaper knob and are deliberately *not* cut further: each step's contribution is multiplied by `VOLUME_DISTANCE_FADING`, so `0.76ⁿ` has already spent 93 % of its total by the eighth step — but the march's depth is precisely what the layer exists for, so cutting it spends the feature to save its own cost. `Volume.Strength` of **0 skips the march outright** behind a uniform branch and restores the scene bit-for-bit to its painted-dome self, which is the lever a weak machine or a quality tier should reach for.
- **`DrawSpace` is the one scene draw that touches the DEPTH state**, so it restores `DepthStencilState.Default` on the way out. Left at `None` the island would not occlude the cluster and the whole frame would draw in submission order.
- **No weather, and saying so is not optional.** `InstancedModel.fx` calls `CloudSunlight` unconditionally, and the parameters persist on the shared effect between frames — so a coverage gain left standing from the scene before would go on shadowing the cluster with a deck that is not in the frame. `CloudField.SuppressOn` pushes a gain of zero (the value `CloudSunlight` reads as "full sun"), through the same cached parameter slots `ApplyTo` uses. The dome and the cloud deck are simply not drawn.
- **It states its own light rig instead of taking the dome's** (`SpaceLightingConfig`, handed over by `SceneRenderer.TryGetLightRig` — which the sky-replacing scenes do **as a group**, the dream and the cavern for the same reason and each with its own colours; **all three** executables honour it, the map editor included, because loading a *level* sets its `_scene` from the level's own scene kind and a space level previewed with the wrong sun is the one thing that editor exists to prevent), and the reason is not purity. The sea and savanna trick of *defaulting to a suitable dome* fails twice here: the key tint is `lerp(white, horizon, SkyLightRig.SKY_TINT_STRENGTH)`, so reaching for the darkest dome to get a dark sky **also halves the sun** and leaves the scene grey and flat — the exact opposite of what it is for; and the drain's gold beads are drawn **metallic**, so their reflectance is almost entirely the hemisphere ambient, and taking that to near-black turns the two rings that mark the drain into black ones. So: a low, cold, deliberately **non-zero** ambient. Zero is not an option either — the balls are emissive and would survive it, but the island, the gun and the glass are not, and at zero their shadow sides reach code 0 and the island reads as a hole cut in the starfield. Measured with the shipping numbers: island top **156–177 codes** — which is the band this file records for the island in the city, so it still looks like itself and only its shadows changed. Under the *same* dome 13 the city's island measures 114 and its gold bead 52–62, against space's 159 and 95–102: space is the **brighter** of the two, because its sun is not being halved by that dome's dark horizon. Planet's lit side 199, its night side 22, the void 3–4.
- **Space forces no dome at all**, unlike the sea and the savanna. Its dome is neither drawn nor read, so it is completely inert in this scene — and silently changing the player's dome to no visible effect would be a side effect rather than a setting.
- **Planetshine is a scene point light, not more ambient.** Ambient is directionless, so raising it to get a coloured flank flattens everything instead; a real light also puts a highlight back into the metallic beads. It stands `PlanetshineDistance` out along the planet's own direction with a range three times that, so across a 26-unit island the `(1 - d/range)²` falloff varies by a few per cent and reads as directional.
- **`SceneRenderer.OpenBelow` includes space**, so the drop cinematic dives under the island and watches the balls pour out of the drain against the starfield. Nothing is down there to hide them; and it is **not** an `IsSolidTerrainScene`, so it gets no pit shaft — the drain looks straight through onto the stars, which is the best thing in the scene.

The sky itself, and the things that decide whether it reads:

- **Everything SMALL is kept UNDER `GLARE_THRESHOLD` (0.55 on luminance), and the brightest stars carry their spikes in the shader instead.** This is forced, not timid: `DrawGlare` binds the *supersampled* scene target into a **half**-back-buffer bright pass — a 4:1 reduction per axis at `ssaa 2` (it was 8:1 before the bloom pyramid, which is when this rule was learned) — taken with a single bilinear tap, so a one-pixel star over the threshold is still sampled *stochastically* and can pop in and out of the glare as the camera turns, which reads as a fault rather than as a star. The pyramid halved the problem; it did not repeal the rule. **The one deliberate exception is the planet's lit limb**, and the difference is size and coherence rather than brightness: the limb is a smooth arc hundreds of pixels long, so the sparse grid lands on it many times over and it blooms steadily — which is what a planet's atmosphere should do — while the disc's own body stays under, or the planet stops being an object and becomes a lamp.
- **A spike must be a MAX with the star's core, never an addition** — the review caught this. Added, the two arms cross at the centre and *double* the peak, so a full-magnitude spiked star reached ~0.97 luminance: nearly twice the threshold, making the one thing in this sky the glare samples stochastically the very thing the in-shader spikes exist to avoid. A max also reads correctly as physics — a diffraction spike is the star's own light spread out, not extra light. Useful corollary: the threshold is on **luminance**, so a saturated hue can be far brighter than a neutral one before it blooms — the red nebula peaks at 0.78 in its red channel for a luminance of 0.32, while the same authored number in teal would be twice as close to the edge.
- **Stars sit one per cell of a cube-face lattice and only ONE cell is ever sampled.** The star is jittered inside its cell but held clear of the edges by however far it actually reaches, so it can never straddle a boundary and the eight neighbours never have to be looked at — the meadow's wildflowers solve their clipping the same way. Three layers (coarse/bright with spikes, middle, fine) give the magnitude spread; brightness is `hash^Falloff` with a steep exponent, because real star counts climb hard towards the faint end and a field of evenly bright dots reads as noise. That no-straddle guarantee rests on the face edge landing **ON** a cell wall, which only an integer cell scale gives — a non-integer one drops the seam mid-cell and a star centred just past it is drawn up to the seam then simply stops, a C0 step of up to `peak · magnitude` along all twelve cube edges; the four `CellScale` properties enforce the integer by rounding on set (#149), so neither the map editor's property grid nor a level saved with a detuned value can break it.
- **That margin is subtracted from BOTH ends of the cell, so it is what decides whether the lattice shows** — and #88 was it showing. A star may only land in the middle `1 - 2 · margin` of its cell, and under about half a cell of that box the spacing between neighbours stops reading as random. The margin is in **cell units** while the star is sized in **pixels**, so the box closes as the cells get smaller on screen: the same sky is scattered at 4K and a grid on a laptop, which is why this went unseen for so long and why every figure here is per-resolution. Two things were charging far more margin than they needed, both since fixed:
  - **`spikes` was a per-LAYER flag, so every star in the coarse layer paid the spike reach.** A spiked star needs `StarSpikeLength · 2.5` = 17.5 core radii against a plain star's ~3 — 5.8× — and only **4.2 %** of the layer is over `SpikeThreshold`. Deciding it per star, before the margin, takes the coarse layer's capped share from **96.8 % to 1.4 %** at 1366×768 and **46.9 % to 1.6 %** at 1920×1080 on the menu's 60° camera; its median jitter box goes from 0.33 to 0.90 of a cell.
  - **The plain cutoff was a flat three core radii for every star in every layer**, the same distance for a 0.50-peak star at full magnitude as for a faint one at a hundredth of it. It is now solved per star from the star's own peak — `exp(-r²) · peak · magnitude = STAR_CUT`, 1e-4 linear, a third of one 8-bit code — which lands at about 2.2 radii typically. That is what the **fine** layer needed: its cells are only ~10 px across at 1920×1080/60°, where three radii of a 0.16-peak star is a third of the cell. Capped share **91 % → 42 %** there. It costs one `sqrt` and no extra transcendental, because `magnitude` is spelled `exp(Falloff · log(roll))` and the margin shares that logarithm.
  - **Still open at the bottom of the ladder**: the fine layer is 66.8 % capped at 1366×768/60° and 32.2 % at 1366×768/43°. Closing that needs more than a one-cell lookup, so it was left rather than papered over. Two smaller findings from the same measurement pass, also left alone: `Hash33`'s components 0 and 2 share a factor, so selecting on the existence roll tilts one jitter axis by about ±6 % across the cell (χ² 144 on 9 dof over 87 k cells — real, but nothing next to a squeezed box); and stars near a face edge were drawn elongated, because the profile was a circle in **chart** space and `uv = tan(angle)` stretches radially by `sec θ` more than tangentially — **fixed in #87**, below.
- **The galaxies had the very defect the star code documents as fixed.** `Galaxies` placed its smudge with `clamp(rollA.yz, margin, 1 - margin)` where `StarLayer` remaps — and a clamp piles every roll outside the box onto the two margin lines themselves, which at that layer's margins is about a fifth of all galaxies sitting on four straight lines per cell. It remaps now, like the stars.
- **The pixel footprint is measured on the DIRECTION, never on the chart.** The cube chart is `uv = tan(angle)`, so `duv/dangle = 1 + uv²` — which depends only on `|uv|`, is therefore continuous across all twelve face seams, and is what converts the direction-space footprint into cell units. `fwidth()` of the chart coordinate instead jumps by a whole cell at every seam and rings the sky with twelve lines of wrongly sized stars. The footprint itself is the **Frobenius norm** of the screen-to-direction Jacobian (`sqrt(dot(ddx,ddx)+dot(ddy,ddy))`), not `fwidth`'s L1 sum — which makes it isotropic in the camera's bearing (#150), so the core does not swell and shrink as the lens turns.
- **Everything on the lattice is drawn round in the SKY, not round in the chart (#87).** The same `uv = tan(angle)` that makes the jacobian well-behaved makes the chart *anisotropic*: per chart unit the view direction turns by `cos²θ` radially but only `cos θ` tangentially. Sizing a star in `pixelCells` (which carries `sec²θ`) therefore gets its **radial** angular size exactly right and leaves its **tangential** one a factor `sec θ` too large — so a circle drawn in the chart is an ellipse on the sky, 1.41:1 at the middle of a face edge and **1.73:1 at a face corner**, with every star in a neighbourhood combed the same way about the face centre. Measured on the free camera at 1366×768/72°, median star axis ratio over a few hundred blobs: face centre **1.22**, edge **1.51**, corner **1.54** — against **1.28 / 1.28 / 1.35** after, i.e. the face-to-corner elongation gradient falls from 0.32 to 0.07 and what is left is blob quantisation rather than direction. `StarLayer` measures the corrected distance as the closed form `dot(offset, offset)·J − dot(offset, chart.xy)²`, which is exactly `radial² + tangential²·sec²θ` with no normalize, no divide and no branch, and which reduces to the plain `dot(offset, offset)` it replaced at a face centre. `Galaxies` needs the corrected **vector** rather than a distance, because it builds an ellipse of its own on top, so it pays one `rsqrt`; its `size` keeps riding the jacobian, since the `J` in the corrected offset and the `J` in `size` cancel out of the radius. Cost of both together, measured pinned on a corner at 1366×768 with `nocap`: **958.4 FPS before against 965.1 after** (34 readings each over two runs) — inside run-to-run variation, which is what four ALU operations in a pass that already marches a volume should be. Since #148 the spike **arms** are round in the sky too, and the margin is **per axis**: the arms are axis-aligned, so they do not need the corrected vector this bullet once sketched for them — the per-axis factor `sqrt(jacobian − chart.xy²)` (= `sqrt(1+c_⊥²)` per axis, because the jacobian the reach already carries cancels against the jacobian in the chart-to-angle conversion) measures the same anisotropy one chart axis at a time for two sqrts and no basis; and each axis's margin divides its own angular reach by its own factor, which keeps every profile short of the cell wall while buying back tangential jitter room where the 0.34 cap binds.
- **The seam #87 was filed for is SETTLED, and it was never a seam: the lattice draws five times as many stars per steradian at a cube corner as at a face centre.** The chart is uniform in **area** and the sky is not — `uv = tan θ` puts the solid-angle element at `dA · J^-1.5`, so a cell at a corner (`J = 3`) covers a fifth of the sky a cell at a face centre covers, while `chance` was a flat per-cell constant with nothing compensating. Density therefore ran **5.196× at the eight corners and 2.83× along the middle of the twelve edges**, and what the eye assembles out of eight knots joined by twelve bands is *the cube* — reported, correctly, as its edges showing. **This is why three passes could not find it.** #87, #88 and #148 all corrected the star's *shape*, and all three were looking for a **discontinuity**: the no-straddle guarantee, the elongation, the arm cut, the facet mismatch. Density is perfectly smooth and continuous across every seam — there is no line anywhere in the field — so a search for a step cannot reach it. It is a gradient whose ridges merely happen to lie along the edges. The paragraph this replaces had the mechanism written down as an aside ("cells per steradian run about 5× a face centre's at a corner") and went on measuring the shape; the aside was the whole fault.
  - **The existence roll now carries the solid angle of its own cell** — `chance · STAR_DENSITY_GAIN · rsqrt(J³)`, evaluated at the **cell centre** and not at the pixel, because the roll has to be one answer for the whole cell or a star is cut in half along an invisible contour. `STAR_DENSITY_GAIN` is `4 / (4π/6) = 1.910`, the chart area of a face over its solid angle: weighting alone would have left the sky 52.4 % of its stars, and this puts the count back so the field only stops piling up. Four ALU and one `rsqrt` per cell, and strictly *fewer* stars evaluated at the corners than before — **no FPS figure is claimed, because none was measured.**
  - **Measured before and after, same 200×200 patch at frame centre, same camera at the origin, `scene=moon nopost arena=none`, only the aim moved**: at the cube corner `(1,1,1)` **4013** lit pixels against the face centre `(0,1,0)`'s **1244** — **3.23×** — becoming **2276 against 2008**, i.e. **1.13×**. The corner figure reads under the closed form's 5.196 because a 200-pixel patch spans about 8° of a gradient that falls off fast and because lit-pixel counting saturates where stars overlap; the direction and the size of the correction are the point. Verified on the space scene at the same corner, which drives the same include with denser layers.
  - **`SpaceStarsConfig.MaxChance` (0.523) exists because of this and is arithmetic, not taste.** A face centre now rolls against `1.91 × chance`, so anything above `1 / 1.91` saturates *there* while the corners still thin out — which is the corner knot back again, in the one place the correction exists to remove it. All three chances clamp on set, like the cell scales above them: the map editor's panel edits them live and a shader has no way to complain. A denser sky wants a finer `CellScale`.
  - What the earlier analyses argued over — whether the single-cell profile cut reads at the eight corners or along the whole twelve-edge rim — is **moot for the report**, since the density gradient explains the observation on its own and its removal flattens it. The cut is still there by construction (`StarLayer` samples one cell, the margin caps at 0.34), and `pixelAngle` is the Frobenius norm of the screen-to-direction Jacobian since #150 rather than the L1 sum that over-measured the footprint by √2 to 2 with the camera's bearing. Neither has been photographed. The fine-layer cap shares quoted above (66.8 % / 32.2 %) were measured before #150 and over-state how close that layer sits to the cap.
- **A star's core is sized in OUTPUT pixels, not texels and not radians** (hence `SceneRenderer.SupersampleFactor`, which each executable sets — the Game's moves with the quality tier). Sized in texels the same star comes out four times dimmer at 2× than at 1×, i.e. the same sky looking different on two quality settings. `Spread` ships at exactly the shader's own anti-aliasing floor (0.62 texels), which is what makes the two factors *measurably* the same rather than roughly: at 0.45 the floor took over at 1× and drew the stars 38 % wider there. Measured over one star field, mean radiance 8.37 at 1× against 7.05 at 2× — a 19 % difference where getting this wrong is a 4× one. **In vacuum a star must not twinkle** — there is no atmosphere to make it — so any shimmer here is an artifact and never a feature. A brighter star is drawn a little *wider* as well as brighter, because no optics resolve a star as a point and a field of equal-width dots reads as a texture.
- **The Milky Way's elongation is mild, and the first build proved why.** Squashing the galactic latitude by five and sampling at a third of the shipping frequencies gave long smeared streaks that read as **motion blur across the sky**, at a brightness (0.30) that tonemapped to a 75 %-grey wash burying the stars it is supposed to be made of. Elongation says "in a plane"; too much of it says "something is broken". It ships at a squash of **1.6** over a cloud frequency of 13 (2.2 over 9 still read airbrushed), `Brightness` 0.115, and most of the band's weight carried by `StarBoost` — which is the right way round, since a Milky Way *is* stars.
- **Dust must dim the stars as well as the glow.** The dark rifts are dark because opaque dust stands in front, so a lane that dims the band but leaves the starfield shining through reads as a stripe painted over the sky. The nebulae do the same through a transmittance the pixel shader carries out of them — without it a nebula is a glow decal with the whole starfield coming out of its densest knots.
- **Domain warping is the single biggest quality lever in the nebulae.** At 0 they are blobs of cloud; at 1.5 they are filaments and cavities. And the density has to be spread hard about its own mean (`(body - 0.44) * 2.9`) before it is used, for the reason the forest's canopy and the meadow's flowers both learned: a timid mottle is exactly what the ACES curve flattens into one tone.
- **The planet is solved analytically in the same pass**, not drawn: a unit sphere at the distance that gives the configured angular radius, so the ray test is a quadratic and the surface normal falls out of it. It is lit by the very `SunDirection` the island and the balls take, which is what ties it to the frame instead of leaving it a sticker on the sky. Its direction decides the **phase** as well as the framing — the lit fraction is `(1 - dot(Direction, SunDirection)) / 2`, and the dot wants to land in −0.4…−0.7 (it ships at −0.51, a fat gibbous): a full disc is flat and shows nothing of the terminator, a thin crescent hides the bands. The terminator is a `smoothstep`, never a hard `N·L` cut, and the night side keeps `NightAmbient` or the planet reads as a hole punched in the starfield.
- **The storm oval is placed by a DIRECTION, and the first version stamped it twice.** `(dot(n, forward), latitude)` is satisfied by two points, one either side of the forward-pole plane, so the oval appeared mirrored — and half of all placements put the ghost on the very face being drawn. Measured as a tangential offset from a centre direction with the antipode rejected (`step(0, dot(n, centre))`), there is exactly one of it. The same trap waits for anything else placed on a sphere by a pair of projections.
- **Angles are authored in DEGREES in the config** and converted where they are pushed at the shader, and the galactic core is **orthogonalised against the pole** on the way through, so a hand-typed pair never has to be exactly perpendicular for the bulge to sit in the plane. Directions go through a `SafeNormal` with a fallback, because these are values typed into a property grid where a zero is one keystroke away and a NaN direction would take the whole sky with it.
- **The config uses named nested objects, never arrays** (`NebulaOne`/`Two`/`Three`, `Stars`, `MilkyWay`, `Galaxies`, `Planet`, `Lighting`) — the map editor's `PropertyGrid` is built `IgnoreCollections = true`, so a list would be invisible in the live scene-config panel.
- Cost is a full-screen procedural pass, so the expensive parts are gated on **uniform-coherent branches** (`[branch]` on a nebula's angular falloff, on the Milky Way's band mask, on the planet's disc) and the footprint is taken **once at the top of the pixel shader**, before any of them — a gradient operation inside a divergent branch is undefined, and that ordering is the only reason those branches are safe. Measured frame cost is in "The quality tier".

## The dream (Game)

The tenth `SceneKind`, and the second that **replaces the SKY** (see Space above — the full-screen-quad machinery, the view ray through `InverseViewProjection`, `DepthStencilState.None` with `Default` restored on the way out, no dome, no weather, black clear, its own light rig — is all the same, and the two share one quad). Where space is the *picture of space*, this is the picture of a hallucination: slow marbled colour flowing across the whole sphere of the sky, hard glassy solids tumbling and melting into one another, soft luminous orbs breathing through the murk, fast sparks whipping between them. `Dream.fx` + `DreamSceneConfig` (named nested groups — Palette, Background, Shapes, Glows, Lighting — the PropertyGrid rule), `scene=dream` on either command line, on the Game's scene menu and in its random pick.

It is deliberately a scene of **contrasts**, and each pair is carried by a different element: **sharp against blurred** — the solids are raymarched surfaces with crisp silhouettes and fresnel rims, the orbs are pure closest-approach gaussians with no edge at all; **fast against slow** — the background marbling drifts over minutes while the sparks cross the sky in seconds (and the marbling itself carries both: thin ribbons race through the slow broad flow, `pow`-sharpened so only their crests survive). Decisions that hold it together:

- **Everything is analytic 3D** — fields evaluated on the view direction, ray-sphere tests, closest-approach glows — and never a 2D chart of the sphere, so there are no pole seams and nothing hides them. The marbling is **twice-warped fractal noise** (`Noise.fxh` — see "The noise library" in `docs/rendering.md`): the first build was plane-wave sines under a sine warp and read as the plasma effect of a 1994 demo, because summed plane waves keep their planes however many there are; two fBm warp layers produce the filaments and mixing of real turbulence, the time rides *inside* the domains so the fluid evolves rather than scrolls, and the **warp intermediates shade the colour** (where the domain was dragged furthest, the fluid "mixed") — most of why it reads as a substance instead of a pattern. The solids carry **SDF ambient occlusion** (four probes up the normal), so the torus's inner ring and the melt seams sit visibly deeper than the open faces — emission dims in the folds, and a hallucination still has depth.
- **One cosine palette** (`colour(t) = A + B·cos(2π(C·t + D))`) is the scene's whole colour identity: every element — marbling, ribbons, solids, orbs, sparks — indexes the same ramp at its own phase, which is what keeps a frame full of saturated colour reading as one hallucination rather than as a box of crayons.
- **The solids morph as distance fields**: each melts between a sphere, a rounded box and a torus on its own slow cycle (half the solids cycle sphere↔box, half box↔torus), and the lerp of SDFs is what makes the change a *melt* rather than a swap. Each is lit by the dream itself — its palette colour glowing from inside (with a **high emission floor**, or a solid whose palette phase lands dark vanishes into the marbling as a silhouette; a hallucination has no unlit objects) plus the marbled sky **mirrored off its surface**.
- **The march is gated per shape**: each solid carries an analytic bounding sphere, the ray is tested against those first (eight quadratics), and only a crossed bound is sphere-traced, inside its own `[t0, t1]` interval. Most pixels march nothing, which is what makes a raymarched element affordable in a full-screen pass.
- **The solids write no depth** (the whole pass draws behind the scene), so they must roam **well outside anything the camera can stand near** (`OrbitRadius` 130 against the island's 26 and the menu orbit's 44) — a solid that drifted between the lens and the island would draw *behind* it.
- **The background stays well under the glare threshold** (`Brightness` 0.24 — at 0.32 it washed out everything hung on it): it is the canvas. The **orbs are allowed over** deliberately (smooth areas hundreds of pixels wide bloom steadily — the planet's lit-limb reasoning); the **sparks stay at the threshold's edge** and read fast through their three-sample trails instead (a small point over the threshold is sampled stochastically by the glare's sparse grid and flickers, which reads as a fault).
- **The light rig is deliberately coloured** (`DreamLightingConfig`: violet sky ambient over teal ground bounce, a rose key against a cyan fill), so the island, the gun and the balls sit *in* the hallucination instead of standing greyly in front of it. `SceneRenderer.OpenBelow` includes the dream (the drop cinematic dives under the island against the flowing sky); it is **not** an `IsSolidTerrainScene`, so no pit shaft.
- **Measured** (front end, 1600×900, dome 13, vsync off, the APU): **61.7 ms at `High`, 18.8 at `Medium`** — `High` is a desktop setting now, deliberately (the twice-warped marbling was bought at full price), and the adaptive probe steps this class of hardware to `Medium`, where it holds 53 FPS. Before the fractal rework the sine version measured 26.9/9.7 — the plasma look was cheap, which is much of why things look like it. **At fullscreen `High` on the reference 6900 XT it measured 18.6 ms (53.7 FPS) until #155**, which is #167's complaint; it is now shaded at the back buffer's size and scaled up like the cavern — **5.8 ms, 172.5 FPS** — see the cavern's own entry for the measurement and for why the trade costs the look nothing.
- **Occupancy-bound like the other two heavy scenes, with one exception that matters (#103).** Attribution on the desktop GPU (front end, 1600×900, dome 13, `nocap`, `High` at 4.40 ms): dropping `Background()` from the reflection **4.42 ms**, sparks 14 → 4 **4.47**, each spark's trail 3 → 1 sample **4.43** — nothing, all three. Cutting an octave off both warp layers **4.08**, which *does* pay, and it is the only reduction in any of these three scenes that pays alone: it is the only one evaluated on **every pixel** rather than only where a solid was hit. Pairs behave as everywhere else — reflection and sparks together 3.62, reflection and trail 3.72, all four **3.15**.
- **So the dream has a reduced program too**, taking all four: the reflection mirrors a flat `DeepColor` (the solids are rounded and semi-matte, and a first-order warp is already past what a reflection at that curvature resolves), the sparks drop to 4 with a single trail sample, and both warp layers lose an octave. Measured with supersampling pinned so only the technique differs: **`High` 4.39 ms, reduced 2.84**. On the real `Medium` the desktop figure goes **1.21 → 0.81 ms**. `High` is untouched.

## The cavern (Game)

The eleventh `SceneKind`, and the third sky-replacing pass (Space's machinery again, the shared quad): a **bioluminescent crystal cavern over an underground river** — deep noise-carved rock veined with glowing minerals, god rays falling from unseen gaps in the ceiling, sharp crystal clusters pulsing cyan and magenta on the walls, spores drifting up through the dark, and the river carrying their light on its ripple (it mirrored the whole cave until #250 traded that away). `Cavern.fx` + `CavernSceneConfig` (named nested groups — Rock, Water, Air, Crystals, Lighting), `scene=cavern` on either command line, on the Game's scene menu and in its random pick.

The scene is **analytic except where marching is gated**, and each layer has one trick worth keeping:

- **The cave shell is a quadratic and a plane** — an infinite cylinder and a ceiling, hit analytically — but shaded as **lit, folded stone**: a 3D fBm's gradient (three extra taps, `Noise.fxh`) perturbs the shell's smooth normal, and every light in the scene — a cool key from the ceiling gaps, and **each crystal as a real point light, N·L over inverse square** — works against that perturbed normal, so the rock around a magenta cluster is *lit from the cluster's side*, bumps shadowing away from it. The first build had only a flat colour mottle, and a wall whose colour varies while its light does not reads as painted plaster. A ridged crack network presses darkness into the crevices (light cannot reach into them — the poor man's occlusion); the **mineral veins** are `pow`-sharpened ridge lines of a **3D world-space field with a patch mask** — they were the scene's one 2D-parameterized field (`atan2` of the bearing) and it paid twice: the branch cut stood as a full-height seam of unrelated pattern down the −X wall (mirrored again by the river), and on the ceiling the mapping degenerated to radial spokes converging on a singularity over the origin. A 3D field has no seam and no pole by construction, and its ceiling slice continues its wall slice across the junction for free; the mask keeps the veins *threading* — ore follows some fractures and leaves others bare — where the unmasked web wallpapered the cave with even neon, half of what read as a screensaver. Rock within a few units of the river is **wet** (darker, a waterline stain). Exponential distance fog sinks the far side into an abyssal blue-purple.
- **The river is a flat plane with a ripple in its normal** — hit exactly, with no search at all: three sum-of-sines components (26 / 8.8 / 3.1 wu, deep-water dispersion so the swell outruns the chop) evaluated three times a pixel for a gradient and nothing else, the amplitude dying between 70 and 170 units of horizontal distance so the far river is dead flat and cannot shimmer. It was a **marched** seven-component height field until #250 — sixteen bracketing steps and a secant inside the band the waves could reach — which bought real silhouettes and real flanks for the glints to catch, and cost sixteen spectrum evaluations before the surface had even been found. The four smallest components went with the march: at 1.15–5.2 wu they sit under a display pixel at any distance the river is actually seen from, so in a *normal* they were noise. The gradient is taken at **full strength**, because the first flat-plane era read as a *blue patterned floor* and it was its fake normal damped to 0.18 that did that, not its flatness. `WaveAmplitude` (0.7 wu) joins `WaveScale`/`WaveSpeed` in the Water config group, the latter two global multipliers around their shipped defaults.
- **The river's mirror is a ramp, not the cave (#250).** The reflected ray used to be handed back to the same wall-shading function, so the water genuinely reflected the veins, the fog, the steam and the crystal light standing over it — and that second `ShadeWall`, paid on every water pixel, was the dearest single thing in the pass. What stands in for it is `lerp(FogColor, RockColor, saturate(bounced.y·2))` — a grazing ray runs into the far wall, which the fog owns at that distance; a climbing one ends on ceiling rock, which nothing lights — plus `RockColor · CrystalLightAt(hit) · 12`, the clusters being the brightest things in the mirror and the one thing it cannot lose (12 against the wall's own 16 is that wall's N·L averaged away: there is no surface here to lean towards a cluster). That pooled light is read **once** and spent twice, in the mirror and in the depths. The steam lerp immediately after closes most of the rest of the gap, since the reflected path starts *on* the water in the layer's densest air, so at any distance the mirrored wall was already most of the way to `MistColor` — which is the same argument the old reduced program made for reflecting `MistColor` flat, and it shipped that way below `High` for two issues. Fresnel's floor is **high for water (0.25)**: a cave river is a black mirror before it is a window, and the more the depths show through, the more the surface reads as flooring. The downward fold of a reflected ray is a **soft max** — `abs()` folded the mirrored image back on itself along a crease that slid with the water. The **crystals glint on the ripple** (a Blinn lobe per cluster on the water normal, inverse-square, riding the pulse) — the cave's stand-in for the sea's sun sparkle — and the crest bioluminescence rides the ripple's own height. **What the glints lost with the march is the flank**, and it shows: on three sines they come out as smooth diagonal *streaks* rather than the broken sparkle a real surface scattered, because a plane offers every glint the same slope over a wide run. Photographed and accepted as part of the trade, not fixed — unlike the caustics below, there is no free stand-in for a surface that is not there.
- **The caustics are a cellular edge field** (`VoronoiEdge2`, `F2 − F1` — zero exactly on the borders between cells), because that is what pool caustics are: a bright **web** where wavelets focus, running around dark cells. Two attempts preceded it and both lessons stand: a wave-interference field *must be normalized before shaping* (`saturate(0.5 + 0.5·h)` over an amplitude of 1.95 clips into broad plateaus — the first "caustics" were a cyan checkerboard), and even normalized, a sine field's crests are blobs, not a net. Caustics also run at a *finer* scale than the ripple itself, being the focused image of the surface — and `CausticStrength` dropped from the first flat-plane era's 0.8 to **0.5**. The plane is back since #250 and the 0.5 stays — but **it did read as flooring again, and the dial was not the answer**. Screenshotted at the menu's own vantage, the shipped plane came out a coarse, regular lattice of cells where the marched version had a fine broken shimmer, and the reason is optical rather than a matter of strength: while the surface was marched, the web was sampled where the ray met a real crest or trough, so the waves squeezed and stretched it *for free*, and that distortion was most of what read as water. Turning the web down would only have made a dimmer lattice. **So the caustic lookup is warped by the ripple's own gradient** (`CAUSTIC_WARP` 6.0 world units per unit of gradient — half a cell at rest, a whole cell on a flank, against a cell 3.33 units across), which is the same optical argument the geometry used to make and **costs nothing measurable**: the gradient is already in registers from the normal, and the fixed-camera pin read 14.5 ms without the warp and 13.3 with it, inside the run-to-run spread. It is the cheapest half of what the march was buying, kept after the march was sold.
- **The crystals are the gated march again** (the dream's pattern — per-cluster bounding spheres, a short interval trace), each cluster three interpenetrating octahedra at different elongations and yaws. Two lessons: they must stand **on** the wall (at 0.86 R they floated thirty units off it and read as balloons; 0.965 R), and they must be shaded off the **SDF's own normal** (four tetrahedral taps) — the octahedra's flat faces give a constant normal per facet that jumps at the arris, so the facet structure appears in the shading for free, where the first build's smooth radial term made every cluster a rounded gem. The facet *contrast* is what says "crystal", not the outline. They also carry **SDF ambient occlusion** (four probes up the normal), so the clefts where the three octahedra interpenetrate sit visibly deeper than the open faces — a crystal glows from inside, and its junctions still swallow light.
- **The clusters light the cave**: an inverse-square sum of all eight pools their colour onto the rock and the water around them — eight multiply-adds in the wall shade, and the single thing that makes the crystals belong to the cave instead of being stickers on it.
- **The god rays and spores are closest-approach glows** (no geometry, no march): vertical-line distance for the shafts — height-enveloped (a `smoothstep`, C1 the whole way down: the old clamped ramp put a visible Mach shelf at each end), dust-shimmered, breathing — and the dream's spark gaussians slowed right down for the spores. Both are clamped to the **final** solid including the crystals, and the shafts are added **after** the crystal branch: accumulated before it, every beam crossing a cluster was punched out along the crystal's silhouette. A spore's climb wraps on a **continuous envelope** (born dark at the water, dimmed out under the ceiling) — the raw `fmod` teleported each mote from ceiling to water at full brightness, a hard pop every few seconds in a scene whose whole point is stillness.
- **The shell's two seams are hidden, in depth.** An analytic shell has machined edges no real cave has, and both showed: the wall-ceiling crease was a sharp lit line (the base normal snapped from radial to down-facing in a hard select) — now the normal **coves**, blending over the top twenty-odd units, because rock coves, it does not mitre. The waterline gets three layers of defence: **steam over the river** — an exponential height layer (`MistColor`/`MistDensity`/`MistHeight` in the Water group), integrated *analytically* along the view ray (height fog rather than a slab, because a slab has a top and its top is a new hard line), so a ray grazing toward the far shore runs its whole length through the densest air and the seam fades exactly where it was sharpest — plus a **shore band** (the last few units of water before the wall show pure mirror, so the depths and the crest glow are gone *before* the contact circle instead of stepping across it — the steam alone left the raw line under a steep look at a near shoreline). **That band's second argument died with the mirror in #250 and the record has to say so**: it used to hold that the reflection of the wall right above the contact converges to that wall by construction, so the two shading rules met at a shared value — true of a real wall shade, meaningless for a ramp. Photographed at `campos=228,-14,0` looking down at the shore, the raw dark line has become a **pale strip**, the band coming out `RockColor·(1 + 12·pool)` against a wall carrying `ShadeWall`'s dimmer, wet-darkened value. It is left standing deliberately: that vantage needs a camera out at radius ~228 of a 240-unit cave, and every camera the Game has sits within tens of units of the origin, where 230 units of fog (`exp(−0.0045·230)` = 0.36) and the steam own the contact completely. **The free camera in the Testbed and the map editor is what can reach it**, and if it is ever worth fixing, the lever is the ramp's top end under `shore`, not the band's width plus the rock's **wet stain** above the contact. An fBm wisp factor rides on the mist's optical depth (not on the blend, so the far shore still saturates while the mid-water steam drifts) and is keyed to the **ray direction, not the hit point** — `bestT` jumps across every crystal silhouette, and a hit-keyed wisp printed a ghostly crystal-shaped cutout into the fog. The water's mirror gets the same mist integral along the reflected path, and the spores are added after it: a mote climbs *out* of the layer, and stays visible doing it.
- **One deliberate deviation from the brief that shaped it**: no per-scene colour grade or contrast pass — BS3D renders linear radiance and tonemaps once (`docs/rendering.md`), so the grade lives in the authored palette and the scene's light rig (dim and cool, the river's teal in the ground bounce, a faint magenta fill answering from the walls). The one cinematic touch kept in-pass is a **subtle vignette on the backdrop alone**, which darkens the cave's corners without touching the island drawn over it.
- The river sits at `LevelY` −34, **below the drain's kill plane**, so a ball that falls through the funnel is culled before it would visibly reach water it can never splash. `SceneRenderer.OpenBelow` includes the cavern; it is not an `IsSolidTerrainScene`, so no pit shaft.
- **Measured, and every figure here is the pre-#250 program's _and_ pre-#155** (front end, 1600×900, dome 13, vsync off, the APU): **73.5 ms at `High`, 21.4 at `Medium` (46.8 FPS)** — `High` was a desktop setting (the bumped, point-lit rock, the cellular caustics and the marched river were bought at full price, deliberately), and the adaptive probe stepped this class of hardware to `Medium`, which still cleared its 45 FPS floor. The flat-plane river measured 59.5/17.5; the flat-mottle wall before it, 24.2/9.0. **Do not quote the 73.5 as current, and #250 quoted it as motivation before checking**: it predates the display-resolution backdrop, which is what actually fixed `High` here. The same machine class on the same code today measures 32.6 ms at a level, less than half of it.
- **What the shipped program costs, measured on the APU** (#250, 2026-08-20, the laptop — the same integrated Radeon the figures above were taken on, and the machine that has *not* been hard-resetting). Instrument: `fpscap=N` on the Testbed, which presents immediately but idles out the rest of each frame's period, so a scene cheaper than the cap never runs the card flat out while anything dearer still reads its true cost (`TestOptions.FpsCap`). Fixed camera over the river (`campos=0,-26,0 camtarget=0,-31,-200`), `arena=none`, 1600×900, dome 13:

  | | ssaa 1 | ssaa 2 | ssaa 4 |
  |---|---|---|---|
  | before the cut | 23.9 ms | 25.2 | 40.0 |
  | shipped | **13.3** | **16.0** | **31.9** |

  **The saving is the same ~9 ms at all three supersample factors, and that is the tell**: since #155 this backdrop shades a target the size of the *back buffer*, so all three runs shade the same 1600×900 in the pass and `ssaa` only moves the resolve over it. **An `ssaa` sweep is therefore the wrong instrument for this scene** — it varies everything except the thing being changed. Use `width=`/`height=` to scale the pass, and mind that a back buffer larger than the panel is silently clamped (a `width=2560` run on this 1920×1080 laptop reported `958x484` on its own `[fps]` line and was thrown away).
- **And in the product, at a level** (`BS3D.exe level=Chest quality=…`, 1600×900, `nocap`, means of 2–3 runs): `High` **32.6 → 26.5 ms** (30.8 → 37.7 FPS), ranges 31.0–34.2 against 24.4–28.8, no overlap. `Medium` **24.2 → 24.3 ms — unchanged, and correctly so**: `Medium` already ran the reduced technique, so the only thing this cut newly takes from it is the river's march, and at a play camera the river is not in frame at all. The whole gain lands where the owner asked for it — on the tier that used to draw the full program. A level's own spread is wide (a descending ceiling, a swinging cluster, physics spikes: 4.4 ms between two runs of the *same* build); a fixed-camera pin repeated 20 minutes later read 13.3 ms both times, so that spread is the level and **not** thermal throttling on the laptop.
- **This pass is occupancy-bound, not work-bound, and every obvious reduction is therefore worth nothing (#102) — _on the desktop GPU, and only there._** Attribution on the 6900 XT (front end, 1600×900, dome 13, `nocap`, `High` at 4.98 ms): dropping the reflection's second `ShadeWall` **5.01 ms**, cutting the spores from 28 to one **5.02**, halving the river search to 8 steps **4.97**, cutting the crystal march to 14 steps at a 0.75 stride **5.01**. Not one of them saves anything. The **pair** of the reflection and the spores measures **3.33**, and adding the river and crystal cuts on top of that gives 3.31 — nothing further. It is the same signature the forest floor has, and it inverts the order the issue's own fix sketch ranked these in: the river and the crystals were second and third there, and they are worthless.
- **That attribution does not transfer to the APU, and #250 is the proof (2026-08-20).** The four rows above are why nobody expected this cut to pay, so the correction matters more than the numbers. Same instrument, fixed camera, `arena=none`, 1600×900: the **spore cut on its own** — the camera turned away so there is no water in frame at all, leaving 28 → 8 spores as the only difference — went **20.7 → 17.9 ms** on the integrated Radeon, where the desktop measured that same cut at 5.02 against 4.98, i.e. nothing. A wide desktop part has occupancy to spare and an integrated one does not; the tiers exist for the machine where the reductions *do* pay. **So never carry a cavern attribution across machine classes** — measure it on the class you are trying to fix, and label which class every figure came from.
- **The reduced program left in #250 and CAME BACK in #298, for a different reason each time — read both halves.** First, why it left:  The owner lifted this scene's "deliberately expensive" constraint outright — it does not need maximum graphics, it needs to run cool on every machine — so the pair that second technique used to drop is cut from the shipped shader instead. What used to be `CavernScene(input, detail)` in two techniques is one program: the water reflects a ramp rather than a second `ShadeWall`, the spore count is 8 for everyone, the river's sixteen-step march is gone and its spectrum is three components read for a gradient alone. `SceneRenderer.SceneDetail` no longer touches this scene at all — the forest and the dream keep their two programs — so one technique serves every tier, which is what "cool on **every** machine" required: the APU's `High` drew the full program, and it is the only tier this cut moved (32.6 → 26.5 ms at a level; `Medium` was already on the reduced pair and measured unchanged). For the record of what those two techniques cost while they existed: **`High` 4.98 ms, reduced 3.56** with supersampling pinned so only the technique differed, and **1.36 → 0.99 ms** on the real `Medium` where 1× supersampling multiplies with it.
- **And why it came back (#298): this is the one scene the quality ladder could not help by any other means.** It is immune to supersampling *by construction* — it shades a backdrop the size of the back buffer and scales it up (#155), so `ssaa` moves it 8 % where it moves other scenes 46–58 % — MSAA moves it 0.30 ms, and rendering below native is refused outright by the owner's standing ruling that a tier drops effects and never resolution. A reduced program of its own was the only lever left, and on the weak machine this is the scene that sits **27 % over the panel at all three tiers**. The new pair is not #250's: it is the wall's **bump gradient** — a gradient of a 3-octave field is four evaluations, i.e. twelve octaves of 3D noise on every wall pixel, half of what the wall spends — and the **crack network**. A pair and not one of them, for the reason this scene taught the project in the first place: the pass is occupancy-bound and a lone cut buys nothing. Measured on the reference desktop, 3840×1600, `ssaa` 1, a level's worth of balls: **4.08 → 3.07 ms, a quarter of the frame**, and 3.4× what MSAA gives the same scene. What it costs: the wall goes from pitted stone to a smooth shell and loses its crevice darkening — the strata, the veins and their patch mask, the waterline stain, the crystals as real point lights, the mist, the god rays and the river are all untouched, so it still reads as a cave rather than as plaster.
- **And the reduced program is not what fixed `High` (#155), because it never could have been.** The bar `High` is defined against is 75 FPS on the reference 6900 XT, and at fullscreen 3840×1600 the scene measured **20.8 ms — 48.0 FPS**, against 6.4 for the meadow. A 0.715× technique lands that at 14.9 ms, i.e. 67 FPS: the cut this issue asked for cannot reach the bar it was asked to reach. What can is the **one dial an occupancy-bound pass has**. Fixed camera, 1600×900: 1.5 ms at ssaa 1, 5.2 at 2, **21.0 at 4** — 4.04× for 4× the pixels, with a fitted fixed cost of essentially zero. So the backdrop is now shaded into a target the size of the **back buffer** and scaled up into the supersampled scene target (`SceneRenderer.DrawBackdropAtDisplayResolution`), which is a quarter of the pixels at `ssaa 2`. What that gives up is the backdrop's *supersampling* and **not its resolution** — the cave still comes out at every pixel the display has, and the balls, the arena and the gun keep every sample they had, being still drawn into the full-size target. Measured at fullscreen `High`: **20.8 → 6.3 ms, 48.0 → 158.5 FPS**, which puts the scene *under* the meadow it was three times dearer than. The dream takes the same path and the same measurement (**18.6 → 5.8 ms, 53.7 → 172.5 FPS**), which is #167's answer as well.
- **It is these two and not all four sky-replacing scenes.** Space sizes its stars in *output* pixels off `SupersampleFactor`, so shading it into a smaller target would change how big and how bright they come out — and at 7.1 ms against a 6.4 ms frame there is nothing to buy. The Moon draws its sky depth-read behind real terrain, so by the time it shades it is not a full-screen pass at all. **The look was checked rather than argued**: the same camera through the direct path at ssaa 1 and through the scaled path at ssaa 2 gives the same cave and the same dream — same crystals, veins, god ray and solids in the same places — with the backdrop a shade softer and the arena and gun visibly *sharper*, which is the trade stated exactly.

## The Moon (Game)

The twelfth `SceneKind` (#125): the island stands on a cratered grey regolith plain, ringed at a few hundred units by the highland belt that is its skyline, under a black, starlit sky with the Earth hanging in it at its **real** angular size — the Apollo-photo look. Stark raking sun, hard shadows, no atmosphere, and **nothing moving anywhere**: no wind, no weather, no birds, no waves, and `Moon.fx` takes no time uniform at all — the stillest scene in the game, which is itself part of the mood. `Moon.fx` + `MoonSceneConfig` (named nested groups — Terrain, Earth, Stars, Lighting — the PropertyGrid rule), `scene=moon` on either command line, on the Game's scene menu and in its random pick; like the forest and the other sky-replacing scenes it sits past the end of the NumPad2/V cycle.

**It is the first scene in BOTH families at once**, which is the fork issue #125 flagged up front and the classification docs on `SceneRenderer` now record. `IsSolidTerrainScene` — real displaced ground, the island's footprint `clip()`ed out, the dark pit shaft behind the drain's glass (without it the ~55 %-opaque glass shows the **starfield** through a hole in the ground, the terrain scenes' bug restated against the night sky), and therefore no dive for the drop cinematic (`OpenBelow` stays the complement it was defined as). `ReplacesSky` — no dome drawn or read, no cloud deck, cloud shadow suppressed, black clear, and its own light rig through `TryGetLightRig`. The two memberships never answer the same question in any caller, which is what makes holding both sound; the option the issue called A (a terrain scene with a private "no-dome" side channel) would have re-created the per-executable special case #75 existed to end.

- **Two techniques over one effect, and the order is measured, not stylistic.** `DrawMoon` draws the **terrain first** (the desert's machinery: a camera-centred grid snapped to its cell, displaced in the vertex shader, base normal per pixel from the height field's own gradient), then the **sky quad depth-READ against what the terrain wrote** (`DepthStencilState.DepthRead`, the quad at `z = w`), so the star shader only runs where sky is visible. The first build drew the sky first under `DepthStencilState.None` like the other sky-replacing scenes and the terrain over it, and in the Game's frame that interleave measured **244 ms at `High` on the reference APU against 17 ms** for this order — an 8× blow-up neither pass shows alone (sky alone 8 ms, terrain alone 18) and which the Testbed's frame never reproduced. The mechanism was not chased past the fix: depth-read-after-terrain is the right order regardless, since it also stops paying for stars the ground was always going to cover.
- **The crater field is a single-cell lattice, and the single cell is the whole budget.** `CraterLayer` holds each crater — skirt included — inside its own jittered cell (`margin = radius · 1.6`, the star lattice's and the wildflowers' trick), so only the pixel's own cell is ever read: three 2D hashes per layer. The first build walked the 3×3 neighbourhood anyway — nine times the hashes answering "no" for craters that by construction can never reach the point — and with the field evaluated four times per terrain pixel (three normal taps and the small-crater detail octave) the scene ran at **2 FPS** on the reference APU against the ~50 the budget affords. Four octaves, each on its own seed, so small craters overprint large ones freely across scales; per crater the existence, position, radius, depth and **rim sharpness** are independent rolls — a sum of identical bowls is the plane-wave-sine failure in bowl form, and an eroded-soft-versus-young-sharp rim spread is what keeps the plain from reading as a texture. The profile balances the rim's lip against the bowl's cup to near mean-zero (a real rim is the excavated bowl put back), for the desert's reason: the field rides the clearing ramp, and a field with a mean makes the ramp a visible bowl around the island. The fourth, finest octave lives in the pixel shader only (normal detail, band-limited), with an isotropic `Fbm2BandLimited` regolith relief and a **cascaded** grain hash under the desert's fade-before-cell-size rule — since #208 a cascade rather than one lattice, because the single arm's-length grain left everything past a few metres of the camera as one flat grey (the owner's "as if it had no texture at all"), and between the cascade and the broad mottling a resolvable octave now exists at every footprint the plain is drawn at. All of it scales with `GrainStrength`, so a config pinning it still silences the lot.
- **⚠ That cascade was three octaves of per-cell hash at ~2 cm, ~1.3 m and ~5.5 m, and the two large ones were the checkerboard the owner reported (#345).** The general finding is worth more than the scene: **a flat per-cell hash is a visible SQUARE whenever its cell covers more than a few pixels**, and a band limit cannot save it — a band limit fades an octave out as its cells get *small*, which is the aliasing end, and says nothing about the near end, where a 1.3 m cell is a hundred pixels across and a 5.5 m cell is most of the near ground. That is not grain, it is tiling. Nothing about it is #240's crater grid, which stays fixed. **The first diagnosis was wrong and the method is the point**: silencing the 5.5 m octave alone left the checkerboard standing, silencing the whole term cleared it, and silencing the 1.3 m one alone left the larger squares — *both* large octaves were doing it. Each was killed in turn and photographed. The fix is one **interpolated** octave at ~2.8 m in place of both (`GradientNoise2`, gained by `GRAIN_SMOOTH_GAIN` because a Perlin field crowds towards zero where a hash fills ±1 uniformly, and swapping without it would have halved the mottling as well as smoothing it). The 2 cm octave keeps its flat hash: at that size the cell is sub-pixel wherever it is drawn at all, so the hard edge never resolves — it is grit, and grit is what a hash is good for. **Two, not two smoothed, and that was decided with numbers**: smoothing both measured **49.9 → 46.5/47.4 FPS** on the reference desktop (pinned camera, ssaa 2, 1600×900, `nocap`, medians of 31 readings) — 1.1–1.5 ms, about 6 % of the frame, since `GradientNoise2` is four hashes against a hash's one — while the single merged octave measured **49.0**, i.e. **0.37 ms**, and the two crops are hard to tell apart. On a shader whose own header records a 2 FPS build, 6 % for a difference nobody can see was not a trade worth making.
- **⚠ THAT MARGIN IS THE CRATER'S OWN RADIUS, SO A CRATER THAT FILLS ITS CELL IS PINNED TO THE LATTICE — and it was, in all four octaves at once (#240).** `margin = radius · 1.6` is subtracted from both ends, so the box left for the centre is `1 − 3.2 · radius`: at the shipped radius range of 0.16–0.30 of a cell that ran from 49 % of the cell down to **4 %**. The jitter was not merely weak, it was weakest **exactly where it mattered most** — the largest crater of every octave, the one the eye picks out first, sat on its cell centre to within a fortieth of a cell. Photographed from above at `campos=0,60,0 camtarget=0,-13.5,150`, the plain was a stamped carpet of rings in rows and columns, unmistakable at a glance and worst in the near field where a hundred cells of the finest octave fall inside one look. **A previous session's diagnosis in the shared journal — "the ejecta is not multiplied by the extinction ramp" — was wrong and had been carried forward unread**: `ejecta = rim * rollB.y` and `rim` already carries `smoothstep(1.6, 1.1, d)`.
  - **The radius pair is now chosen for the JITTER, and the octave periods carry the size** — 0.12–0.21 of a cell against 0.16–0.30, with the periods rescaled by the same 1.43 (90/34/13/5 → **129/49/18.6/7.2** world units), so the worst case leaves 33 % of the cell instead of 4 % and **no crater changed size on the ground**: 27.1, 10.3 and 3.9 world units at the top of the three height octaves, against 27.0, 10.2 and 3.9 before.
  - **The radius roll is squared**, which weights the field towards small craters — what a real crater count does, N climbing steeply as the diameter falls — and it pays twice here, because a crater's margin *is* its radius: mostly-small craters are mostly-free centres. Median radius 0.14 of a cell against a uniform law's 0.165, median jitter box 55 % against 47 %.
  - **Each octave is turned to its own bearing before `floor()`** (13°, 41°, 74°, 56°, sharing no small ratio). A separate seed stops two lattices sharing a *crater*; it does nothing about their **rows**, and four unturned `floor()` domains all had theirs along world X and Z, so every octave redrew the others' grid at its own scale. That is why the fault photographed as one carpet rather than four faint ones. Two multiplies and an add per octave, off compile-time constants.
  - **`chance` is per octave** (0.86 / 0.82 / 0.70 / 0.64 against one flat 0.62) and it is the count knob: the bigger cells cost 49 % of the craters per unit area, and these buy most of that back. Tuned by eye against the same capture, in two passes — the first pass at 0.72/0.68/0.55/0.50 broke the grid but left the mid-field bare, which is the honest reason the second exists.
  - **Cost, measured at that pinned vantage with `nocap`: 52 FPS before, 51 after, one reading each** — the turns are free and the hash count per pixel rose about 15 % because the chances did, and no more precision than "inside the sample spread" is claimed from two samples. Verified from the play camera as well (`F10`, `Maps/Full.json`), which sees only the highland belt's band and now shows it scattered.
- **The horizon is curvature, not haze — occlusion, not fog.** There is no air, so the atmospheric siblings' horizon-haze fade would be physically wrong; instead the height field drops `Curvature · distance²` (8e-5 ≈ a 6.25 km moon), so nearer ground occludes everything past the bulge and there is no fade anywhere. Two figures are chained to that one: `MOON_EXTENT` (±600) only has to cover the horizon distance, and the whole visible grid stays inside the **Game camera's 500-unit far plane** — halve the curvature and the far plane cuts the terrain first, which puts a dead-level, camera-locked clip line where the horizon should be.
- **The highland belt exists because the play camera grazes the island's own deck, and without it the scene had no ground in the frame at all.** As first shipped the curvature was the whole horizon, and from the gameplay vantage that horizon was *invisible*: `GameCameraFit.LENS_FLOOR_Y` puts the lens at −7.9, a bare **0.6 units** over `ArenaIsland.TOP_Y` = −8.5, so the deck occludes everything below about **half a degree** of depression — and a curved plain's skyline sits `2·√(eyeHeight · Curvature)` **below** the eye, **2.4°** at the shipped figures. The Moon played as a black starlit sky over bare stone. Neither obvious dial reaches it: raising `LevelY` leaves the skyline under the deck even with the plain flush against it (0.8° at zero eye height, and the island's drum would be buried to its neck), and slackening `Curvature` runs into the 500-unit far plane long before the skyline clears. What clears the deck is **relief standing above the lens** — which is, checked against the siblings, exactly why *their* ground is visible: the desert's 14-unit dunes crest at y = +0.5, eight units over the same lens. So the mare is a basin with a rim (`HighlandHeight` 40, `HighlandInnerRadius` 190 → `HighlandCrestRadius` 310): the skyline is now the crest, ~4.5° over the lens, and past it the curvature takes back over — quadratic drop against a saturated rise, so elevation falls monotonically outward and the far-plane cut stays hidden behind the crest. `HighlandSaddleFloor` (0.5) is the reason the belt never dips to the plain: a saddle that did would be a notch the eye looks straight through, onto the ground the crest exists to occlude, and at a shallow enough angle that is the clip plane.
- **The belt's shape is `MareBase`'s noise, reused rather than its own — and that reuse is the whole reason it is affordable.** `MoonHeight` is evaluated **four times per pixel** (the vertex tap plus the normal's three), so a private two-octave massif field cost eight extra gradient noises a pixel and measured **2.0 ms of an 18.9 ms frame** at ssaa 2 on the reference APU — 12 % of the frame for a silhouette the plain's own undulation carries for nothing. Driving the belt off the already-computed `MareBase` value put the frame at **17.7 ms against 16.2 ms with no belt at all**: ~1.5 ms for the feature, of which about half is simply the extra terrain now covering pixels that used to be black sky. It buys a correlation — where a massif stands high the mare undulation runs high too — which is invisible and can stay that way: `MareBase` moves the plain by about two units against the belt's forty, and the belt only exists 190 units out. It also reads *better* than the private field did, its 91/32-unit octaves giving a craggier crater-rim skyline where the hand-picked 160/65 gave a smooth swell.
- **The sky is the shared starfield plus an analytic Earth, and the Earth is deliberately small.** The star lattice moved out of `Space.fx` **verbatim** into `Stars.fxh` (uniforms included, so one C# push routine serves both effects) rather than being copied — the two no-atmosphere skies want an identical field, and a second copy would be the duplicated-classification mistake in shader form. No Milky Way, no nebulae, no galaxies, no volume: an Apollo sky is stars on black and the Earth, tuned sparser than space's through the same `SpaceStarsConfig` shape. The Earth reuses the space planet's machinery (ray-sphere at the distance that gives the configured angular radius, `smoothstep` terminator — narrower than the gas giant's, Earth's atmosphere being thin — `NightAmbient` floor) with continents instead of bands: a 3-octave `Fbm3` thresholded at the real ~30 % land fraction, arid belts off a second coarser field, fractal-broken polar ice, and swirled weather whose mask band is deliberately high and narrow — the first build's wide band handed back a white planet with blue flecks, and the marble must stay blue with white on it. **Its angular radius ships at the true 0.95° (1.9° across, ~30 px at the default window), which is an expectation-setting decision, not a bug**: far smaller than the mental image cropped Apollo photos leave, and saying so up front is why the dial is in the config. Being that small its lit body — clouds included — stays **under** `GLARE_THRESHOLD`, unlike the space planet's limb: a 30-pixel disc is no long coherent arc, and a small bright thing the glare's sparse grid samples stochastically flickers. Only the thin atmosphere rim carries a touch of bloom, through a saturated blue whose luminance stays modest, and the rim is lit **per point of the ring** off the sun's side of the disc — one global phase factor drew a complete blue circle around a gibbous Earth, night limb included, which reads as a ring around the planet rather than as its air. Continent/cloud octave counts are chosen against the disc's ~30 on-screen pixels: a fourth octave contributed speckle, not coastline.
- **The light rig is the one rig whose ground half outshines its sky half** (`MoonLightingConfig`): the sky is black and the sunlit regolith below is the only diffuse source there is — Apollo photographs fill their shadows from the ground, not the sky. The terrain's own sun (`MoonTerrainConfig.SunColor`) is the config's and never the frame's dome-derived `SunColor`, the `TryGetLightRig` argument painted onto the ground: there is no dome, and a dome-derived sun on a domeless ground would be a lie. The terrain's fill is a tiny non-zero `AmbientColor` (at zero the shadowed wall of every crater reads as a hole cut in the ground — the space scene's island lesson at terrain scale) plus a directional **earthshine** term off the Earth's own direction; the same figures feed `TryGetMoonEarthshine`, the scene point light (the planetshine pattern — a real light rather than more ambient, so the metallic drain beads get a highlight out of it), so the ground and the island cannot disagree about how bright the Earth is. The ambience bed is the quietest in the set, deliberately — near-static sub-bass under the faintest slow hiss, no wind (wind is the one thing this scene cannot have); it also unseated `ProceduralAmbience.BakeScene`'s cavern-as-`default:` arm, which is exactly how a thirteenth scene would have silently played cave drips.
- **Measured** (reference APU, 1600×900, `nocap`): Game front end **~17.7 ms at `High`** (ssaa 2) — desert-class, cheaper than the city — of which the highland belt is ~1.5 ms. No reduced program: the scene sits comfortably inside the terrain-scene band, and the figures above are why (the single-cell crater field and the depth-read sky are each worth an order of magnitude against the first build, and the belt's borrowed noise a further 1.2 ms).

## The outback

The thirteenth `SceneKind` (#112): weathered red-orange monoliths standing on a wide, near-flat spinifex plain, the island in a clearing among them — Uluru and Kata Tjuta rather than a Sahara. `Outback.fx` + `OutbackSceneConfig` (named nested groups — Terrain, Surface, Air, Birds — the PropertyGrid rule), `scene=outback` on any command line, on the Game's scene menu and in its random pick; like the forest and the sky-replacing scenes it sits past the end of the NumPad2/V cycle, and the editor reaches it by loading a level whose scene names it.

**It is the desert's arid sibling and deliberately shares nothing of its look.** The Sahara is golden dunes whose only silhouette is their own crests, with a braided ripple surface; this is red ground under **rock** — a skyline of bornhardts with the gullies water has cut down their flanks, and a cellular spinifex ground rather than a wave one. It is a `IsSolidTerrainScene` (flat clearing at the island's foot, the island's footprint `clip()`ed out, the dark pit shaft behind the drain's glass, and therefore no dive for the drop cinematic), it takes the dome like every terrain scene, and it shares the savanna's and desert's [flock of birds](#the-savanna-testbed) — the one flock's per-bird state is sized from all three configs' counts.

- **The monoliths are geometry, on a jittered single-cell lattice** — the Moon's crater trick, the space starfield's and the meadow's wildflowers': a formation is held inside its own cell by its own reach, so only the pixel's own cell is ever read (three to five hashes) rather than the nine a neighbourhood walk costs. That matters more here than anywhere, because the field is evaluated **four times per pixel** (the vertex tap plus the normal's three). Each formation is two lobes at its own bearing, elongation and crest flatness (a bornhardt comes in shoulders — Kata Tjuta is thirty-six domes leaning on each other, and a field of identical single domes is the plane-wave-sine failure in dome form), with a talus apron at the foot. A second, rotated lattice carries boulder-scale outcrops, so the two grids share no lines.
  - **The clearing gates the rock per FORMATION, not per pixel** — off the formation's own centre. Applied per pixel a radial ramp slices a monolith with a gradient and draws it half sunk; taken from the centre a formation is either standing whole or is not there. The plain's own swell is not ramped at all, so the trap the desert's `DuneSum` carries a trailing constant to avoid — a field with a mean times a radial ramp is a bowl with the island at the bottom — never arises: the one field with a big mean is the rock, and it is never partially raised.
  - **The margin bites, and it is honest about it.** A formation's margin inside its cell is its *full* reach — radius × apron × gully depth × elongation — so the largest, most elongated one is left a jitter box of `1 − 2 × 0.44`, i.e. **0.116 of a cell** (0.16 × 1.3 × 1.18 × 1.8 = 0.4419, against the 0.45 the clamp allows). It still reads as random because the visible irregularity is in **world** units: 0.116 of the 270-unit cell is **31 units of wander** for a rock some 80 units across. The empty cells (`RockChance`) are what break the lattice for the rest. (This read "a quarter of a cell … ninety units" against the old 370-unit cell until #171; the fraction was the loose half of that sentence, and it is the one the arithmetic above pins.)
- **The lattice was one skyline too sparse for the camera the game is played from, and the camera is not what #171 thought it was.** The issue was filed on the arithmetic that `GAME_FOV`'s half-angle (21.43°) against a view axis pitched ~25° puts the frustum's **lower edge above the horizon**, cropping the plain away. Measured instead of estimated, on `Pyramid_Small`: the axis is pitched **+15.52°**, not 25 — the 25 in `GameCameraFit`'s remarks is the angle to the hanging *cluster*, while the axis aims at the field's centre (`CameraTargetY` 1.89) — so the lower edge sits at **−5.91°**, *below* horizontal, and the horizon is in frame at 85 % of the frame's height. (A 20×20×20 field reads +17.41° and −4.02°, the same answer.) Nothing about the framing was cropping anything.
  - **The control settles what it is instead.** Through that identical frustum, sampled at eight bearings from the play camera's own geometry: the **desert** fills its band with dunes at every bearing and the **savanna** with acacias at many distances at every bearing, while the outback held **one** formation at seven bearings of eight and bare horizon either side of it. So it is this scene's own density, and a camera change would have moved all fifteen settings to fix one.
  - **Why it was that sparse.** The play frustum is 70° wide horizontally and the haze has taken the ground by about 350 units (`HorizonHazeDistance`'s quartic bites in the last fifth of 480), so a player's wedge held some two lattice cells of 370 — times `RockChance` 0.62, about one formation. **`RockSpacing` is 270 and `RockChance` 0.70 since #171**, which roughly doubles what stands in that wedge; every bearing of the eight now carries a formation and most carry a second at depth.
  - **The cell shrank rather than the formations growing, and that is forced.** A formation's radius is a fraction of its *own* cell (0.10–0.16), and its margin already stands at 0.4419 against the 0.45 clamp — raising the fraction to hold the world size would push every formation over its cell edge and break the single-cell read the whole design is built on. So the monoliths came in with the cell: 27–43 units of radius against an unchanged `RockHeight`, still twice as wide as they are tall, which reads as a steeper bornhardt and not as a spire. The wide free camera the scene was authored at is unharmed — it gains a second rank of formations at depth and keeps its red plain.
  - **What it does not do, and the report's other half.** At the gun's own *rest* bearing the before and after are nearly indistinguishable, because that wedge already held the near monolith; the gain is across bearings, which is what a player traversing actually walks through. And **the red plain is still a sliver**, which is geometry rather than density: the lens sits 0.6 above the island's deck (see "The city and the glass arena") and the plain is only about half a unit below it, so the ground is seen at grazing incidence — the rows between the island's rim and the horizon span 50 to 500 units, and the far end of that is what the haze owns. Nothing short of moving the camera changes that, and the camera is aimed at a hanging cluster.
  - **Measured cost**: two whole builds run alternately on the reference desktop, windowed 1920×1080 at ssaa 4 through the *wide* free camera — far more rock on screen than the play camera holds, so the harshest reading available — **+0.183 ms of 12.0**, slower in three pairs of three. `RockLayer` early-outs on a cell with no formation, so what this buys is paid for in pixels that now take the full rock path.
  - **Note the outback is not in any shipped level** (a level's own `scene` wins over the player's pick — `GameplayScreen.Session`), so today it is reached in the Game through the scene menu and the front end, and in the Testbed and the editor. That is worth knowing before weighing what a play-camera framing costs.
- **The gullies are geometry too, and that is not where they started.** They were a per-pixel field combed along the terrain's own downhill direction (`Fbm2Combed`) driving the normal through `PerturbNormalFromHeight` — and the rock drew in **horizontal terraces**, one band per row of grid triangles. Both of that path's inputs are screen derivatives, and a screen derivative is constant across a triangle and jumps at every facet edge; on the gentle terrain of every other scene here adjacent facets very nearly agree and nothing shows, on a flank falling forty-four units over eight cells they do not. So the ribs moved into the height field, where the terrain normal's own finite differences pick them up and no screen derivative is involved — and they buy the **silhouette** as well, which a normal never could. They are radial by construction (a gully runs downhill, and downhill on a dome is radial), sampled on the **unit circle** of the formation's own frame: a closed curve in the noise's domain, so it comes back round to itself with no seam and no pole, which is what a rib count taken off an `atan2` bearing cannot do (the failure the cavern's mineral veins shipped with once). The gully value is handed back **out of the height field** so the desert-varnish streaks cannot land anywhere but in the gullies they belong to.
  - **The terraces were not the ribs' fault, and finding that out is what the two-stage debugging cost.** They were the **heat shimmer**: its noise was keyed to `worldPosition.y`, which on a near-vertical flank is a set of horizontal stripes one world unit apart, strongest exactly where the haze fade is half done. The distortion belongs to the air the ray passes *through*, not to the surface behind it, so its domain is the **view ray's own direction** now — features of a fixed angular size, sliding upward against the clock, sticking to nothing. The rock's fine relief went back to `PerturbNormalFromHeight` afterwards and no terrace returned.
- **The near monolith was an airbrushed orange dome, and every field was present when it was.** From the play camera the closest formation fills a third of the frame, and the colour selector — `broad + gullies + surface field` — was summed at weights whose worst case ran well past 1, so the whole face pinned to `RockColorBright`: three procedural fields in the shader and not one of them reaching the screen. The weights are cut to land inside 0…1 for all but the extremes. The surface field itself folds **world height** into its domain, because a field of the XZ plane alone is constant down a vertical face and would draw the flank as one smeared vertical streak.
- **The spinifex is cellular, and a raw Voronoi is a honeycomb.** Hummocks of spiky grass space themselves out the way plants competing for the same water do, which is a cellular field by nature — `Voronoi2`'s low values are closed round cells with bare red sand between them, where an fBm mottle only gives patches with no edge. But its sites are one per cell jittered by 0.4, so the first build tiled the plain on a near-perfect hexagonal packing with the bare ground reading as a connected web: the plane-wave-sine failure in cellular form. Two things break it and both are needed — the domain is **warped** by a field running at about a third of the hummock spacing, which slides neighbouring sites off their lattice rows, and the same field jitters the **threshold**, so hummocks vary in size instead of tiling at one radius. One noise evaluation does both. The **rim** is what is band-limited, not the hummock: a cellular field's finest feature is the width of the band its threshold cuts (0.3 of a cell), so a fade keyed to the cell leaves the rim aliasing for the whole distance between, and a fade keyed to the rim takes the tussocks off the plain within a hundred units. Widening the threshold band by the pixel keeps a distant hummock as a soft blob of the right average brightness; the field itself only fades once the *cells* approach pixel size, and finishes before they reach it (the desert's grain rule).
- **The outcrops carry no gullies at all, and their radius is held near their own height.** At a fifth of their 110-unit cell they came out twenty-five units across and five tall, and a disc at that aspect with gullies cut round it is not a rock but a **starfish** — five radiating spines lying flat on the sand, which is exactly what it drew. A boulder is a rounded thing (water never ran far enough down one to cut it), and at that size the ribs' world width falls under the grid cell anyway. Their talus apron is taken off the **unribbed** radius for the same reason: the apron is wide and only a tenth of the height, so on a small formation it is most of what can be seen, and scree fills a gully in rather than following it out.
- **The distance fade is in TWO stages, the forest's arrangement and for the forest's reason: one colour cannot do both jobs.** The mid-distance has to keep the scene's own red, or a dome with a teal horizon (13, the benchmark one) paints the far plain and the shadowed flank of every monolith green — aerial perspective behaving correctly and still the wrong picture. The last stretch has to arrive at the dome's exact `HorizonColor`, or the terrain's own edge shows as a seam against a sky it does not match — which is what a single fade to a dust colour drew, a red plain butting straight onto a green sky. So the dust builds up quadratically and the dome takes over on a quartic that only bites in the final fifth. Note the far edge going saturated at the dome's horizon colour is **shared with the desert** and not this scene's doing: dome 13's bottom band is a teal the visible dome above the skyline never shows, and both scenes reach it.
  - **The dust takes the sky's brightness in full and only half its hue**, which is `ForestScatterRenderer.ApplySkyTint` run the other way round. Lit by the sky's colour outright — `HazeTint * skyLight`, the obvious spelling — the product keeps whichever channel the dome happens to be strongest in, so a teal-horizoned dome handed back a green dust however hard `DustStrength` was pushed: a multiply cannot be out-weighted by a blend whose other end carries the same hue.
- **The grid is 400 over 1000** (a 2.5-unit cell) rather than the desert's 360 over 1000, because this terrain carries a real silhouette and not only a shaded surface: the shipped profile's flank falls its whole height over some eight cells, which the mesh can hold. Pulled tighter it would fall between vertices and the per-pixel normal would shade a cliff the silhouette does not have — the desert's crest-sharpening rule at monolith scale. Over 255 a side, so `CreateGridMesh`'s 32-bit index buffer is load-bearing here (the mountain's lesson).
- **The desert varnish is the one glossy thing in the scene.** The near-black mineral skin water leaves where it has run repeatedly down one line takes the low side of the gully field, sharpened into streaks and gated on how steep the rock is — a monolith's crest is bald rock, its flanks are ribbed and stained, and that difference is most of what says "weathered". Its Blinn lobe is gated on the varnish *and* the rock mask: a glossy plain reads as wet ground, which is the one thing the outback is not.
- **Measured** (desktop GPU — Ryzen 9 5900X / Radeon RX 6900 XT — front end, windowed 1600×900, dome 13, `quality=high` i.e. ssaa 2, `nocap`, 18 s runs with the first four readings dropped): **outback 443.2 FPS / 2.26 ms**, against **desert 511.9 / 1.95** and **mountain 531.6 / 1.88** on the same machine in the same pass. So it is the dearest of the three terrain scenes here, at **+0.30 ms and +15 %** over the sibling it is closest to in construction — three noise octaves on the rock's surface field and the rock lattice read four times per pixel, against the desert's four sines. No reduced program: it is well inside the terrain band and nowhere near the occupancy-bound class the forest floor, the dream and the cavern are in. **No figure is claimed for the reference APU**, because these three do not keep their order across machines — on the APU the mountain is the *dearest* terrain scene (19.1 ms against the desert's 16.9), the reverse of what this machine reads — so extrapolating from here would be inventing a number.
- **It forces no dome**, like the mountains, the meadow and the desert — it keeps whatever is up, and the two-stage haze is what lets it keep its own colour under any of the twenty. Its ambience bed is a thin dry wind under the shrill of cicadas, which is what heat sounds like; giving it one also turned `ProceduralAmbience.BakeScene`'s `default:` arm into a deliberately near-silent neutral, so the *next* scene is audibly missing a bed rather than plausibly playing somewhere else's (that arm has now silently handed the wrong atmosphere to two scenes running — cave drips to the Moon, then vacuum to the outback).

## The tropical beach

The fourteenth `SceneKind` (#244, the owner's own playtest request): the island stands in the middle of a tropical island's lagoon. A ring of white-gold sand at the island's foot carries the palms and the mossy rocks; the sand slopes through a wiggling waterline into calm turquoise water; and all around, some three hundred units out, the island's own green shore ridge rises out of the lagoon and closes the horizon, with one channel through it where the open sea reaches the skyline. `Tropical.fx` + `TropicalSceneConfig` (named nested groups — Terrain, Water, Palms, Rocks, Birds — the PropertyGrid rule), `scene=tropical` on any command line, on the Game's scene menu and in its random pick; like the forest and the scenes past the cycle's end it sits beyond `CycleLength`, and the editor reaches it by loading a level naming it.

**It is the first scene with terrain AND water in the one frame, and it takes the Moon's lesson: state the order, don't inherit it.** `DrawEnvironment`'s tropical case draws the land first — an ordinary depth-writing opaque draw — then the lagoon depth-READ over the bed the terrain just wrote, then the palms, the rocks and the flock. Two consequences worth knowing:

- **The lagoon is `Sea.fx` itself, unchanged.** `DrawTropicalWater` pushes the tropical config's water values into the very same effect and grid the open sea draws with — calmer swell, turquoise body colours — so the two scenes cannot drift apart in how water is *drawn*, only in what it is. The clip radius is the innermost the wiggling waterline ever reaches, less the shoulder of `Sea.fx`'s `CALM_BAND`, so the swell dies approaching the shore and the surf laps onto the sand rather than breaking against a circle; `FunnelPoolRadius` is 0 (the drain's standing pool is the sea scene's own arrangement). **Both water draws now push their whole uniform set every frame, config-static values included**: the two scenes share one effect instance and a NumPad2/V switch applies no config, so pushing only the per-frame half would leave whichever scene was switched to drawing the other's water — the shared flock's sizing trap, in shader-parameter form.
- **The actual waterline is the depth test's, not the clip's.** Between the clip radius and the coast the water plane extends under sand that is still above it, and the opaque depth-writing sand rejects it from every vantage — so the waterline the eye sees is exactly where the beach profile crosses the water level, wiggle included, with no shader doing anything about it. The clip only keeps the calm band anchored and stops the two surfaces grazing at the line itself.

**The land is one radial profile keyed on the distance to the WATERLINE, not the island** — flat dry sand at the island's foot, a hermite down through the water level exactly at the coast radius, the lagoon bed under opaque water, then the far shore's own rise. Two hermites meet at the coast so the profile crosses the water level *by construction* — the waterline and the coast are the same line and cannot drift apart, which is what lets the wet-sand band, the far shore's sand fringe and the lagoon's clip all read one figure. The waterline is a mean radius wobbled by sine octaves of **bearing** (integer multipliers, continuous round the circle), so the coast breaks into bays and headlands; the far shore wobbles on its own octaves so the two coasts share no lines, and its ridge carries **one channel** carved by a cosine bump of the bearing — a closed ring reads as a crater lake, and the channel is what says this lagoon belongs to an island in an ocean.

- **The height field is sines and hermite ramps ONLY, no gradient noise** — because `SceneRenderer.TropicalTerrainHeight` mirrors it on the CPU to plant the palms and the rocks on the ground the shader draws, the contract `SavannaTerrainHeight` has held since the acacias. Everything noisy (sand patches, the shell grain, the canopy mottle, the wind bands) lives in the pixel shader, where no plant ever asks for it. The far ridge's hills are domain-warped sines — the forest's trick, and warp-by-a-sine is still a sine, so the mirror covers them too.
- **The surface answers the two coasts differently.** The near beach is sand all the way down to its waterline, with a **wet band** darkening the last unit above the water (keyed on height above water, so it follows the wiggle exactly) and a fine wind-combed ripple. The far shore is jungle: the same ridge ramp that raises it gates a **green mask**, over a sand **fringe** just above the far waterline (every coast keeps a band of bare sand — it is what a shore is), with two band-limited mottle scales on the green (the forest floor's lesson: one scale reads as camouflage), travelling wind bands through it (the savanna's grass trick, mild at this distance) and a canopy relief tilting the normal. One relief field, lerped by the green mask, through one `PerturbNormalFromHeight` — the outback's single-perturbation rule.
- **The haze is the outback's two-stage arrangement** — mid-distance keeps the scene's own colour under any dome, the last stretch arrives at the dome's exact `HorizonColor` — with a weaker, near-neutral tint: clear marine air, not dust.

**The palms are real 3D geometry on the acacia's path (#202), and they sway.** `PalmMesh` is two meshes over one instance — a slim ring-scarred trunk bowed on a rolled curve (a chained tapered tube, not a lathe: a lathe is strictly about the Y axis) with the skirt of dead fronds hanging under the crown (a live crown over a bare trunk reads as a lollipop; the skirt is half of what says "palm"), and the crown itself: eight to eleven fronds, each a tapered strip arcing out and drooping, radiating from the trunk's tip. Every frond strip is **double-sided geometry** — each quad added twice, once per face — because a frond is one leaf-thickness of material read from both sides and the shared instancing path culls (the birds' wings solve the same problem with `CullNone`, which a draw sharing its rasterizer state with lathe-wound solids cannot take). Four variants at rolled proportions and structural seeds; the variety is in the mesh and never in a per-instance stretch, for the shader's inverse-transpose reason. `Palm.fx` is `Acacia.fx` plus **the sway**: each vertex carries a weight in its UV.x — zero along the trunk, rising along each frond — so the wind moves the crown and never the trunk (a palm whose whole body waves reads as a kelp), phased off the instance's own world position so a grove never beats in unison, off the wall clock like the clouds and the sea.

- **⚠ That weight lives in `TEXCOORD0`, an ordinary texture coordinate, and it is only a sway weight because `PalmMesh` chooses to bake one there — so `SwayStrength` is a per-DRAW argument and must never be a per-frame one.** `SwayStrength` was set once in `ApplyPalmFrame`, which `DrawTropicalRocks` also calls, so the palms' own strength reached the waterline boulders. Both a `RockMesh` and its moss cap **are** `LatheMesh`es (`RockMesh` hands out `_lathe`'s own buffers), and `LatheMesh` writes `s / segments` there — 0 to 1 **around the circumference**. So one side of every ring was displaced at full frond-tip weight while the other side stood still: the stones did not merely drift downwind, they **sheared open**, and they did it off the wall clock so it read as motion. Reported by the owner in as many words — "the rocks on the beach move with the wind, which is nonsense". `DrawPalmPart` takes `swayStrength` beside `dappleStrength` now, so no mesh can inherit a sway nobody meant it to have, and both the shader and the draw carry the warning. **Verified as a paired diff of two frames at different wall-clock times from one fixed camera**: over two boxes holding rocks, *not one pixel* changed by more than 24 (mean |d| 0.00 and 0.07), while the palm crowns in the same pair moved 18.7 % of their pixels past that threshold at a max of 198 — which is what says the test can see geometry that moves, rather than that nothing in the frame does. **The palms are planted only on dry sand** — a height test against the water level, which follows the wiggling waterline — clumped into groves with `ScatterSpacing`, sunk a fraction into the sand (the forest's own figure; planted at the exact surface a flared root reads as standing on a pinhead).

**The rocks with green tops are two meshes over one instance, not a shader trick.** Each waterline rock is a `RockMesh` (the forest's own boulder) plus a moss cap — a low lathe dome whose rim is buried in the stone's upper flank, on its own `irregularityPhase`, so where the green emerges over the grey is the two wobbles disagreeing and no two rocks share the line. The cap is drawn at 0.84 of the stone's radius with its rim well under the stone's surface *at that radius* — the stone is a dome, and a cap as wide as the stone itself would float over its flank. The rocks are planted in the **band straddling the waterline** (a height test again), so some stand half in the water — which is what the issue asked for and what a shore looks like — with a tumble a washed boulder has earned, on their own occupancy list (a boulder at a palm's foot is fine; the forest's split).

**It is an `IsSolidTerrainScene` — the first member of that family with water in it.** Under the island there is sand: the terrain cuts the island's footprint as every solid-terrain scene does, the dark pit shaft backs the drain's glass, and there is no dive for the drop cinematic (`OpenBelow` stays the complement). The lagoon's water starts past the beach, well outside the hole, so nothing about the shaft's reasoning changes. It takes the dome like every terrain scene, and **it forces the brightest one**: entering the tropical scene defaults to dome 1 (clear blue over a warm horizon) by the sea's and savanna's own mechanism, overridable as always — white sand and turquoise water are the postcard, and only read as one under a sunny sky. The flock circles the lagoon on the scene's own `BirdsConfig` (the shared state is sized from all four scenes that draw it now). Its ambience bed is the sea's wash at a calm remove — smaller swells, the foam hiss still a quarter turn behind — under the lightest warm breeze of the set.

- **Measured** (desktop GPU — Ryzen 9 5900X / Radeon RX 6900 XT — the Game's front end, windowed 1600×900, dome 13 pinned, `nocap`, ssaa 2, 14 s runs with the first four readings discarded): **tropical 501.4 FPS / 2.0 ms**, against **sea 598.4 / 1.7** on the same pass — i.e. desert-class (the desert measured 511.9 / 1.95 on this machine for #112) and inside the terrain-scene band, which is what a gentle-sloped grid plus the sea's own water plus ~110 palms and ~30 rocks in instanced draws should cost. No reduced program, for the same reason as the outback's: nowhere near the occupancy-bound class. No APU figure is claimed (the outback's caveat — the terrain scenes do not keep their order across machines).
- **Verified in captures, not by eye at the desk**: the play vantage shows the sand ring, 15–20 palms reading as palms and the turquoise water starting beyond the beach with no artifacts; a shore vantage along the waterline shows the mossy rocks strung through the surf line and the wet band on the sand; a high vantage outward shows the beach falling into the lagoon and the green ridge closing the horizon. The one fault the captures caught — palms planted at the exact surface reading as floating — was fixed by the forest's sink before anything was committed.


**#268 rebuilt how it READS, after looking at it from the play camera rather than from the vantages it was built against.** Four findings, three fixed, one disproved — and the disproved one is the most useful thing on this page.

- **The far ridge showed the lagoon its sandy flank.** The vegetation mask was `ring * fringe`, where `ring` is the ridge's own *rise* — the same 95-unit `RingWidth` ramp that lifts it in the height field — so greenness grew **outward**: zero at the ridge's inner edge and full only most of a hundred units further out, i.e. over the crest and beyond. The near flank, which is the whole of what the lagoon and the play camera look at, was sand by construction. Measured across the ridge band before the fix: **(204, 199, 168)**, red over green, with no green in it anywhere. It is two questions now, answered separately: a **short radial gate** says which landmass this is, and the ridge's own **height above the water** says how vegetated it is (`SHORE_SAND_FRINGE` 1.1, `JUNGLE_RISE` 4.0 — form constants, the meadow's rosette rule). The ridge stands 15–28 units out of the water, so all but its own beach comes up green.
- **And the haze was eating what the mask then delivered.** The final stage was `haze⁴` to the dome's exact horizon colour — the outback's arrangement, borrowed with its exponent. That is right for the outback, whose far field is empty plain; here the horizon **is** a feature, and the ridge stands at 300–420 units against a haze distance pinned at 480 by the grid's own half-extent, so at the fourth power its crest was already 48 % of the way to the sky and the jungle came out beige anyway. It is `haze⁸` in this scene, which still reaches 1 at the grid's edge — the seam it exists to hide is still hidden — and stops eating the ridge.
- **The lagoon was grey, and `WaterShallow` was never the fault.** `Sea.fx` gives its pale colour only to the up-facing faces of the swell, caps that at half and biases a calm patch darker still — right for water with nothing under it, wrong for a lagoon, so the basin was drawn mostly in `WaterDeep`, a dark navy. Measured across the water band: **(140, 146, 133)**, blue *below* red, against a turquoise sitting in the config the whole time. `Sea.fx` gained one uniform, **`ShallowBias`**, lifting the floor of that mix; the lagoon runs it at 0.78 and the open sea at 0, where `lerp(x, 1, 0)` is bit-exactly `x`. The lagoon's own body colours went up with it (a white-sand basin is far brighter than open water: `WaterDeep` 0.012→0.030 in green, `WaterShallow` 0.24→0.52), which is what actually delivers the turquoise — the bias alone moved it barely at all, because at these viewing angles the Fresnel sky reflection is most of what you see and only a much brighter body can compete with it.
  - **The open sea is unchanged, and that was checked rather than asserted** — a shared shader deserves it. A per-pixel diff of two runs is worthless here (the swell and the cloud deck both run off the wall clock, so two launches catch different phases: 45 % of pixels differ between two builds that are the same). The regional means over 62 400 water pixels are the honest instrument: **R 69.55 / G 88.77 / B 123.29 before against R 69.30 / G 88.49 / B 123.08 after**, i.e. a third of a level out of seventy — wave phase, not a shift.
- **The sand's ripple existed only as a tilt of the normal**, where `Desert.fx` learned to darken its own troughs as well — "with the normal tilt alone a dune's shadowed flank goes back to being a flat patch of colour", which on flat beach sand under a high sun is the whole story. It shades now (`RIPPLE_SHADE`). And that alone was not enough: the ripple's domain scale of 0.85 made a feature about **1.2 world units** across against the desert's ~3.9, so `Fbm2BandLimited`'s own contract faded it out at a third of the distance and the beach was smooth everywhere the eye looks. Coarsened to 0.28 at four octaves. Measured, sand-pixel luminance sd from the same vantage under the same dome: **4.42 original → 4.70 shaded → 4.92 coarsened**, against the desert's **5.98**.
- **The lagoon cannot be put into the play frame by moving the waterline, and #268 established that the expensive way — by trying it.** The island's stone cap stands 5 units over the beach and the play camera looks out *across* that cap; its far rim cuts the sight line and everything at the sand's own level behind it is gone. Proved with `arena=none`: the identical play-camera frame shows the lagoon plainly with the island hidden and none of it with the island drawn. Then `ShoreRadius` was brought 100 → 66 with the palm ring following it — **still no water**, because a nearer waterline is a nearer thing to hide — and the test was repeated on a small map in case the stand-off was the variable. Also none. All of it reverted. **What follows is worth keeping: from the play camera the only things visible past the island are things that STAND UP** — the palms, the far ridge, the sky. A flat beach and a flat lagoon are equally invisible whatever their radii, so the lagoon belongs to the raised vantages (the front end's orbit, the drop cinematic, the editor) and the far ridge is what the play camera actually gets. Which is why the ridge got the work.
- **Measured** (desktop — Ryzen 9 5900X / Radeon RX 6900 XT — Testbed, fixed camera, windowed 1600×900 at ssaa 4, dome 1, `fpscap=150`, 16 s runs, first four readings discarded, **paired** on the one pin): **119.8 FPS / 8.3 ms before against 117.2 / 8.5 after**, i.e. about **0.2 ms** for one more fBm octave and a handful of ALU on a full-frame terrain. It stays desert-class on the same pin (the desert reads 118.2 / 8.5, the sea 141.9 / 7.0). No APU figure is claimed, the outback's caveat holding.

## The volcano

The fifteenth `SceneKind` (#223): the island stands on the flank of an **erupting volcano**. A cone of black basalt rises a couple of hundred units off to one side with its crater against the sky, gullies raked down its flank; rivers of red-orange lava run in those gullies and out onto the plain, one of them passing the arena; lava fountains spurt on real ballistic arcs out of the crater and two side vents, a grey smoke column stands over them, and ash drifts through the whole frame. `Volcano.fx` + `LavaFountain.fx` + `Ash.fx` with `VolcanoSceneConfig` (named nested groups — Fountains, Eruption, Ash — the PropertyGrid rule), `scene=volcano` on any command line, on the Game's scene menu and in its random pick; like every scene past `CycleLength` the editor reaches it by loading a level naming it.

**It is the first scene whose ground is the light, and that is the whole reason it exists.** Every other backdrop takes its light from the sky and the strongest lamp in the game is the savanna's campfire; here the crater and the flows are real `SceneLights` point lights throwing red-orange **up** at the underside of the cluster, the island and the gun, while the dome goes on lighting their tops. Slot 0 is the crater (it takes the eruption envelope on top of its base glow); every other slot is a **flow front travelling downhill**, swelling in and dying out over its run so it arrives, passes and is gone — the first moving lamps in the game, and the reason `VolcanoLightPosition` takes a time at all. Slots 1 and 2 both ride the river aimed past the arena, a third of a span apart, so there is nearly always a front near the play field.

- **The readability constraint is a dial, not a taste.** Red and orange balls hang over red-orange lava. `VolcanoSceneConfig.LightStrength` (0.22) is what the ground is allowed to contribute, and it is set against the campfire — the only other lamp of this kind — which is (2.4, 1.0, 0.32) at range 32 where this is `LavaHot` × 0.22 at range 130. The first pass ran 0.55 at range 190 and turned the island's stone gold. Verified in capture with `Thirteen_Colors.json` hanging over the cone from the play camera: all thirteen read, the reds included.
- **The rivers run where the ground drains.** The gullies are a bearing term in the height field, and `SceneRenderer.SnapToGully` solves that same term for its floors and lays each river in one — four passes of a contraction, since the shape's `2·sin(3b)` bend is small against 22 gullies. Laid on arbitrary bearings the flows crossed the channels obliquely and read as paint on a cone; in the channels the ground dips where the lava is and the flank reads as drainage. River 0 is aimed past the arena and then checked: if the snap walks it inside the island plus two river widths, it takes the next gully out.
- **One crackle field at two temperatures** carries the "crust over liquid" read. `Noise.fxh`'s `VoronoiEdge2` is zero exactly on the borders between cells, so `1 - saturate(k · edge)` lights the web *between* plates: on the cold flank a dim seam glow between basalt plates, on a river the incandescent net between drifting rafts, scrolled downhill so the flow visibly moves. The rafts' cells are drawn **anisotropically** (short across the flow, long along it) because a raft on a moving flow is stretched downstream, and both fields' domains are **warped** by a coarse noise first — a Voronoi over a jittered lattice still has a lattice in it, and at this scale the flank came out as a regular honeycomb.
- **The summit shipped as a sharp point, and the crater term could never have fixed that on its own.** `VolcanoMassing`'s flank is `ConeHeight · pow(1 − r/ConeRadius, ConeProfile)` evaluated at the pixel's own radius `r`, which is maximal exactly at `r = 0` for any profile — its slope there is `−ConeProfile / ConeRadius`, never zero. Subtracting a crater term that is *also* maximal at `r = 0` cannot move the true summit off that point; it can only steepen the final approach to it, which is what a "crater" reading as a nozzle tip was — caught by looking at a capture, not by the maths. The fix clamps the radius the flank itself is evaluated at to `CraterRadius`, so the flank plateaus at its own rim height for every point inside it, and the crater is cut into *that* plateau (`smoothstep(CraterRadius, 0, r)`, a bowl full-depth at the vent and zero at the rim) rather than into a cone that kept climbing underneath it. Outside `CraterRadius` nothing changes — same flank, same rivers, same gullies — because the clamp is a no-op there. Mirrored in `SceneRenderer.VolcanoGroundY` as the file's own comment requires (vent and light placement read this, not the shader).
- **Three figures were wrong on the first pass and each was wrong for a stated reason.** The **haze** was the desert's 420 to the dome's own horizon colour, which under a cream horizon painted the entire cone the colour of sand — the volcano vanished and a dune stood in its place; it is 900 to `HorizonColor × HazeTint` now, an ash pall rather than the lit sky. The **halo** (how far a flow visibly heats the ground beside it) was six river-widths, and with the arena standing beside the river that passes it the halo covered the whole foreground and the entire plain glowed; it is three. And `LavaHot` was (7.5, 2.4, 0.3), which ACES takes straight to white-yellow — past a point, adding radiance to a saturated colour only desaturates it, and a white river is a lit crack rather than molten rock.
- **The eruption is an event on a schedule, and the schedule is a pure function of the wall clock.** `SceneRenderer.VolcanoEruption(time)` puts one burst in each period, hashing *where in the period* it starts and *how big it is* off the period's own index so it never becomes a metronome, with a fast attack and a long decay. One figure drives the jets' reach, the plume's density and the crater's light together, so a burst reads as one event — and because it holds no state, the game, the Testbed and the map editor all see the same eruption at the same second. **Light first, sound a beat behind**: the eruption *sound* is deliberately not built, because it is the same decision as #219's thunder and the two should share one mechanism rather than be invented twice. `VolcanoEruption` is public and is where it hangs.
- **The fountains are ballistic, in the vertex shader.** One static billboard buffer, its first slice drawn as the plume and the rest as the jets — two index ranges over the one buffer and two techniques, so neither pass touches the other's particles and neither carries a runtime branch. Each blob picks a vent, a lifetime, a launch azimuth and a tilt (sqrt-distributed, so the cone fills evenly rather than crowding its axis), and flies `p = vent + dir·v·t − ½g·t² + wind·drag·t²`; it is thrown white-hot and its colour *is* its age, which is what makes the top of a jet darker than its root with no extra particles. Life is set so most blobs land back near their vent, and the terrain's depth hides anything that goes below the surface.
- **`BlendState.AlphaBlend` in MonoGame is PREMULTIPLIED** — `(One, InverseSourceAlpha)` — so a shader returning a straight colour has that colour *added* at full strength on every layer. The smoke column was written non-premultiplied and 900 puffs of 0.085 grey came out as a **white steam plume brighter than the sky behind it**, which sent this looking for a bug in the colour being pushed. The plume and the ash both premultiply now. (`Snow.fx` and `Spray.fx` do not, and were left alone: they were tuned by eye against that behaviour, and #208 holds the snow.)
- **Its dome was chosen by looking** (#207's lesson), against 16 and 13: **dome 9**, a dim mauve-and-slate dusk with no bright band anywhere in it. Dome 16 is darker at the zenith but its horizon is a bright cream and it stands its sun disc up beside the cone, both of which compete with the crater for the eye. Entering the volcano defaults the dome to 9 by the sea's and savanna's own mechanism, overridable as always. It is an `IsSolidTerrainScene` — the island's footprint is cut out of the flank and the dark pit shaft backs the drain's glass — and it does **not** replace the sky.
- **Measured** (desktop GPU — Ryzen 9 5900X / Radeon RX 6900 XT — Testbed, **fixed camera**, windowed 1600×900 at ssaa 4 (23 Mpix, a 4K-class High load), dome 9 pinned, `fpscap=150`, 16 s runs with the first four readings discarded). Wide vantage with the whole cone in frame: **volcano 96.0 FPS / 10.4 ms**, desert 124.8 / 8.0, mountain 117.7 / 8.5. Play-level vantage beside the arena: **volcano 90.8 / 11.0**, desert 117.9 / 8.5. So it is the dearest terrain scene by about **2.5 ms** at this load — two `VoronoiEdge2` evaluations (nine hashes each) plus four gradient-noise taps for their warps, over a full-frame terrain. **No APU figure is claimed**, and that is the gap: the tiers exist for the weak machine and #209 defends a 75 FPS budget there, so the particle counts, the halo and the crackle are dials from day one and a reduced program is where a tier entry would go.
- **No levels play here yet.** The scene is built, reachable and shared; the campaign block that would use it is not written, because `Tools/LevelGen` and `Game/Levels` were held by another agent's work while this was made. That is a scene-and-a-block task, and the block is the second half of it.

## The storm

The seventeenth `SceneKind` (#219): the arena hangs in open air among broken cumulus, with lightning breaking through the space between the cells. `StormClouds.fx` + `StormSceneConfig` (named nested groups — Clouds, Surface, Flash, Air — the PropertyGrid rule), `scene=storm` on any command line, on the Game's scene menu and in its random pick; like every scene past `CycleLength` the editor reaches it by loading a level naming it.

**⚠ IT WAS BUILT AS TERRAIN FIRST, THE OWNER REJECTED IT ON SIGHT, AND THE REASON IS THE MOST TRANSFERABLE THING IN THIS SECTION.** The first build drew the storm as a displaced grid at the island's foot — the same camera-centred terrain mesh every ground sibling uses — with convective turrets standing out of it. It photographed as landscape from every camera the game has, and the verdict was "it does not look like clouds at all, more like some strange rocky land". **Three separate rounds of fixing its SHAPE each made it a better landscape and none of them made it cloud:**

- the turret profile was `smoothstep(1.0, shoulder, d)` — a smoothstep run *backwards*, which holds a flat crown inside `shoulder` and then falls off hard. Flat top, steep wall, hard silhouette: that is a **mesa**, and it photographed as a field of buttes. Rounding it to `pow(1 − d², k)` fixed the mesas and left snow;
- the folded-noise fields were written `1 − |n|`, which is **ridged** noise: it peaks along the noise's zero contours, and a zero contour of a smooth 2-D field is a *line*, so it draws ridges — it is the standard ridged-multifractal terrain primitive. `|n|` peaks at the noise's *extrema*, which are points, so it draws rounded blobs with creased troughs: a cumulus field. **One character, and it decides whether a white lumpy surface reads as rock or as cloud.** Written the wrong way round it came out as crumpled ice ridges, sharper and more obviously stone than the smooth version it replaced;
- and with both fixed it still read as a snowfield, because **the fault was never the shape**. A height field is a *surface*: one height per XZ, so it can be lumpy but it can never be **broken**, and torn cumulus with sky showing between the cells is not expressible in it at all. Nor can its silhouette be anything but a hard geometric edge, where a cloud's dissolves — that is a property of the medium, not of the outline. Brightening it made it snowier; lowering the whole deck bought nothing and left the pit shaft hanging in the air.

**So the clouds are volume now**, and the height field is gone with everything built on it (`Storm.fx`, the turret lattice, the anvil, the clearing, `StormDeckConfig`). What replaced it is soft-edged **billboard puffs clustered into cumulus cells**, alpha-blended, scattered through a volume around and below the arena with real gaps between them — the sea's spray and the mountain's snow pattern, which #151 measured 2000 of at exactly nothing.

**It is therefore no longer an `IsSolidTerrainScene`, and this is the one place the first build's own reasoning has to be read as retracted.** It argued the flag from what the deck *did* — it was drawn as a grid, sat at the terrain level, needed the island's footprint cut out of it and needed the pit shaft behind the drain's glass — and every one of those facts died with the deck. There is no surface to cut a footprint out of and nothing for an opaque shaft to stand in, so the drain looks straight through onto sky and cloud exactly as it does over the space scene (verified from a camera under the island). That makes `OpenBelow` true and hands the drop cinematic its dive to about y = −66 — which is fine here and was checked rather than assumed: the field's own `InnerRadius` keeps every cell at least 105 units out, so directly under the island there is open air, and the dive looks out at cloud rather than through it. It is still **not** `ReplacesSky` — the open air above the cells is the point.

- **A cell's shape is what makes it cumulus, and it is built rather than noised.** Puffs are laid on a profile widest through the middle and tapering at both ends, biased low so the body is heavier than the crown, with each puff's own radius shrinking as it climbs — that gradient *is* the cauliflower. Laying them in a plain ellipsoid gives a bun, which is what the height field's turrets already were.
- **⚠ Every puff carries the middle of the cell it belongs to, and without it the field reads as bubble wrap.** That is not a figure of speech — it is the first thing the eye names. A billboard shaded from its own disc alone gets a full light-to-dark gradient across it and a crisp circular edge, so a cell built of them is a heap of glossy balls. The shader reconstructs the sphere the disc is a section of (one square root) and then blends that normal towards the direction out of the cell's own middle (`MassNormalMix`), which makes the **cell** the thing being lit and lets the individual puffs disappear into it.
- **`EdgeSoftness` runs the opposite way from how it reads, and it was written down backwards once.** It is where a puff's falloff *starts*, so **low is soft and high is hard**: at 0.05 the puff fades from near its middle, at 0.9 it is a solid disc with a rim on it — and a field of those is the bubble wrap again. The outline also carries two turns of gradient noise on the quad's own corner, because a perfect circle reads as a sprite however well it is shaded; on the corner and not on world space, so it rides with the billboard instead of swimming across it as the camera turns.
- **The gaps are the subject.** What makes the scene read as cloud rather than as a ceiling is that sky shows *between* the cells and *through* them, so the two dials that matter most are the cell count against the field's radius and `PuffOpacity` (0.40, deliberately low). Filling the volume evenly turns a cloudscape back into a lid.
- **The field is static in the world and drifts bodily downwind in the vertex shader**, and is generated well past the 500-unit far plane on purpose: a wrap-around would tear a cell in half, which is the one artefact a cloud cannot survive. Unsorted, which is a real approximation and an acceptable one — every puff is the same near-white medium, so two of them the wrong way round changes the blend weights and nothing the eye can name. Depth-**read** and never depth-write: a soft-edged billboard that wrote depth would punch its own quad's silhouette out of everything behind it, which is the hard edge this whole scene exists to avoid.
- **The shared cloud field cannot be used for a deck below the arena, and there are three independent reasons — all read before anything was built.** (1) `Sky.fx`'s ray–plane crossing is not sign-safe from above: `climb = max(direction.y, 0.02)` is clamped *positive* while `(CloudPlaneY − CameraPosition.y)` goes negative once the plane is below the eye, so every direction returns a negative distance and samples a mirror point behind the camera. (2) A second gate makes it moot anyway — `smoothstep(0.0, CloudHorizonFade, direction.y)` is zero for every ray at or below the horizon, so the shared field draws **nothing** in the lower hemisphere. (3) `CloudSunlight()` does not return 1 above the plane; its own `max((CloudPlaneY − worldPosition.y) / climb, 0.0)` degenerates to sampling the point's own XZ column, which under a storm preset returns about `ShadowFloor` (0.16) — a deck field with a live coverage gain would put the arena, the balls and the gun at a permanent 16 % of sun. And the `Cloud*` uniform namespace on a scene effect belongs to the sky's weather regardless, the host pushing `frame.ApplyClouds?.Invoke(effect)` into every scene effect every frame. Hence own uniforms under own names, and **`DrawStorm` deliberately does not invoke the cloud hook** — this field does not want a cloud shadow cast on it, it *is* the cloud. All three reasons survived the rewrite unchanged: they are about the shared field's own gates, not about what the storm is drawn with. (Every preset's plane is *above* the arena: Scattered and Clear 190, Broken 175, Overcast 215, Storm 140.)
- **Its `Weather` is `Clear`, and left alone it would have been actively wrong.** The base class defaults to `Scattered`, so a scene whose whole subject is a storm below the arena would have drawn Earth cumulus *above* it at the same time and sandwiched the player between two unrelated weathers. Mars overrides it for the same class of reason.
- **It brought a twentieth `SkyDome` palette, and dome 11 is the proof that it needed one.** Every one of the nineteen existing domes warms towards its horizon, because that is what haze at sea level does; at altitude the horizon goes **paler and bluer** instead. The reason it was originally *forced* was the grid: a terrain scene's distance fade must arrive at the dome's exact `HorizonColor` to hide the finite mesh's own edge, so whatever the dome's horizon is, is also what every far surface becomes — and under dome 11 (the set's brightest daylight, and the first pick here) **the cloud deck photographed as beige desert dunes**. ⚠ **That forcing argument died with the grid** — a field of billboards has no mesh edge to hide and never has to reach the horizon colour — but the dome stays, now on the plainer ground that a pale blue-white horizon is what altitude actually looks like and is what keeps white cloud reading as white cloud. Dome 20 is a deep blue zenith over a pale blue-white horizon, generated the way dome 19 was: colour as a function of captured vertex height rather than painted by eye. The scene also carries its own `HazeTint` for the mid-distance so it stays cloud-coloured under any of the twenty — the outback's two-stage arrangement, and its recorded trap that aerial perspective can behave correctly and still give the wrong picture.
- **The flash is an event on a schedule, and the schedule is a pure function of the wall clock.** `SceneRenderer.StormFlash(time)` puts one strike in each period, hashing *where in the period* it starts and *how big it is* off the period's own index so it never becomes a metronome, with a near-instant attack, a fast decay and a **flicker** over the envelope — real lightning is a train of return strokes, and that stutter is most of what separates it from a light being switched on. Holding no state, the Game, the Testbed and the map editor all see the same strike at the same second. It is `public` for the reason `VolcanoEruption` is: so a sound can hang off it without owning the schedule.
- **It reaches the arena as a `SceneLights` point light far below, on the planetshine recipe — because that is the only channel that works when the deck is out of the play frame.** `TryGetStormFlash` mirrors `TryGetMoonEarthshine`, and `SceneLights.Apply` gained a sixth branch that is self-gating in **time** as well as in kind: between strikes it returns false and the scene takes no slot at all. A lamp far along −Y gives `dot(N, L)` ≈ 1 on every downward-facing normal and ≈ 0 on every upward one, which is precisely "the deck below flashed" — the undersides of the balls, the island's coping and the gun's carriage catch it and their tops keep the dome. ⚠ **The range must comfortably exceed the distance or the lamp arrives as literally nothing**: the shader's attenuation is `saturate(1 − dist/range)²`, exactly 0 at `dist ≥ range`, hence the 3× recipe.
- **Three figures were wrong on the first pass and each was found by measurement rather than by eye.** The **glow's reach** was derived as `TurretSpacing × 0.8` = 136 units, which sounded like "its own cell and a little beyond" and was in fact a patch *smaller than the distance to the strike* (a strike stands 173–348 units out), so the glow landed almost entirely off frame and the flash read as simply not working — diagnosed by deleting the falloff outright, whereupon the identical build drove the deck's mean luminance from 189 to 255, which is what said the plumbing was sound and only the radius wrong. It is its own dial (`GlowReach`, 430) now. The **cloud was overexposed** at a near-white 250 of 255, so the flash had nowhere to go; `TopColor` and `AmbientStrength` both came down. And the **glow's strength** window is narrower than it looks: at 2.6 a strike moved the mean by 3 of 255 and was invisible, at 14 it washed the near cloud to featureless white and took its form with it. 7 works because the term is weighted towards the *shaded* side — a discharge lights cloud from within, so it shows where the sun does not already.
- **⚠ And a strike goes off INSIDE A CELL, not at a hashed radius.** Placed by radius and bearing alone it lands in clear air about as often as in cloud, and a discharge with nothing around it lights nothing — which matters more than it sounds, because from most cameras the glow *is* the flash, the channel itself being inside the cell it went off in. The cells' own middles are kept when the field is generated (`_stormStrikeCells`, those within 420 units) and the strike picks one off the same period index everything else about it is hashed from.
- **The channel is drawn, and it is generated entirely in the vertex shader.** #219 asked for it in as many words, and the first build left it out with the note that light with no source in the frame reads as the sun blinking. The buffer carries only which bolt a vertex belongs to, how far down it stands and which side of the channel it is; the path — the lean, the fork's spread, the stepped-leader kinks, how far each branch reaches — is hashed off the strike's own period index, so a bolt costs no per-frame CPU work, is frozen for the length of one flash, and is the same one in every executable at the same second. It is a camera-facing **ribbon** rather than a tube: a lightning channel is a filament far thinner than a pixel at this distance, and what is actually being drawn is its glare. Additive, because a discharge adds light to what is behind it and never hides it — drawn with alpha over cloud it reads as a painted stripe — and drawn at all only while an envelope is running, so between strikes it is the whole cost of the lightning.
- **⚠ The channel has to run through the band the cells occupy.** Its first cut ran from above the arena down past the whole field, which put its brightest end in clear air over the island and its tail below anything a camera can see, so what reached the frame was a white line crossing the arena rather than a discharge inside the weather. It runs `LayerTopY + 8` down to `LayerBottomY + 18` now.
- **The flash was verified deterministically rather than by catching a strike, and the reason is worth keeping for anyone testing a short event here: nothing in `TestOptions` pins the wall clock, and a 0.45 s strike is shorter than the capture harness's own timing slop.** Four captures aimed at a computed strike peak came back identical to 0.1 of a luminance mean. So the period was temporarily collapsed until a strike is always running, and the scene shot with `DeckGlow` at 7 and at 0: deck mean luminance **189.4 against 155.5**, a 33.9 difference of 255, independent of when the frame landed. (The volcano only escaped this because its burst lasts 4.5 s.) A `time=` pin on `TestOptions` is what would make this a one-shot check; it is not built.
- **Measured, and the medium cost about a millisecond** (reference APU — Ryzen 7 5700U + integrated Radeon — Testbed, fixed camera at the play pose `campos=0,-4,30 camtarget=0,-8,0`, dome 20, windowed 1600×900 at ssaa 2, `fpscap=150`, 110 s runs with the first 50 readings discarded, median of the rest, two passes). The **terrain build against the cloud build, both drawn in the same session out of two worktrees**: **26.56 ms against 27.55** — 26.316/26.810 and 27.548/27.548, so the spread is under 2 % and the difference is real. Eleven and a half thousand alpha-blended billboards for one millisecond is the same not-fill-bound answer #104, #151 and the sea's own 2000-particle spray all got on this class of machine. In the same session's three-scene pin the storm sits at **30.03 ms against Mars's 33.11 and the meadow's 25.93** — cheaper than the scene before it, well inside the terrain band. ⚠ **The two sessions' absolutes differ by 2.5 ms for the same scene and that is the machine, not the build**: this laptop's iGPU shares one 15 W package budget with its CPU, so only figures taken in one session may be compared (the trap is #151's, written up in the benchmark skill). No desktop figure is claimed.
- **⚠ The thunder is NOT built, and that is a stated gap rather than an oversight.** #219 asks for it and #223 deliberately deferred its own eruption sound on the explicit promise that the two would share one mechanism and land together — so it is now a debt owed by two issues. The design is researched and specific: bake it as a one-shot in `Game/Audio/ProceduralAudio.cs` beside `BakeFireworkBurst` (that file already holds `Loudness` and `RollingEcho`, which is exactly what a thunder roll is), schedule it with the Fireworks' own countdown pattern in a small new `SceneEventSounds.cs` ticked from `BS3DGame.Update` right after the ambience, unplaced for thunder and placed for the eruption, scaled by a new `WeatherGain` off the existing Ambience row and authored quiet. What must **not** be done is mixing it into the ambience bed: that bed is a sealed 16-second loop, so an event baked into it repeats on a fixed interval — a metronome, the very failure the hashed schedule exists to avoid.
- **Its ambience bed is thin high-altitude wind** — a wide steady rush with none of the desert's grit and none of the sea's rhythm, because at this altitude there is nothing for the wind to move against, under a very low near-steady swell that is the deck itself working.

## Mars

The sixteenth `SceneKind` (#277): rust-red cratered ground scattered with dark stone, under a dusty butterscotch sky with two small moons in it. `Mars.fx` + `MarsSceneConfig` (named nested groups — Terrain, Surface, Air, Moons — the PropertyGrid rule), `scene=mars` on any command line, on the Game's scene menu and in its random pick; like every scene past `CycleLength` the editor reaches it by loading a level naming it.

**It is deliberately not a red-palette copy of the Moon (#125), and the reason is atmospheric rather than aesthetic.** The real Moon has no air at all, which is the *only* reason that scene replaces the sky and closes its horizon with planetary curvature (see "The Moon (Game)"). Mars keeps a thin one, so it stays an ordinary `IsSolidTerrainScene`: real ground under a real dome, the island's footprint cut out of it, the dark pit shaft backing the drain's glass, and no dive for the drop cinematic. What *does* transfer from the Moon is the crater field itself (`CraterLayer`/`TurnCrater`/`CraterField`/`MareBase`) — lifted into `Mars.fx` **verbatim**, because it is generic height-field math with nothing Moon-specific in it; only the colour it is coloured by and the constants that tune it are Mars's own. What does not transfer is the Moon's highland belt and curvature, both of which exist purely to close a horizon with no air to fade into — Mars closes its horizon the ordinary way instead, the outback's own two-stage haze fade to the dome's exact `HorizonColor`.

- **A stone field stands on the plain, and it is the outback's `RockLayer` lattice, ported verbatim a second time.** Real rover photographs are not a skyline of a few tall formations but ground littered with rock at every scale, so this is retuned rather than reused as-is: two lattices (`RockSpacing`/`RockChance`/`RockHeight` for boulders, a second finer `PebbleSpacing` tier rotated against the first for the litter between them), both with `ribDepth` 0 and a low `maxElongation` — Mars rock is wind-worn, not water-cut, so there are no gullies and no desert varnish to speak of. The stones are coloured **dark volcanic basalt** rather than a darker version of the ground's own rust (`BoulderColorDeep`/`BoulderColorBright`): real Mars rock is the one thing on the plain that is not red, dusted with the same colour everything else wears, and that contrast is most of what makes a scattered field read as *rock* rather than as lumps of the dust it stands in. This was added after the scene first shipped bare and cratered (the issue's own stated fallback) — a first look at it read as a plain missing exactly this.
  - **The boulders are sized for the litter they are, not for the mesh they stand on, and that costs something up close.** `MARS_GRID_N`/`MARS_EXTENT` give a cell of about 2.8 world units — sized for the crater field's tens-of-units features, the same grid every camera-centred terrain scene here shares. A 3–6 unit boulder spans only one or two of those cells, so its *silhouette* (real mesh geometry, coarse) reads softer up close than its *shading* (a per-pixel normal off the continuous height function, smooth) would suggest — a gap the outback's own monoliths never hit, because theirs are tens of units across against the same cell. At the distances the play camera actually holds — the island's own foot to the haze, never a lens standing on top of a single stone — this does not show; it would if anything here were framed for a close inspection of one rock; it deliberately is not (`docs/scenes.md`'s convention: measured, not assumed — this line records the boundary that was checked before shipping past it).
- **The sky is a nineteenth `SkyDome` palette, not a new code path.** None of the eighteen existing ones is right for a dust-scattering Martian daytime sky, which gets *brighter* near the horizon and dimmer at the zenith — the opposite of Earth's blue-zenith Rayleigh falloff. Nothing in `SkyDome.ApplyPalette`/`BuildRamp` assumes which end is which; they only average and resample whatever 92-vertex palette they are handed. So the nineteenth entry is pure **data**, generated as a function of each captured vertex's own height (five colour stops interpolated by the vertex's share of the mesh's Y range) rather than hand-painted, because no `.dae` for a Martian sky ever existed to paint by eye. Its own sun (#220) sits high — `(58°, 50°)` — a thin-aired midday sun rather than one of the set's many dusks.
- **Phobos and Deimos are not the Moon's Earth, and copying that mechanism would have been the wrong instinct.** The Earth is drawn inside `MoonSky`, a full-screen sky-*replacement* pass that owns the entire pixel — it only exists because the Moon has no dome to draw instead. Mars keeps its dome, so its two moons are their own small technique (`MarsMoons`) sharing the sky-replacing scenes' full-screen quad and `InverseViewProjection` machinery, **depth-read** against what `MarsTerrain` just wrote (the Moon's own measured reason — depth-read after the ground, never before it) and, unlike the Moon's opaque sky pass, **alpha-blended**, because this composites two small discs over a dome and terrain that are already drawn rather than replacing the frame. Each moon is `MoonDisc`, the Earth's ray-sphere test stripped of continents, weather and an atmosphere rim — neither moon has an atmosphere of its own to put one on.
  - **Neither moon is drawn at its true angular size, and that is a deliberate reversal of the Earth's own rule.** The real Phobos subtends at most ~0.2° and Deimos under 0.06° — a twentieth and a hundredth of the Earth's own carefully-kept 30-pixel disc, i.e. sub-pixel points that would simply never resolve. They are sized instead for the same legibility the Earth was sized *down* to, just from the other direction (`MarsMoonsConfig`'s own doc has the figures).
- **The stone field's cost lands inside the terrain-scene band, not outside it.** Measured on the reference APU (Ryzen 7 5700U + integrated Radeon), Testbed, the play-camera vantage (`campos=0,-4,30 camtarget=0,-8,0`), dome 13 pinned (so the comparison is the terrain shader's own cost, not two different domes), windowed 1600×900 at ssaa 2, `fpscap=150`, 16 s runs with the first four readings discarded: **Mars 32.1 FPS / 31.2 ms** against **outback 31.5 / 31.7** on the same pin — the same class, Mars if anything a shade cheaper despite the added stone lattices, because it carries none of the outback's gully/varnish/heat-shimmer terms. Not run against a paired alternation (`alt=`), so a half-millisecond either way is inside this machine's own run-to-run noise rather than a claimed ranking; what the figure supports is "the same band", not "faster than".
- **What #277 leaves out on purpose, and it is one real gap rather than several now**: a foreground dust-haze/dust-devil overlay (`Spray.fx`'s or the volcano's ash's machinery). Left for later — a bare, stoneless plain would have been the gap that mattered, and that one did not ship.

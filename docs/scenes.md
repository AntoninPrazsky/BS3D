# The scenes

The eleven backdrops the game, the Testbed and the map editor all share, plus the island the gun stands on and the glass drain in the middle of it.

Part of the BS3D documentation. CLAUDE.md holds the project overview, the build commands, the ball grid and the repo-wide conventions, and says which of these documents covers what.

## The setting, in one copy

Every executable used to build the setting for itself, and #75 ended that the way #74 ended three copies of the frame's exit (see "Color management" in `docs/rendering.md`). The island's stone cap and concrete drum, the glass drain with its two gold beads and the dark pit shaft behind it (`Prazsky.Core.Render.ArenaIsland`, which owns every mesh, texture, renderer, world matrix and figure of them); the glass plate a cluster hangs from (`CeilingPlate`); the dome-derived light rig, its hemisphere scales, its tint strength and its overcast palette (`SkyLightRig`); a scene's own point lights (`SceneLights`); the drain's collision mesh (`Prazsky.BS3D.Physics.FunnelPhysics`); the ten look values of the cloud deck and the two applies that push them (`CloudField`, which already owned the field's shape); the forest's wood — the fifteen tree, boulder and stump meshes, the twenty-five renderers that dress them, the three matte materials, the encoded tints and the six instanced draws (`ForestScatterRenderer`, the last piece of #75, and the one whose absence had left two of the three executables drawing a bare clearing — see "The forest"); and every question anybody asks about a `SceneKind` — `IsSolidTerrainScene`, `OpenBelow`, `SceneCount`, `CycleLength`, `SceneName`, `TryParseScene`, all statics on `SceneRenderer` now — are one copy each in the libraries. They had been identical value for value, which is not something a *shared* setting should ever have been able to be, and the duplication had already cost twice: the forest was missing from **both** hand-kept `IsSolidTerrainScene` lists (below), and the seven-of-eleven scene cycle was a bare `% 7` in two files.

What the hoist deliberately did **not** move is the frame sequence. `ArenaIsland` hands its draws over in three slices — `DrawIsland`, `DrawPit`, `DrawGlass` — precisely so each executable goes on placing them itself: the Game puts the gun, the cluster and the shot trails between the pit and the glass, and its front end runs the same frame with nothing between them at all. Sky-lit enrolment stays the caller's for the same reason, each having its own list and its own reasons (the Testbed's ceiling is always there, the Game's is rebuilt per level and may be null, the editor lights the balls, the city and the forest's scatter, there being no island, drain or ceiling of its own to light) — and all of them leave the pit shaft out, so no dome bleaches the inside of a hole in the ground. Every scatter renderer takes part wherever the wood stands, walked as the one flat array `ForestScatterRenderer.Renderers` hands back, or a spruce of the variant the list missed would stand under the rig of whatever dome was up when it was made — and since a re-planted wood is twenty-five brand-new renderers, whoever re-plants one has to re-light it too, exactly as a refitted `CeilingPlate` must be.

Three things came out of reconciling the copies:

- **One deliberate visible change, and it is an improvement to the Testbed rather than a no-op — the old number must not be "restored".** The two copies disagreed on exactly one figure: the ground-proximity darkening anchor the balls' and the gun's renderers are handed (`InstancedModelRenderer.GroundHeight`). The Testbed was passing **−9.49**, a value its own comment admitted was kept from the old ground-block layout whose recessed centre block's top sat at −9.5 — geometry deleted long since, so the referent no longer existed. It is the island's own top (`ArenaIsland.TOP_Y`, −8.5) in both executables now. Measured against the shader's ground-occlusion range of 2.0: a ball resting on the stone was getting a proximity of `1 − 0.99/2 = 0.505` instead of `1.0`, i.e. about half the contact darkening it should have, and the effect faded out at y = −7.49 instead of at −6.5. The Game was already correct. (The anchor stays `TOP_Y` now that the walkable top dishes below it — the shader's term saturates under the anchor, so a ball rolling down the dish keeps full contact darkening rather than an overdriven one.)
- **`OpenBelow` is defined as the complement of `IsSolidTerrainScene`, not as a second list.** It was two hand-kept sets in different files, and the negation is a consequence rather than a shortcut: the opaque near-black pit shaft those scenes need is exactly what closes the view from below, so a scene that wants the shaft cannot also give the drop cinematic a vantage under the stone. A twelfth scene is one decision now instead of two that can quietly disagree. Split them again only if a scene ever wants the shaft and the dive both, and say why on the spot.
- **`SceneFrame.SunColor` and the clouds' lit side are one number** (`SkyLightRig.SunRadianceTinted`): the sun's own radiance carried towards the dome's horizon colour by `SkyLightRig.SKY_TINT_STRENGTH`, which is what makes the deck lit by literally the same light as everything under it. They were two separately-computed expressions that happened to agree, written out twice per executable, and unifying them took a per-frame `Lerp` **and** a per-frame delegate allocation off the draw path in all three: the cloud hook a `SceneFrame` carries is set once at load on the rig, where a method group written at the call site built a fresh delegate every frame it was evaluated. It is taken off the **dome** and never off `KeyTint`, even though the two are the same expression wherever no scene overrides the rig — a sky-replacing scene states its own key tint precisely because a dome-derived one would be a lie, and folding that override into the sun would change what the terrain shaders are told the sun is.

Verified at runtime in all three executables, and the figures reconciled: every numeric literal of the old island code survived onto `ArenaIsland` at the hoist (the dish has since added `DISH_DEPTH` and moved the drain family that far below `TOP_Y` — see "The city and the glass arena"), and the Game still reports 315 balls / 1088 constraints on `One.json`.

## The city and the glass arena

The default of the seven settings (NumPad2 cycles through the sea, the savanna, the desert, the mountains, the meadow and the neon city, below) — no flag: a **small round stone island** (the drain funnel ringed by a moulded stone platform, below) in a clearing between the towers of a procedural city (`Prazsky.Core.Render.City`, shared with the map editor; see "The shared scene backdrops"). The arena used to be a big square marble-and-glass plaza sized to the ground blocks, but it ate the whole lower frame and hid the scene it stood in; it is now the round island in **every** scene, so the sea, the dunes or the city fill the frame rather than a plaza. The castle backdrop and the shadow overlay are gone, and so are the marble ground blocks — the island's only physics floor is the funnel's own mesh (see "The drain funnel").

The city stands **over** the arena and falls away **underneath** it at the same time, and both halves of that took a correction. The first version cleared the blocks under the glass, which left nothing to look through the glass at; the city now continues underneath, cut off far below so the drop reads as a drop. The second version put the roofline below the floor, so no tower was ever seen against the sky — only bright roofs with dark canyons beside them, which the eye reads as holes punched in a white plain rather than as buildings (it was reported as inverted normals, and it was not). `ROOFLINE_Y` therefore sits well above the play surface.

The island is a **cast-concrete drum with a dressed stone top and a moulded coping**, built by `Prazsky.Core.Render.IslandMesh` and assembled by `ArenaIsland`, which the Testbed and the Game both draw and the map editor deliberately does not (above). Its top is `ArenaIsland.TOP_Y` (−8.5) — a plain figure of the island's own now rather than something derived from the ground blocks that are long deleted, and the plane of the stone top's **outer arris**, from which the walkable top falls `ArenaIsland.DISH_DEPTH` (1.2) to the drain: the drain's rim, its gold bead and the pit's mouth all share the lower plane `TOP_Y − DISH_DEPTH`, and the collision floor runs between the two by construction (before the dish all four were flush at `TOP_Y`, which is what the Testbed's old `FUNNEL_TOP_Y`/`PIT_TOP_Y` aliases were saying). The top runs from the funnel's rim (`ArenaIsland.FUNNEL_TOP_RADIUS`) out to `ArenaIsland.RADIUS` and the platform drops `ArenaIsland.EDGE_HEIGHT`. The old marble-and-glass panel mosaic, its four marble bands, its mullions and the recessed centre "bath" are all gone, and with them the whole square-plaza machinery (`ARENA_HALF_EXTENT`/`ARENA_PANEL_SIZE`/the panel loop). What replaced *that* was a plain extruded washer (`DiscMesh`, now deleted), and it read as exactly what it was — a cylinder with a hole in it, every edge a raw 90° cut.

- **The shaping is all downward, and that is forced.** The physics floor mirrors the walkable top exactly (its outer arris at the top plane), so anything raised above it would be a lip balls pass straight through; every moulding is therefore cut *below* the top face — the walkable top itself included, which is the dish falling `DISH_DEPTH` from its arris to the drain's mouth so a landed ball rolls in. Outward from the arris: a chamfer, the coping's wash, a bullnose turning down to the platform's widest point, the coping's vertical face, then a **drip** where it oversails the concrete. The drum is battered slightly inward, flares into a string course a third of the way down, and **splays into a plinth** whose foot returns to very nearly the coping's radius.
- **What makes an edge read here is the hemisphere ambient, not a shadow** — there are no drawn shadows in this scene. An upward-facing chamfer takes the sky and draws a bright ring; the underside of the coping's overhang takes the ground bounce and draws a dark one. That pair of lines around the rim is most of the difference between a platform and an extruded circle, and it costs nothing.
- **The plinth flares rather than tapering, for two reasons.** A base that widens carries weight; one that narrows reads as balanced on a point. And the foot's radius is what covers the hole the five solid-terrain scenes cut out of the ground (`TerrainHoleRadius`): the island floats half a unit over that ground, so a foot drawn in much narrower than the platform opens a slot at every grazing angle and the sky shows through it as a pale ring. An earlier profile chamfered in to `r - 1.15` and did exactly that.
- **The physics ring stops at `ArenaIsland.FLOOR_RADIUS`, not at `ArenaIsland.RADIUS`** — `IslandMesh.FloorRadius` of it, the dish's own outer arris rather than the edge of the platform. Carried out to the widest point it would hold a ball up on air over the coping's wash.
- **Two meshes because it is two materials**, split at the coping's drip and built from one polyline so they cannot drift apart: the stone cap (coursed slab joints, the sky reflection turned down so it is matte) and the concrete drum (no joints — it is cast, not laid — a coarser relief and barely any reflection). `Prazsky.Core.Render.LatheMesh` is the general machinery: a surface of revolution from a cross-section polyline, per-point creases, and an optional radial irregularity (a function of angle and *world height*, so two lathes sharing an edge are displaced identically along it) that keeps the drum from reading as a machined part. Its triangles are wound clockwise from outside, so unlike `DiscMesh` the island now takes ordinary back-face culling — which is what would *show* a winding mistake rather than hide one.

Three things it cost to get right, all worth knowing before touching any other stone surface:

- **`Ground_8.png` does not tile, and never did.** The stone covers only the left ~1073 columns of its 2048² canvas; the rest is pure black. The triplanar projection therefore multiplied roughly half of every surface it was mapped onto by zero — which is what left the island's wall reading as a black band, at *any* detail scale, since no scale avoids the black. Both members now use `Prazsky.Core.Render.SurfaceTexture` instead: procedural concrete and stone generated at load on a noise lattice that wraps at the texture edge, so they tile by construction, with a linear-space mip chain. No content-project change in either executable, and no asset to get wrong.
- **That broken texture was silently acting as an exposure control.** Same vantage, same nominal albedo, only the texture swapped: the top face measured 151 grey with the photograph and 181 with a texture whose mean is 1 by construction — half a stop brighter for a number nobody changed. **Any albedo carried over from a surface that used it arrives overexposed**; the island's came down accordingly. `SurfaceTexture.LinearMean` exists so `DetailBoost = 1 / LinearMean` makes the texture a pure modulation about 1, leaving the material colour the honest albedo and `DetailStrength` a pure contrast dial.
- **A neutral vertical surface in this world reads distinctly warm, and that is the light rig, not the material.** The key light is carried halfway to the horizon colour (`SkyLightRig.SKY_TINT_STRENGTH`) and half of the hemisphere ambient a vertical face sees is the ground bounce, which is that horizon again. Measured in one frame: the cannon's steel 89,57,35 and the island's concrete 82,51,31, with the city's facades in the same family. A first pass read this as a fault in the concrete and pushed its albedo blue to cancel it — which would have made the platform the one object in the scene not taking the light everything else does. The albedo is a plain concrete grey, within a hair of `CannonRig.STEEL_COLOR`. Two more dials that misled on the way: the relief is a **sum of sines**, and past about 0.02 it stops reading as a rough surface and starts reading as the waves it is made of (a regular diagonal weave across the whole drum, at 0.045); and because the **specular ambient is not multiplied by albedo**, it and not the material colour is the dial that decides how *polished* a face reads — halving the stone's colour barely dimmed a top face whose brightness was mostly that term.

Note `ReliefShadowStrength` and `ParallaxScale` were dropped from both renderers rather than carried over: the triplanar path builds its own height field and never runs the self-shadow or parallax marches, so both were dead where they were being set. Measured on `Dense20x10x15` in the city, uncapped: 188.2 FPS before the rework and 189.8 after — one extra draw call and a couple of thousand triangles against a 3000-ball scene.

## The drain funnel (Testbed)

Where the recessed square used to be, the centre of the arena is a **glass funnel** the shot balls fall into: they run down the cone to a hole at the bottom and drop through it, below the platform, into the drop — where the kill plane removes them. It is what replaced the flat bath that just let balls pile up.

- **Rendering** is `Prazsky.Core.Render.FunnelMesh` — a single-sided truncated-cone wall (a round rim flush with the stone dish's low inner lip, `ArenaIsland.TOP_Y − DISH_DEPTH`, down to a small hole at `ArenaIsland.FUNNEL_BOTTOM_Y`, which the dish takes nothing off) with inward-and-up normals, and **no collar** now (`squareHalf` 0): the round stone dish meets the round rim directly, so there are no square corners to fill. The rim's height below the stone's arris is `ArenaIsland.DISH_DEPTH`, the one figure the dish added — the drain's mouth, the pit's mouth and the dish's lip stay one plane by construction, carried by the drain family's own world matrix (`_drainWorld`), while the island's mesh keeps the arris plane (`TOP_Y`). It is drawn translucent through its own `InstancedModelRenderer` at `ArenaIsland.FUNNEL_GLASS_ALPHA` (~0.55, more opaque than plain glass, so the funnel reads clearly as a frosted-glass drain). Because it is one open surface it is drawn with `RasterizerState.CullNone` so it reads both looking down into it and up through the hole, and it is one of the four renderers `ArenaIsland` hands the caller's sky-lighting pass (`SkyLitRenderers`/`AppendSkyLitTo`) so its sheen takes the dome.
- The rim (radius `ArenaIsland.FUNNEL_TOP_RADIUS`, 14) meets the stone dish's inner lip exactly, and the gold top-rim bead rings that stone-meets-glass junction — `DISH_DEPTH` below the stone's arris now, sunk at the centre of the dish — so the drain reads at a glance where the two materials meet. (The old square-collar machinery that filled the corner gaps between the round funnel and the square pit is gone with the square plaza.)
- **Gold rims, and they lie flat on the surface rather than standing on it (#94).** A polished-gold band runs around **both circles** — the wide top rim and the small bottom hole — which is what makes the glass drain read at a glance (`FunnelRimsMesh`: two bands in one mesh, at the funnel's own local y = 0 and y = -height, so they share the drain family's world matrix — `TOP_Y − DISH_DEPTH`, the glass and the pit under the same one; one gold `InstancedModelRenderer` draws them). Each was a **torus** until #94, a round bead revolved around its own tube as well as around the ring, and the shape was wrong for where it sits: it stood a half-unit proud at the exact junction every released ball is *guaranteed* to cross on its way into the drain, and nothing underneath it agreed — `FunnelPhysics.Build` collides the smooth cone and the smooth dished stone and says so itself, so the bead was a lip a ball rolled straight through. Each band now lies **on the surface that actually meets it there**: the top one on the stone dish, tilted to `ArenaIsland.DISH_GRADE` (derived from `DISH_DEPTH` over the dish's run, so it cannot drift from `FloorHeightAt`), the bottom one on the glass cone at the cone's own grade, which `FunnelRimsMesh` works out from the three figures that already describe it — which is why only the dish's grade is a constructor argument. Each is lifted `SURFACE_LIFT` (0.05, a tenth of a ball radius) along its surface normal, because coplanar geometry z-fights across the ring's whole width rather than at a fringe. Widths carry the tori's read across (`FUNNEL_RIM_TOP_WIDTH` 1.0 = the old top bead's diameter, `FUNNEL_RIM_HOLE_WIDTH` 0.6), and it is **16× less geometry** — two rings of quads against two double-revolved tori. What it cost is the bead's roundness: a tube curving through every angle always caught the light somewhere, so it glinted, where a band on a shallow dish holds one near-constant normal all the way round and reads as flatter, more uniform gold. That is the trade, and it is the right way round — the colour and the metalness are what make the drain legible, not the cross-section. They are **opaque**, so they draw with the opaque scene *before* the glass, and `CullNone` — which is now load-bearing rather than merely convenient: a zero-thickness annulus has one face, and culling off is what lets the top band read from under the island as well as from above it. `ArenaIsland.DrawGlass` puts both draws inside **one** `CullNone` sandwich, restored to `CullCounterClockwise` after, which is image-identical to the Testbed's two separate pairs because the two draws want culling off for different reasons and one setting satisfies both. They are drawn **metallic**, which needed a shader addition: `InstancedModel.fx` now has a `Metalness` uniform (a renderer property, set unconditionally every `Draw` like `SpecularAmbientStrength`, **0 for every other surface** so nothing else changes) that makes the environment-reflection F0 the material's specular color itself rather than the 4 % dielectric `DielectricF0 * tint` — so gold reflects the sky *in gold*. The gold specular (`ArenaIsland.FUNNEL_RIM_SPECULAR`) is passed as a per-draw `BasicEffectParams` override; the gold diffuse (`ArenaIsland.FUNNEL_RIM_COLOR`) keeps the bead visible even under a dark dome. The rims are visual only — no physics; the stone dish and the cone are the floor, which since #94 is what their geometry says too.
- **Physics** is a Bepu triangle-`Mesh` (`Prazsky.BS3D.Physics.FunnelPhysics.Build`, one copy for both executables since #75) — the island's whole physics floor now that the wide grid of ground blocks is gone: the sloped cone wall plus the **dished stone ring** from the rim up to `ArenaIsland.FLOOR_RADIUS`, all **double-sided** — eight triangles a segment, every quad's two added in both windings — deliberately, so a ball collides with the surface regardless of Bepu's one-sided-triangle normal convention rather than depending on getting the winding right. It is handed the very figures `ArenaIsland` draws from, `DISH_DEPTH` included, so the drawn surface and the collided one cannot drift. A ball that lands on the ring **rolls down its dish into the glass** — Bepu spheres carry no rolling resistance (friction resists sliding, not rolling), so the ~6.4° grade is plenty — then within the rim it runs down the cone to the hole (wider than a ball) and falls through; past the ring it drops off the island's edge into the drop and the kill plane takes it. The dish is what made the drain work for big maps at all: on the flat ring a ball parked where it stopped, and the wider the field the larger the share of released balls that never reached the mouth's radius 14. `KILL_PLANE_Y` sits below `ArenaIsland.FUNNEL_BOTTOM_Y` so a ball that drops through falls a visible distance into the drop before `RemoveFallenBalls` culls it. Steep walls (~55°) keep balls running down to the hole rather than resting (a ball that did come to rest would be sleep-culled, i.e. vanish where it sits). Dumping a very large cluster all at once (the `End` debug release of a full map) briefly floods the funnel and spikes the physics until it drains; ordinary matched clusters are small and drain smoothly.
- **The terrain hole and the dark pit shaft (solid-ground scenes).** In the city the funnel drains into the canyon that falls away below the arena, and in the sea into the water; but the **solid-terrain** scenes (mountains, meadow, savanna, desert, forest) are a flat clearing at the island's foot, and the flat ground plane sliced across the glass funnel just below its rim — the drain read as a shallow bowl and balls falling through vanished into the ground. The fix is in two halves. First, the terrain shaders `clip()` the island's footprint out of the ground (`IslandHoleRadius`, set from `SceneRenderer.TerrainHoleRadius` = `ArenaIsland.TERRAIN_HOLE_RADIUS`, a couple of units inside the disc's rim so the terrain tucks under the stone edge with no gap; **0 in the map editor**, which draws no island, so its terrain stays whole under the field AABB — and making an island available to a host must not hand the editor one as a side effect). But a hole alone made it worse — the ~55 %-opaque glass then showed the bright sky-haze straight through it, reading as a glass ring on the ground. So second, a **dark pit shaft** (`ArenaIsland`'s own mesh and renderer, drawn by `DrawPit` in the five `SceneRenderer.IsSolidTerrainScene`s only, opaque and `CullNone` before the glass, which composites over it) backs the glass: a near-black `FunnelMesh` cone that **hugs** the glass funnel — it *shares the funnel's mouth* (`ArenaIsland.FUNNEL_TOP_RADIUS`) and descends just outside it past the kill plane, so a ball runs down the glass into darkness and is culled inside the pit rather than against the bright sky. A wide cone would hide behind the disc's stone ring and leave the bright hole showing through the narrow (radius 14) aperture — the pit **must** stay just outside the funnel at every depth. It is near-matte (`SpecularAmbientStrength` 0.03) so no dome bleaches the well, and the gold rim bead hides the mouth it shares with the funnel. Purely visual — no physics; the funnel mesh is still the only floor.

Windows are not lit once and for all: each keeps its own rhythm off `CityWindowTime` (wall clock, like the balls' pulse — a city's lamps do not stop when the simulation is paused), holding a state for an interval of its own length and then deciding again, with a short fade rather than a cut. `CITY_WINDOW_BRIGHTNESS` stays **under** `GLARE_THRESHOLD`: a window that glares veils the tower behind it, and a thousand of them turn the skyline into one white haze.

Everything about the buildings is procedural and there are no assets. One 1×1×1 `BoxMesh` serves every building — each is that cube under a different instance matrix, so the whole city is a single instanced draw call (the arena floor is no longer part of it; that is the separate `IslandMesh` platform, above) — one call, but fed a frustum-culled array ordered near to far rather than the generator's own, for the reasons in "Drawing the city near to far" in `docs/rendering.md`. Non-uniform scale is safe here because the boxes are axis-aligned in object space: a diagonal scale maps each face normal onto itself, so `normalize` in the pixel shader recovers it without an inverse transpose. The `InstancedCity` technique lays the window grid out **relative to each building**, not on a raw world grid. A raw world grid (what it used to do) put the pattern at a random phase against every tower's edges, so windows landed jammed into corners or clipped — which reads as unreal. Now a dedicated `CityVS` hands the pixel shader the pixel's offset from the building's centre and the building's world size (the box is the 1×1×1 unit cube, so a transformed unit axis is the world size and the transform of the object origin is the centre; all Bone-agnostic), and `CityPS` holds a fixed wall margin (`WindowMargin`, world units) clear of glass at **every** edge and spreads the windows evenly across the interior — so no window touches a corner and every tower carries the same border. The floor/column **count** is still solved from the building's world size against the target pitch (`WindowPitchX/Y`), so a hundred-storey tower still gets more floors than a low one and a wide one more columns; only the phase is now the building's rather than the world's. The per-building cell pitch is the target pitch nudged so a whole number of windows fills the interior exactly (kept near the target, so it still reads natural), and the `buildingId` — for the neon per-tower hue and the lit-window pattern — is taken from the tower's own centre so it is one value across the whole facade. Windows band-limit to their own average coverage against that per-building pitch, so a distant tower is a dim glowing block rather than a moire of floors.

Two things learned tuning it, both worth keeping in mind elsewhere. The **specular ambient is not multiplied by albedo** (a reflection does not care how dark the surface under it is), which is physically right but washes a whole skyline in sky color at grazing angles. Almost every facade of a city seen from inside it, and almost every square metre of a floor beyond your own feet, is at a grazing angle where Fresnel is near 1 — so the city runs the term at 0.07 and the marble floor at 0.4, and both were bleached white before that. And a translucent sheet over a lit city reads as *no floor at all*; the mullions dividing the glass into panels are what make the eye accept there is glass there.

## The shared scene backdrops

The settings are **shared between the game and the map editor** so a map looks in the editor the way it will play. Eleven `SceneKind`s exist, but only the first seven are on the cycle the Testbed's NumPad2 and the editor's V walk (`SceneRenderer.CycleLength`, deliberately **not** `SceneCount` — the cycle stays on the seven scenes a map is authored against, and both executables wrote the 7 as a bare literal until #75): the forest, space, the dream and the cavern are past its end and only the Game selects them (plus `scene=forest`/`scene=space`/`scene=dream`/`scene=cavern` on either command line). The split:

- The self-lit backdrops — sea, savanna (with its acacias and birds), desert (with the same flock of birds), mountains (with its snow), meadow, the forest's floor and hills (its scatter of trees, rocks and stumps is `ForestScatterRenderer`'s beside them since #75, so all three draw the wood too) and **space** — live in `Prazsky.Core.Render.SceneRenderer`, which owns their effects, meshes, particle buffers and every tuning constant. Each is a dedicated shader that computes its own lighting from a `SceneFrame` handed over per frame (camera, sun direction, the sky palette in linear radiance, the sun's tinted radiance, the wall-clock time, and an optional cloud-apply hook). `DrawEnvironment` draws the terrain/water (plus the savanna's acacias and the savanna's and desert's birds); `DrawOverlays` draws the foreground weather last — the mountain's snow, the sea's spray, the savanna's campfire flame. The Testbed passes `_clouds.ApplyTo` as the hook; the editor passes null, so its scenes get full sun with no cloud shadow (the cloud uniforms stay zero and `CloudSunlight` returns 1). Both build the scene shaders out of the one Testbed content directory. **Space is the odd one in this list** — it replaces the sky rather than the ground (`SceneRenderer.ReplacesSky`, which the dream and the cavern share), so it is one full-screen pass with no grid, no cloud hook and no dome behind it, it is the only draw here that touches the depth state, and like the other two sky-replacing scenes it states its own light rig (`TryGetLightRig`) instead of taking the dome's. See "Space (Game)".
- The **city** (and its neon relight) is `Prazsky.Core.Render.City` — a seeded generator of one instanced box per building — drawn through the shared `InstancedModel` city technique by an `InstancedModelRenderer` each executable owns and sky-lights like the balls (`CityWindowBrightness > 0` selects the technique; `CityNeon` flips it to neon). The arena floor (marble/glass panels) is **not** shared: it is tied to the physics ground blocks and stays in `Testbed.cs`, which is why the editor draws no platform and marks the field with the AABB instead.

So a change to any scene reaches both executables at once, exactly as the shared shaders do. The per-scene sections below describe what each one is doing; the tuning dials named in them now live on `SceneRenderer` (or `City`) rather than at the top of `Testbed.cs`.

## The sea (Testbed)

The second setting, reached with NumPad2 the way NumPad1 cycles the sky domes (or `scene=sea` on the command line, since a synthetic NumPad press never reaches the SDL window). Only the environment swaps: the round stone island stays as it is, now floating a little above open water instead of standing among city towers. It is `Testbed/Content/Shaders/Sea.fx` with its switch and draw plumbing in `Testbed.cs` (`SceneKind`, `SwitchScene`, `DrawSea`), and it borrows the scene's whole toolkit rather than inventing its own.

The water is **real geometry** now, not a flat quad: a camera-centred grid (the shared `CreateGridMesh`, the same machinery the desert, mountains and meadow use), snapped to a cell each frame so it does not swim, displaced by a sum of **Gerstner (trochoidal) waves** in the vertex shader — sharp crests, rounded troughs, the horizontal pinch that sells a wave over a plain height field. The analytic normal and the horizontal **Jacobian fold** (which drops below 1 as a crest pinches and negative where it overhangs — the whitecap generator) come free from the same per-vertex evaluation. The wavelengths are kept **short relative to the scene** (a ball is ~1 unit) so several crests fall inside the visible water and it reads as waves rather than one flat tilt — a 100-unit swell only ever shows a corner of one wave at this scale, which is why the first flat-quad version, whose whole "wave" was a normal-only slope, read as a low-res mirror. The mean level (`SEA_LEVEL_Y`) is dropped well below the island so the crests have headroom without poking up through it, and the waves **fade to flat** between `WAVE_FADE_START`/`WAVE_FADE_END` so the far sea settles into a clean hazed horizon rather than a jagged fringe. Everything runs off the wall clock, so the sea keeps moving while the simulation is paused, like the clouds and the balls' pulse.

On the wave geometry the pixel shader piles the rest. Fine **wind chop** — a few band-limited octaves tilting the normal through the ground relief's `PerturbNormalFromHeight`, scrolled downwind — breaks the surface so it is rough rather than a mirror between the crests. **Whitecap foam** appears where the Jacobian folds and where a crest rises past `FOAM_CREST_START`, broken up by the shared cloud noise into flecks and streaks rather than a painted band, and lit as a matte cap by sun and sky. **Subsurface scattering** glows the crests when the sun is low behind them and the eye looks into the water. A Fresnel **sky reflection** samples the dome's two-color gradient in closed form (`SkyRadiance`'s trick, so the water takes the mood of whichever of the eighteen skies is up), a sharp **sun glint** sparks where the reflected ray points at the sun, and the **cloud shadow** is the same one line the rest of the scene gets (`CloudSunlight` off `Clouds.fxh`), so the water darkens under the very cloud overhead. A horizon haze fades the finite grid into the skyline. Because the water mirrors the sky, its whole mood follows the dome — a bright sunny sky gives a bright, breezy sea, not a stormy one — so **the sea scene defaults to a darker dome** (`SEA_DEFAULT_SKY_DOME`, a violet/teal dusk) on entry, whether from `scene=sea` at startup or a NumPad2 switch into it, unless a command-line `sky=` overrode the startup default; NumPad1 still cycles domes freely from there (`SetSkyDome` is the shared runtime dome-change, used by both). The dials (`WAVE_*`, `CHOP_*`, `FOAM_*`, `SSS_*`, `WATER_COLOR_*`, `SEA_LEVEL_Y`) live on the shared `SceneRenderer`, so the map editor draws the same water. It is drawn opaque, first in the scene block where the city would be, `CullNone` (one open surface, read from above and through the crests), so the arena glass composites over it unchanged. Shader Model 5.0, like every scene shader.

The free camera can dive **below the surface** (in the game, the drop cinematic's lens does), and there the sea alone gets an **underwater murk** so being submerged reads as underwater rather than showing the world unchanged with a water plane cutting through it. It is a post-resolve tint in `Tonemap.fx`, applied in **linear light before the ACES curve** (so the submerged scene rolls through the same highlight response as everything above it): the frame is absorbed towards a blue-green (`UNDERWATER_ABSORB`, a multiplier under 1 — red goes first, so it blues and dims) and the water's own in-scattered glow is added (`UNDERWATER_INSCATTER`), both by `UnderwaterAmount`. That amount is set on the CPU only in the sea scene and ramps `0→1` over `UNDERWATER_FADE_DEPTH` world units below the mean surface (`SceneRenderer.SeaLevelY`, a touch above it so partial submersion already tints), and is **0 (a shader no-op) in every other scene** — the terrain scenes have solid ground, not water, to get under. The tint itself is uniform (no per-pixel depth in that pass, so no distance fog — good enough, and it fades in smoothly as you dive; the spray billboards double as suspended bubbles down there). On top of it the same pass adds a **peripheral blur** — a diver's vision goes soft at the edges: a cheap 8-tap golden-angle spiral disc whose radius and blend both grow from **0 at the frame centre** (which stays crisp) out to the corners, as `edge² · UnderwaterAmount`, so it is stronger towards the edges and off entirely above water. It is inside a branch on the `UnderwaterAmount` **uniform**, so the whole frame takes the same path (no cost above water, no divergence). The overlay and the crosshair draw after the resolve, so they stay sharp. Above water the whole block is byte-for-byte the old resolve.

Blown spray and spindrift ride over the waves (`Spray.fx`, drawn by `SceneRenderer.DrawOverlays` in the sea scene exactly as the mountain's snow is): a static buffer of billboards animated entirely in the vertex shader, like the snow, but in a **thin slab that follows the camera in XZ while clinging to the sea surface in Y** (`SPRAY_LEVEL_Y`, just above the mean), whipped fast downwind (`SPRAY_WIND`, aligned with the waves' own wind) with a turbulent per-particle sway. One buffer carries two reads split per particle — fine **droplets** and larger, fainter **mist wisps** (the spindrift). It feathers towards the slab's top and edges so it has no hard boundary, is depth-read (the waves and platform occlude it) and runs off the wall clock, so the storm keeps blowing while the simulation is paused. The one trap, worth remembering for any water/light particle here: `GLARE_THRESHOLD` is only **0.38** (on linear luminance), and at a grazing angle the view ray threads hundreds of particles through the thin slab which **stack to full opacity** — so any spray colour above the threshold blooms into a starfield once it accumulates, *however low the per-particle alpha*. The spray colour (`SPRAY_COLOR`) is therefore kept a cool grey-blue **under** 0.38: it reads as cold storm haze/spindrift rather than white foam-spray, but it never glares. Making it a brighter, whiter spray would need a raised glare threshold (globally, or per-scene) or drawing it in a post-resolve pass — which would forgo the depth occlusion that keeps it behind the platform and cluster. The spray dials (`SPRAY_*`) sit with the sea's on the shared `SceneRenderer`, so the map editor draws the same spray.

## The savanna (Testbed)

The third setting, next in the NumPad2 cycle after the sea (or `scene=savanna`; the enum slot is `SceneKind.Savanna`). It **grew out of the old Sahara dunes** — which are now back as their own [Desert](#the-desert-testbed) scene, below — into open golden grassland rolling gently to a wide horizon, dotted with acacia trees, the island standing in a flat clearing. The rework was prompted by the dunes showing a **grid** — the coarse displaced mesh's per-vertex normal left a faint facet / Mach-band pattern across the sand — and the per-pixel-normal fix is the one thing the savanna terrain does differently from its siblings (and the fix the reborn desert now shares).

`Savanna.fx` displaces a camera-centred grid (`SAVANNA_GRID_N` a side over `SAVANNA_EXTENT`, snapped to its cell so it does not swim) by a smooth rolling field — flat in a clearing around the origin and rising into low rises with distance, flatter than the meadow's hills since a savanna is open. Its **normal is taken per pixel** from the height field's own gradient (three cheap `TerrainHeight` taps in the pixel shader), not interpolated from per-vertex normals: interpolating a coarse mesh's per-vertex normal is exactly what made the grid show (a normal discontinuity, i.e. a Mach band, at every cell edge), and evaluating the gradient per pixel makes the shading smooth regardless of tessellation, so the grid is gone. The grid is also finer than the old dunes (400 vs 200) so the silhouette is smooth too. The meadow and mountains still take their normal per vertex; they get away with it because their per-pixel relief and detail hide the seams, but the per-pixel approach here is the clean fix and the one to reach for if either of them ever shows the same grid.

The grass is dry gold-green, varied in patches at two noise scales so the field is patchy gold and green rather than one flat tone, combed by travelling wind bands and tilted by a fine footprint-band-limited relief. It is matte — sun plus the sky hemisphere, dimmed by the shared cloud shadow — and melts into the skyline over `SAVANNA_HORIZON_HAZE_DISTANCE`, so it takes the mood of every dome. Like the sea's dusk default, the savanna **enters under a warm golden dome** (`SAVANNA_DEFAULT_SKY_DOME`, the set's warmest gold horizon) on `scene=savanna` or a NumPad2 switch into it, unless `sky=` overrode it; NumPad1 still cycles freely. Drawn opaque, first in the scene block, CullNone; the grass, wind, relief and terrain dials (`SAVANNA_*`, `GRASS_COLOR_*`) sit on the shared `SceneRenderer`. Shader Model 5.0, drawn in both executables.

Scattered over it are **acacia trees** — the flat-topped umbrella tree that reads as savanna at a glance (`Acacia.fx`). Each is one upright (cylindrical) billboard from a static buffer the C# side plants on the ground (`SavannaTerrainHeight` mirrors the shader's `TerrainHeight`, so a tree sits where the terrain is), and the pixel shader *is* the tree: a thin trunk under a wide, vertically-flattened, flat-topped green crown. They are **alpha-tested** (`clip`) rather than alpha-blended, so they **write depth** and occlude the terrain and each other correctly (the hard cutout edge is softened by the scene's supersampling); the crown takes the dome's ambient and a flat sun term, so it too follows the mood. `ACACIA_COUNT` of them are scattered in a ring clear of the island (`ACACIA_MIN/MAX_RADIUS`), each at its own size off a packed random. Drawn after the terrain, before the birds.

**The crown's leaf mottle was a checkerboard for a long time (#97)**, and it is the small version of the mistake the mountain's field made at scale: a value hashed off `floor(input.UV * 11.0)` is *constant across each cell and steps at every cell edge*, which is a grid by construction, not a mottle. Measured across a crown at the resolution a near tree is actually drawn at, the old field put its whole range into a handful of edges — the largest single-pixel step was **0.216 of a dapple range of 0.22**, i.e. one pixel could carry the entire variation, with 6.3 % of steps over 0.02 and flat ground between them. It is `Fbm2BandLimited` over three octaves now: same measurement, largest step **0.073** and a quarter of the steps over 0.02, the change spread across the crown instead of stacked on twenty edges. Three things about it are worth keeping:

- The domain is the plant's **own extent in world units**, not its UV. In UV a big tree gets the same count of *bigger* clumps as a small one, so every plant reads at the same size whatever it is; and the billboard is not square, so a round clump comes out stretched. It is laid out in the vertex shader, which is also where the per-plant offset is hashed — off `rand` **through a hash**, because the plants' randoms are only ~1/`Count` apart and a straight multiple of one lands neighbouring trees inside a single noise cell wearing the same pattern.
- The mottle needed **more** amplitude, not less: what made a ±6 % cell hash visible at all was its hard edges, and a smooth field of that spread reads as no mottle at all — a flat green blob for a crown. Its **mean is the old one's exactly** (0.890 against 0.890 measured), so the savanna's trees keep the brightness the scene was tuned to and only their texture changed; the spread went 0.064 → 0.088.
- `Fbm2BandLimited` is `Fbm2` with each octave faded out as its own period reaches the pixel (`Noise.fxh`; the mountain's `RockOctave` and the desert's `SandPattern` band-limit the same way and against the same yardstick). It earns its place here: the finest octave's period is 0.204 world units, so at the Testbed's default 2× supersampling it reaches Nyquist about **220 units** out (half that with supersampling off) and the trees are scattered to `MaxRadius` **340**. The coarsest octave holds to ~900, so a far tree keeps a soft broad mottle and loses only the leaf detail rather than flattening outright. The footprint is taken **before the `clip` and before the crown/trunk branch** — both diverge inside a quad, the trunk running up the middle of the crown and the cutout edge crossing it everywhere, and a quad whose lanes took different paths has undefined derivatives. Off a linear interpolant taken up front they are exact.

A flock of birds circles overhead — the only moving creature in the whole world, and still no sprite sheet; the savanna and the desert **share the one flock** (the buffer, the shader and the orbits live once in `SceneRenderer`, drawn in both those scenes). `Birds.fx` draws each bird as a camera-facing billboard whose pixel shader *is* the bird: a dark flapping-wing silhouette, a resting dihedral plus a `sin` beat with the wing tips travelling furthest, wings tapering out, a rounder body at the centre, softened a pixel wide on the footprint so distant birds do not crawl. The C# side circles the quads — `BIRD_COUNT` of them orbit `BIRD_FLOCK_CENTER` over the field, each at its own radius, altitude, phase and unhurried speed with its own flap phase, all off the wall clock, so the kettle wheels slowly and never beats in time. They draw alpha-blended and depth-read (the terrain or the platform in front hides one) writing no depth, in the savanna and desert scenes; the flock's count, size and place sit with the other dials in `SceneRenderer`.

## The desert (Testbed)

The fourth setting, next in the NumPad2 cycle after the savanna (or `scene=desert`; the enum slot is `SceneKind.Desert`). A Sahara: rolling sand dunes stretching to a hazed horizon, the round island standing in the sand exactly as it floats on the sea and stands in the grass. It was the third scene originally, was **turned into the savanna** to fix a grid artefact, and is now **back as its own scene alongside the savanna** — the two arid siblings adjacent in the cycle — because the fix that killed it (the per-pixel terrain normal) means it can come back clean. `Desert.fx` with its dials, mesh and `DrawDesert` on the shared `SceneRenderer`, so the map editor draws the same dunes.

The dunes are **real geometry**, not a flat plane: the shared `CreateGridMesh` grid (`DESERT_GRID_N` a side over `DESERT_EXTENT`), snapped to its cell each frame so it does not swim, displaced in the vertex shader by a low-frequency **direction-mixed sine sum** (`DuneSum`) — a dune with no silhouette against the sky is just a lit patch of floor. `DuneSum` is two long low waves for the shape of the field plus a third term **waveshaped into crest lines** (`1 − |x|`, smoothstepped), because a dune has a sharp crest and a long windward face where a sine has a rounded hump; how far that crest may be sharpened is set by the mesh and not by taste — the cell is ~2.8 units and the ridge terms' own wavelengths are ~57 and ~30, so the crest stays several cells wide and the geometry can still hold it. Sharpened past that it falls between vertices and the per-pixel normal shades a ridge the silhouette does not have. The trailing constant takes the field's mean back to zero: it is multiplied by the clearing ramp, so a field with a mean would rise with the ramp and the desert would read as a shallow bowl with the island at the bottom. Like every terrain scene since the arena became the small round island, it is **flat in a clearing** around the origin (the island's foot, `DESERT_LEVEL_Y`, the same level the savanna's grass sits at) and **rises into dunes with distance** (`DESERT_CLEARING_RADIUS`/`_TRANSITION`, `DUNE_AMPLITUDE` the peak height out in the far field), so the sand fills the frame and the dunes read against the horizon rather than crowding the platform.

The one thing it does deliberately, and the whole reason it can return: the base **normal is taken per pixel** from the height field's gradient (three cheap `DesertHeight` taps in the pixel shader), *not* interpolated from a per-vertex normal. Interpolating a coarse displaced mesh's per-vertex normal is exactly what left the old dunes' faint facet / Mach-band grid; evaluating the gradient per pixel makes the shading smooth regardless of tessellation — the same fix the savanna taught, applied here (the grid is finer too, 360 vs the old 200, for a smooth crest silhouette). On top of the smooth dune normal, fine **wind ripples** (`SandPattern`, band-limited against the footprint and fed through `PerturbNormalFromHeight`, scrolled downwind) crawl the sand, and a veil of **blown dust** (the shared `Clouds.fxh` noise) drifts across it and thickens towards the horizon into a windblown murk. It melts into the skyline over `DESERT_HORIZON_HAZE_DISTANCE`, so it takes the mood of every dome.

**The sand's own surface is the part that was rebuilt, and the flat version is what argued for it**: one uniform tan, ripples that read as a regular diagonal crosshatch — which is what a sum of plane-wave sines always reads as (see `Noise.fxh`'s opening) — and dunes whose tops were the rounded crest of a sine. Real wind-rippled sand is **braided**: sets of wavy lines running across the wind, each set wandering, one handing over to another in patches. `SandLayer` draws exactly that, and all three of its parts are load-bearing — two sets of repeat rounded lines at **+10° and −9°** (enough to cross visibly, little enough to stay one family), each made wavy by perturbing its across-line **phase** with `Noise.fxh` gradient noise running at roughly the line frequency itself (a slow perturbation bends the set like a bent comb; one at the line spacing lets each line wander independently of its neighbours), and the two **screen-blended** with a weight that wanders over the ground. One set alone is corduroy; a straight average of two is a lattice, the same failure as multiplying sines instead of summing them; and the screen blend rather than a mix because averaging darkens exactly the crossings the eye reads the weave from. The technique's lineage is Shane's *Desert Sand* on Shadertoy, rewritten rather than ported: its hashes are sine-based (which band differently across drivers, and `Noise.fxh` exists to end that) and it band-limits by distance alone, which would flatten near sand along with far.
  - **The colour does two steps from one noise evaluation**: a broad field mixes `SAND_COLOR`'s warm ochre against the new `SAND_COLOR_PALE`, and the *same* field offset varies the brightness of what that gave — patches with no tone read as stains, tone with no patches reads as vignetting. On top of it the ripple field itself darkens its own troughs (the cheapest ambient occlusion there is, and what makes the ripples read on the faces the sun is *not* raking — with the normal tilt alone a dune's shadowed flank goes back to being a flat patch of colour), and a per-pixel hash over a 60-cells-per-unit lattice puts **grains** in the near sand. The grain's fade must finish *before* its cells reach pixel size, not at it, or its last few metres are a crawling speckle. The sand is no longer quite matte either: `SHEEN_STRENGTH` adds a wide Blinn lobe (quartz grains are little mirrors, so it is a broad sheen off a million grains facing every way rather than a highlight), gated on `ndotl` or the lee faces would glint in light that never reaches them.
  - **One pattern layer, and that is measured.** The original stacks a second layer turned 15° and a quarter finer, mixed in by a further noise field — four more gradient-noise evaluations per pixel on a shader that covers the whole frame. Measured from a vantage low over the sand at `ssaa=2`, where the frame *is* terrain: flat sand 47.6 FPS, two layers **30.5** (+56 % frame time), one layer **36.1** (+32 %). What the second layer bought was large-scale variety, and most of that comes free instead — `SandLayer` already computes a wandering field for its blend, so the same field is fed back as a slow **bend** of the line family. It must be a bend of the *position* and never of the frequency: scaling the frequency by a field puts `coordinate × field` in the phase, and the coordinate here is a world position running to ±500, so the phase gradient goes as hundreds of cycles per unit while the band-limit is still computed from the nominal frequency and has no idea. The near sand came out as a violent rainbow moiré across the entire frame.
  - And `SandPattern` has **no early exit** once the ripples are unresolvable, tempting as one is: the value feeds `ddx`/`ddy` through `PerturbNormalFromHeight` and the resolvable factor varies per pixel, so a branch on it would diverge inside a quad along the very line where the ripples fade — and a quad whose lanes took different paths has undefined derivatives. A `lerp` does the same job for a handful of ALU. The sharpening of the line crests fades **quadratically faster** than their amplitude, because the shaping is a nonlinearity and generates harmonics well above the fundamental: fading the amplitude alone would leave the crests as the last thing to go and the first thing to crawl. Drawn opaque, first in the scene block where the city would be, `CullNone` (the winding is moot on a heightfield); the dune, ripple, dust and sand dials (`DUNE_AMPLITUDE`, `DESERT_LEVEL_Y`, `DESERT_RIPPLE_*`, `DESERT_DUST_*`, `SAND_COLOR`, `SAND_COLOR_PALE`, `SHEEN_STRENGTH`) sit on the shared `SceneRenderer`. Shader Model 5.0, drawn in both executables. It carries **no point lights** (no campfire), so unlike the savanna its draw sets `SceneLightCount` to nothing, and it forces **no default dome** — like the mountains and the meadow, it keeps whatever dome is up. It shares the savanna's [flock of birds](#the-savanna-testbed) circling overhead (drawn from the one buffer in both scenes), the one moving creature over the empty sand.

## The mountains (Testbed)

The fifth setting in the NumPad2 cycle (or `scene=mountain`). A snow basin the arena sits in, ringed by tall snow-capped peaks that rise with distance and fade into an alpine haze — the city's clearing-among-towers rebuilt in rock and snow, the platform standing in the basin as it stands anywhere.

The terrain is the desert's machinery with a different field: a camera-centred grid (they share `CreateGridMesh`), snapped to its cell on the CPU so it does not swim, displaced by a *ridged fractal* heightfield — `Noise.fxh`'s `RidgedFbm2`, five octaves at a 130-unit first spacing — rather than the dunes' smooth sines. It is low and gentle around the arena (world origin) and rises into peaks with distance (`ClearingRadius`/`ClearingTransition`), so the play surface sits in a clearing the way it does in the city, and the basin stays below the platform glass. Its base normal is taken by finite differences — three field taps per vertex — because the ridge folds and the distance ramp would make a closed-form gradient more trouble than it saves; a fine `RockRelief` (band-limited against the footprint) then perturbs it per pixel so the near rock reads as rough stone. Snow lies where the surface faces up (a `smoothstep` on `normal.y`) *and* above an irregular, noise-broken altitude snowline, so the range reads as rock with snow on it rather than one white blanket; rock varies between a dark and a lighter grey-brown in patches; the blue zenith fills the shadows so the snow reads cold where the sun misses it, and the range dissolves into the skyline over `MOUNTAIN_HORIZON_HAZE_DISTANCE` — the strong aerial perspective that says a lot of cold air.

**The peaks read as low-poly for a long time, and the tessellation was never the reason (#86).** The field was four octaves of `1 - |sin(dot(p, direction))|`, and both of that construction's failures were on the screen at once. Plane waves keep their planes: every face carried the same long straight corrugations, which read as wind-rippled sand rather than rock. And its finest octave's ridges ran about **thirty units** apart, so an 82-unit peak had three features from base to summit and its outline against the sky was a smooth simple cone — the "1998" read is a field with no high frequencies in it, not a mesh with visible facets. Measured against the grid, the case closes: the cell is **3.34 units**, so the vertex grid was oversampling the field about ninefold and a denser one would have changed nothing. `Noise.fxh`'s opening calls this exact construction out — "a sum of plane-wave sines keeps its planes however many terms it has, and the eye reads it instantly as the plasma effect of a 1994 demo" — and the library exists to replace it; the mountain was simply the scene that never got converted.

Converting it needed one non-obvious step. `RidgedFbm2` **cannot be dropped in raw**: sampled over the annulus the peaks occupy it means 0.70 with a standard deviation of 0.13, against the plane-wave field's 0.36 and 0.17 — it sits high and narrow, so it lifts the whole basin and leaves shallow dips in it, which draws a white blanket rather than a range (it did, first try). Dropping a 0.24 floor out and **cubing** what is left puts the terrain back on the height distribution the rest of the scene was tuned against — median 0.30 and p95 0.67 against the old field's 0.35 and 0.66, so `Height` 82 and the snowline both stand unchanged — and the curve is itself the ridged look: valleys flatten, crests keep their height. Measured slope says the same, the median falling from 0.77 to 0.67 while the p99 rises from 2.48 to 3.72. That steepening is why `SnowSlope` moved 0.95 → 0.78: the old band wanted a face within 18° of flat, which on the new massing left the snow only in the gullies and a bare rock range in a scene it snows in. Cost, measured on the integrated Radeon at `High`: **~25.0 ms against ~24.2**, about 1 ms for five gradient-noise octaves evaluated three times per vertex (the finite-difference normal) over 129 600 vertices.

`MOUNTAIN_GRID_N` was raised from 240 to 360 for craggier detail, and **that is what forced `CreateGridMesh` onto a 32-bit index buffer**: a grid over ~255 a side has more than 65 536 vertices, and the old 16-bit index silently wrapped, so far triangles referenced the wrong vertices and stretched into garbage. On the near-flat sea (380) and savanna (400) that garbage stayed down at the surface and never showed — but the mountain's tall peaks stretched it into strong, regular *dark horizontal bands across the whole sky*, which read exactly like a glare/shading artifact and cost a long hunt down the wrong path (glare threshold, snow brightness, normal band-limiting — none of it). The lesson is in the code: **any `CreateGridMesh` grid over 255 a side must use 32-bit indices**; the sea and savanna were silently over the line already and are now correct too.

And it snows. `Snow.fx` is a static buffer of billboard flakes — one quad each, its base a fixed point in a unit cube — animated entirely in the vertex shader: each flake falls, drifts on the wind, sways and tumbles, wrapping with `frac` inside a box that follows the camera, so the snowfall is endless and always around you. Following the camera rather than the world trades a little translational parallax for never popping at a box edge, the right trade for a uniform veil of small flakes. They draw last, alpha-blended and depth-read (the terrain and the cluster hide the flakes behind them) writing no depth, in the mountain scene alone. Their color's luminance (~0.76) actually sits *over* `GLARE_THRESHOLD` (0.55); what keeps a near flake from blooming into a glowing orb — which reads as a bug rather than a snowflake — is the feathered speck alpha and the quarter-resolution glare downsample diluting its few pixels. Dim the colour, not the opacity, if flakes ever glow.

**For a long time a flake was a snowball (#85)**, and the reason is worth keeping: a feathered white circle is exactly what a lit sphere looks like. The old speck was a disc feathered only over its outer half at opacity 0.9, and at `FlakeSize` 0.13 one eighteen units out still covered 23 pixels — against a ball a single unit wide, that is a snowball falling past the camera, not weather. Three things separate a flake from a ball, and the shader now carries all three. **Shape**: the silhouette is a six-armed crystal, `cos(6θ)` built from the direction cosines by the double-angle identity — a handful of ALU, and unlike `atan2` no branch cut to seam on — normalised by `1 + lobe` so the arms always reach the quad's inscribed circle and a spikier flake loses the area between them rather than growing out of the quad that carries it. The per-flake lobe depth is decorrelated from the size random and never zero: a flake with no arms is the disc the whole shape exists to escape. **Tumble**: the quad turns in its own plane while the mask stays in flake-local space, so the crystal itself rotates, and a twinkle at twice the spin rate glints it broadside-on. **Focus**: the box is centred on the camera, so `length(boxPosition)` *is* the distance from the lens — a flake nearer than a quarter of `NearFade` is dropped entirely, because real snow that close to a lens is a blur and drawing it crisply is what filled the screen with a white coin. Softening the mask alone would not have done it: wherever a flake crosses something dark the tonemap crushes the feathered rim back to white, which is why `Opacity` came down to 0.6 as well.

The dials — count, size, shape, fall speed, wind — sit in `MountainSceneConfig.Snow` (`SnowConfig`), and `SceneRenderer` rebuilds the flake buffer when the count changes, so the map editor's property grid retunes the snowfall live. **They are also baked into the two mountain levels' JSON**, `Six.json` and `Ten.json`, which `LevelGen` writes from `new MountainSceneConfig()`: a change to the defaults does not reach the shipping game until the generator is re-run. Retuning for #85 halved the flake and dropped its opacity a third, which cost the veil most of its coverage — hence `FlakeCount` 1400 → 2800.

## The meadow (Testbed)

The sixth setting in the NumPad2 cycle (or `scene=meadow`). The Windows XP *Bliss* hill: vivid green rolling to a crisp horizon under a blue cloudy sky, a flat flowering clearing where the arena stands rolling up into hills with distance. It is the desert's and mountains' displaced-grid machinery again (shared `CreateGridMesh`), but the field is smooth rolling sines rather than dunes or ridges, low around the arena (world origin) and rising with distance, its normal by finite differences. The grass is matte, varied in broad patches so it is not one flat green, and the whole field takes the mood of the dome like everything else.

The motion is the wind and the weather, with no particle system: bands of wind comb through the grass — bright and dark travelling downwind — while the shared cloud shadows drift across the hills, and a fine wind-animated relief tilts the grass normal so the light plays over it. And it is in bloom: wildflowers are scattered through it, little rosettes rather than dots — a ring of petals around a warm golden eye, `|cos(N * angle / 2)|` cutting N rounded lobes (even in the angle, so it survives the `atan2` seam), with a per-cell petal count, size, rotation and colour (white daisies, yellow buttercups, the odd pink one). Each flower's centre is held within `[size, 1 - size]` of its grid cell so the whole rosette fits inside and no petal is clipped by the cell edge, and the lot fades against the pixel footprint so the distant meadow stays clean green rather than a shimmer of dots. Grass, hill, wind and flower dials sit at the top of `Testbed.cs`.

## The neon city (Testbed)

The last of the seven, at the end of the NumPad2 cycle (or `scene=neon`). Not a new place but the same procedural city relit: the `CityNeon` switch on `InstancedModelRenderer` flips `InstancedModel.fx`'s city technique from warm-and-cool lamps to a neon night — near-black facades and windows glowing pink and blue, the magenta and cyan of a neon street, one dominant hue per tower with the odd off-colour accent, about one window in six sparking the opposite hue, big sign bands wrapping some towers in the contrast colour, and a fraction of it all buzzing. The window brightness is driven well over `GLARE_THRESHOLD` (`NEON_WINDOW_BRIGHTNESS`), so every lit pane blooms into a sign; the facades stay dark under any dome, so the neon carries whatever the sky, but it reads best under a dark or dusk one. The `#if !OPENGL` guard the neon path used to sit behind is gone now that both executables build the file for Shader Model 5.0 — the map editor draws the city, and its neon, through the same shared technique, so V reaches the neon scene there too. It is the heaviest scene — the whole city plus the extra sign/flicker work and the bloom it feeds — so it runs the lowest frame rate.

## The forest

The eighth `SceneKind`, a temperate woodland glade: a mossy needle-strewn clearing whose hills dress in a dark conifer canopy, scattered with procedural trees, rocks and stumps. The terrain is `Forest.fx` on the shared `SceneRenderer` (the meadow's displaced-grid machinery, cool and floorless of blooms), and **the wood standing on it is shared too** — `ForestScatter` for where every piece stands and `Prazsky.Core.Render.ForestScatterRenderer` for everything it is drawn with, both in the library since #75, drawn through the shared instanced effect. Until then the scatter lived in the Game alone, which is exactly why the Testbed and the editor used to show a bare clearing: the terrain under it was always the shared `SceneRenderer`'s, so all three had the glade and only one of them had the trees in it. Nothing about it was ever the Game's by nature; the split was an accident of where the scene was built. Like Space the scene sits past the end of the NumPad2/V cycle (`SceneRenderer.CycleLength`, which stays on the seven scenes a map is authored against), so `scene=forest` is how the Testbed reaches it and loading a forest **level** is how the editor does; the game also has it on the scene menu and in the random launch pick. Where in the frame the wood goes stays the caller's, and so does the scene gate — `ForestScatterRenderer.Draw` touches no GPU state, assumes the opaque scene state the callers already set, and draws whenever it is called, so each keeps its own `if (scene == SceneKind.Forest)` and places the draw after the terrain the trees stand on and before the island.

Two tree species split by `ConiferFraction` — a **conifer**, a spruce built as **tiers of branch whorls** (each layer a drooping `LatheMesh` skirt with a shadowed tuck beneath it — the sawtooth silhouette is what says "spruce", where the first build's smooth wobbled cone said "toy"), and a **broadleaf**, a crown of several overlapping **leaf lobes** over a base noise field (a deciduous crown is a cluster of masses; a single noise-bulged sphere still read as a ball, because gradient noise has no structure at the one scale a crown does). `RockMesh` and `StumpMesh` are lathes with strong and modest irregularity. Each kind is built at **several sets of proportions AND its own structural seed** (six spruces, four broadleaves, three boulders, two stumps): the seed rolls what proportions cannot — the spruce's tier count, taper, droop and pinch; the broadleaf's lobe count, directions and weights; and every part's wobble phase (`LatheMesh` grew an `irregularityPhase` for exactly this — without it every variant undulated identically, and **two lathes that share an edge must share the phase** or the seam opens). `ForestScatter` splits its instances between the variants, because a uniform scale and a yaw do not change a silhouette and the silhouette is what the eye counts — proportions alone made copies of one tree at three sizes, and the eye caught it. So a kind's instances come back as `ModelInstance[][]`, one array per variant, each its own instanced draw (and two per tree, since bark and foliage are two materials and the tint is per-draw) — and each foliage variant takes its own **tint multiplier on the config's linear colour** (`ForestScatterRenderer.CONIFER_TINTS`/`BROADLEAF_TINTS`, means near 1 so the config stays the authored average, encoded to sRGB only after the multiply), because a real stand is never one green. **All twenty-five of those tints are encoded once at build time**, which is the one thing the hoist changed rather than moved: the Game re-derived every one of them on every frame the forest was on screen — a `GetSceneConfig`, six `ToVector3` and a `LinearToSrgb` per variant — for values only a config edit can change, so the draw is six indexed loops with no arithmetic in them now. The cost of that is one rule to know: a caller that mutates a config **in place** has to call `ForestScatterRenderer.Replant`, or it goes on drawing the colours the old config asked for (which is why the editor's live grid re-plants — see "The scene-config editor" in `docs/formats-and-tools.md`). And the tint tables must be at least as long as their variant arrays, which nothing checked: a seventh spruce used to throw from *inside the draw*, in the one scene where the forest is on screen at all, so it would have been a crash nobody met until they picked the forest. It fails at construction now, naming both lengths. The variety is in the **mesh** rather than in a per-instance stretch: the shader transforms normals by the world matrix with no inverse transpose, so a non-uniform scale would shade a squashed tree as the shape it was authored at. A **rotation** is safe, which is why every instance also gets a small lean (a boulder a much larger tumble). Near-tip lathe rings get their wobble **capped at a share of their own radius**, or the displacement crosses the axis and turns the ring inside out. The scatter is the savanna's clumped generator, planted on the floor through `SceneRenderer.ForestTerrainHeight` — a CPU mirror of the shader's height field, and **`MathHelper.SmoothStep` is not GLSL `smoothstep`** (value-interpolate vs clamp-then-hermite); that misuse scattered the first forest six thousand units into the sky, and the same misuse inverted the first crown's bottom tuck.

The first build's trees rendered as **black eggs sitting on the ground**, and every cause is a trap worth keeping:

- **The crown used its height as a semi-axis**, spanning `[baseY − h, baseY + h]` — the bottom pole a unit under the ground and the whole trunk buried inside the egg. A crown spans `[trunkHeight, trunkHeight + crownHeight]`, so the trunk stays visible under it.
- **Every lathe profile was traced bottom-up**, the reverse of the direction `LatheMesh` documents (top → outside → underside), so trunk, rock and stump were all **inside out** — the sphere-winding trap in its lathe form: nothing disappears, the far side is drawn with inward normals, and a shallow inside-out dome even reads *lit* (you are looking at the interior of its bottom disc, whose normal faces the sun).
- **The config colours are linear radiance, but the material-diffuse uniform is sRGB**: the instanced shader decodes `DiffuseColor` with `SrgbToLinear` at the tap (ball tints are display values), so linear-authored greens landed near black — 0.16 decoded to 0.02. The scatter draws encode once at the boundary (`ColorSpace.LinearToSrgb`); the terrain shader's own colours stay linear like every scene shader's. **And a tinted draw is not a pass-through**: `Draw` reduces the material diffuse to its luminance and multiplies by **1.25**, a boost calibrated for models whose brightest material is 0.8 — over a white material that lands every encoded colour half again too bright, so the scatter meshes are built at 0.8 (`ForestScatterRenderer.SCATTER_MATERIAL_DIFFUSE`) and the two cancel.
- **A procedural-mesh renderer defaults to a white specular** (`(1,1,1)` power 16 — right for vinyl and glass), which on a dark crown reads as wet obsidian. The scatter passes matte per-draw `BasicEffectParams` — three of them, built once in `ForestScatterRenderer` (foliage, bark and weathered stone) out of the scene's flat ambient fill, which is the only thing about the scene that component is told — and a **zero** specular there means "no override, use the white default", so matte is a dim non-zero colour, never zero.
- **A `LatheMesh` span whose first ring sits on the axis got a `+Y` face normal for the whole span.** The ring tangent was taken from the span's first ring, and a ring at radius 0 has all its segments at one point — so the cross product collapsed and the fallback fired. That is *right* for a flat top (a disc's first ring is its centre) and wrong for anything that comes to a point: the fix takes the tangent from whichever of the span's two rings is off the axis, without which the top third of every conifer shaded as though it faced the sky.
- The forest belongs in **`SceneRenderer.IsSolidTerrainScene`** — its clearing is flat ground at the island's foot like the meadow's, so the terrain shader cuts the island's footprint out and the dark pit shaft has to be drawn behind the drain's glass, or the funnel reads as a glass ring over bright sky haze. It was omitted from the hand-kept list in the game **and** from the Testbed's own, which is the exact failure a duplicated classification invites and the reason there is one shared list now (#75, above). The five are mountains, meadow, savanna, desert and forest.

The terrain took a second pass after the first build read as **blurred, low-res mounds**, and each fix names the cause:

- **The hills' sine octaves are domain-warped** before they are summed (two long sines bending the sampling plane — exact to mirror on the CPU), because summed plane waves keep their planes however many there are, and straight wavefronts are what made the hills a *row* of smooth mounds. Five octaves rather than three, the two new ones filling the gap between hill and lump scales — the band a "smooth blob with bumps on it" is missing — with amplitudes summing to 1 so `HillHeight` keeps its meaning.
- **The floor lumps run under a broad mask** and gained a third wave: unmasked, two sines interfere into an even weave across the whole floor, which reads as a pattern rather than as ground; masked, the roughness comes in patches the way roots and hollows do.
- **The base normal is per pixel now** — the savanna's fix, three `TerrainHeight` taps in the pixel shader — because interpolating the coarse displaced grid's per-vertex normal leaves a Mach band at every cell edge, and on this scene it read as the whole floor being slightly out of focus.
- **The needle relief is four octaves, band-limited per octave** (the ball relief's rule — one global fade flattens the lot at arm's length), and a **needle-scale colour grain** joins it near the camera: the relief tilts the light, but a floor with no fine *albedo* change still looks airbrushed however it is lit. Both fade to nothing before they can shimmer.
- **The near slopes' canopy gained a third, crown-top mottle scale** under its own band limit — the two broad scales alone read soft-focus exactly where the hills fill the frame.
- Measured on the APU (front end, 1600×900, `quality=high`, dome 13, 16 s runs): **17.2 → 20.0 ms**. The +2.8 ms is mostly the per-pixel normal's three field taps; the forest stays inside the terrain-scene band (the mountain is 19.1) and above the adaptive probe's floor on this machine.
- **The floor's second pass — FBM mottle, triplanar normal variation, slope-driven colour and procedural tree shadows — is the most expensive thing on this scene, and it was not free.** Measured on the desktop GPU (front end, 1600×900, `quality=High`, dome 13, `nocap`, two 18 s runs each): **540 → 371 FPS**, i.e. 1.85 → 2.70 ms, **+0.84 ms and −31 %**. That is at windowed 1600×900; the work is per-pixel on the floor, so it scales with resolution and 4K is the stated base. `ForestShadow`'s 10-step march is the bulk of it, and unlike `Sky.fx`'s `CloudSlices` this scene has no tier branch to put it behind — see the open issue on shrinking it.
- **The floor colour answers the form now**: `slope` (from `baseNormal.y`) washes the flats towards moss and the banks towards bare earth and litter. Without it the floor reads as one material however it is lit, because the colour never answers the shape it sits on. The four-octave `ForestFbm` replaces the single `CloudNoise` tap the patches and litter used to read off — one octave reads as smooth blobs where four sum to broken ground.
- **The tree shadows are cast by trees that do not exist.** The terrain shader does not know where the scatter's ~240 instances stand, and passing them in would be a limit and a cost; `ForestShadow` plants a *virtual* tree per hash-grid cell instead and marches the sun ray across the cells. The two never argue about who shadows whom because the shadow's density rides `canopyRamp` — 0 inside the clearing, where the real trees stand and only their own shadow should show.

The wooded hills are **colour, not geometry**: past the clearing the floor dresses in a dark canopy mottle (`TreelineColor`, grove- and crown-scale noise) with its **own ramp, nearer and tighter than the hills'** — the slopes that fill the frame are the transition band itself, and a canopy keyed to the full hill ramp only arrives where the haze already owns the colour. Its edge rides the grove noise so the woods begin on a ragged line. The horizon haze is **two-stage**: distant forested ridges turn *blue* (the air scatters skylight), so the hills recede first into a zenith-derived murk and only the last stretch melts into `HorizonColor` — reaching it exactly at `HorizonHazeDistance`, which must stay at or inside the terrain grid's half-extent or the mesh edge shows against the dome. And **`CloudNoise` is gradient noise, which clusters hard around zero**: measured over four million samples, one sigma is 0.18, the 5–95 band is ±0.30 and only the extremes approach ±0.8 — so the usual `* 0.5 + 0.5` spans about 0.35–0.65 and the ACES curve flattens that into one tone. Spread it by ~1.6 (and saturate) before using it as a mottle, here and anywhere else a noise has to *read* on screen.

## Space (Game)

The ninth `SceneKind`: the island floats in deep space under a long-exposure astrophoto sky — a dense starfield, the Milky Way with its dust lanes, three coloured emission nebulae, scattered distant galaxies, one large banded planet, and a **volume the island is inside of**. Physically an eye in orbit would see mostly black; this is deliberately the *picture* of space, which is what the eye recognises as space. `Space.fx` on the shared `SceneRenderer`, so all three executables compile it; only the Game can select it (`ScenePage`, the random pick, `scene=space`), and the Testbed reaches it with `scene=space` too — the Testbed's NumPad2 and the editor's V both still stop at `SceneRenderer.CycleLength`, as they do past the forest.

**It was the first backdrop to replace the SKY rather than the ground — the dream and the cavern followed it, and `SceneRenderer.ReplacesSky` is now the three of them — and every difference below follows from that one fact.** The other eight leave the dome standing over their ground; these have no ground, no horizon and no weather.

- **One full-screen pass, not a displaced grid.** A four-corner quad already in normalized device coordinates, the view ray recovered per pixel from an `InverseViewProjection` uniform (`Matrix.Invert(View * Projection)` — row vectors, as everywhere here), drawn `DepthStencilState.None` so it writes no depth and the island, cluster and gun simply cover it. It covers the **whole** frame rather than a hemisphere, which is why the clear colour goes to black in this scene and the wide-aspect corner band the other scenes clear the horizon for cannot happen. No `OriginXZ` (nothing on the ground to swim), no `IslandHoleRadius` (nothing drawn under the island), no cloud hook.
- **One layer of it is marched through, and every other one is not — which is what the scene was missing.** Everything above `StarNestVolume` in `Space.fx` is a function of the view **direction** alone; the camera's position enters the shader only to build the ray. So the whole sky was a painted dome: move the camera and not one pixel changed, turn it and the picture slid rigidly, near and far together. A starfield is entitled to that (real stars have no parallax worth drawing) but it left the scene with no depth of its own to be inside of, and it read as a texture rather than as a place. The volume is stepped along the ray **from the camera's own position**, so structure one step out crosses the frame faster than structure eight steps out — parallax, from real depth. Measured directly: two frames taken along the *same* view direction from positions 45 units apart differ by a mean **|ΔRGB| of 39.8** over 14 000 samples, and since every other layer is direction-only by construction that difference is entirely the volume. Before it, the same test is exactly zero.
  - The field is Pablo Roman Andrioli's **Star Nest** (MIT, so usable rather than merely admirable). Space is folded into mirrored cells by `abs(TILE − fmod(p, 2·TILE))`, which repeats the volume for free and mirrors at every wall so no seam shows; inside a cell an iterated map `p = abs(p)/dot(p,p) − FORMULA` runs — a sphere inversion, a fold into the positive octant, a translate — whose attractor is a filigree of sheets. What is drawn is not where the point lands but **how far it moved** at each iteration, summed: points near the attractor move little, so the sum is an inside-out distance field and the filaments come out as dark tracery inside a glowing web. Colour comes from `(s, s², s⁴)` along the ray, so the near web is cool and the far one warm, which is a cheap stand-in for scattering and most of why the field reads as having a front and a back.
  - **Four things had to change for it to belong to this scene rather than replace it.** It is entered at the camera (above). It is rotated into a basis of its own, or the fold's cell walls read as a grid squared up with the island and the gun. The world is scaled down hard on the way in (`Volume.Scale`, the parallax dial) — the cells are 0.85 units wide in the field's space against an island 26 across and a camera 33 out, so raw world coordinates would fly the eye through thousands of cells and the whole thing would boil. And it carries an **extinction**, which the original needs none of because it draws nothing behind itself; here a bright web that lets every star shine through its densest knots reads as a decal over the sky. The extinction comes off the emission's own luminance rather than a second accumulator, so it cannot disagree with what is drawn, and it is a reciprocal so it can never go negative however hard the strength is turned up.
  - **The flat haze term is the one number that decides whether this is an addition or a takeover.** The original's `v += fade` adds a constant lift to every pixel — the series sums to about 3.7 — and at full weight it took the void from the 3–4 display codes this scene is authored around to a uniform grey that swallowed the Milky Way's band and both bright nebulae whole. Star Nest is free to do that because nothing else is in its frame. Here it is kept at `VOLUME_HAZE` = 0.22, and its job is the thin stuff *between* the filaments and nothing more.
  - **Cost, and why the counts are constants rather than dials.** They multiply, and a dial invites a setting that drops the frame off a cliff. The original marches 20 steps of 17 iterations — 340 evaluations of the formula for every pixel of the frame, a Shadertoy's budget and not a backdrop's. Measured from one vantage at `ssaa=2` against the painted sky this was added to (**60.4 FPS**): 11×12 gives **29.5** (+105 % frame time, a backdrop taking the frame over); the shipped **8×10** gives **36.5** (+65 %), and the two are not distinguishable side by side. Steps are the cheaper knob and are deliberately *not* cut further: each step's contribution is multiplied by `VOLUME_DISTANCE_FADING`, so `0.76ⁿ` has already spent 93 % of its total by the eighth step — but the march's depth is precisely what the layer exists for, so cutting it spends the feature to save its own cost. `Volume.Strength` of **0 skips the march outright** behind a uniform branch and restores the scene bit-for-bit to its painted-dome self, which is the lever a weak machine or a quality tier should reach for.
- **`DrawSpace` is the one scene draw that touches the DEPTH state**, so it restores `DepthStencilState.Default` on the way out. Left at `None` the island would not occlude the cluster and the whole frame would draw in submission order.
- **No weather, and saying so is not optional.** `InstancedModel.fx` calls `CloudSunlight` unconditionally, and the parameters persist on the shared effect between frames — so a coverage gain left standing from the scene before would go on shadowing the cluster with a deck that is not in the frame. `CloudField.SuppressOn` pushes a gain of zero (the value `CloudSunlight` reads as "full sun"), through the same cached parameter slots `ApplyTo` uses. The dome and the cloud deck are simply not drawn.
- **It states its own light rig instead of taking the dome's** (`SpaceLightingConfig`, handed over by `SceneRenderer.TryGetLightRig` — which the sky-replacing scenes do **as a group**, the dream and the cavern for the same reason and each with its own colours; **all three** executables honour it, the map editor included, because loading a *level* sets its `_scene` from the level's own scene kind and a space level previewed with the wrong sun is the one thing that editor exists to prevent), and the reason is not purity. The sea and savanna trick of *defaulting to a suitable dome* fails twice here: the key tint is `lerp(white, horizon, SkyLightRig.SKY_TINT_STRENGTH)`, so reaching for the darkest dome to get a dark sky **also halves the sun** and leaves the scene grey and flat — the exact opposite of what it is for; and the drain's gold beads are drawn **metallic**, so their reflectance is almost entirely the hemisphere ambient, and taking that to near-black turns the two rings that mark the drain into black ones. So: a low, cold, deliberately **non-zero** ambient. Zero is not an option either — the balls are emissive and would survive it, but the island, the gun and the glass are not, and at zero their shadow sides reach code 0 and the island reads as a hole cut in the starfield. Measured with the shipping numbers: island top **156–177 codes** — which is the band this file records for the island in the city, so it still looks like itself and only its shadows changed. Under the *same* dome 13 the city's island measures 114 and its gold bead 52–62, against space's 159 and 95–102: space is the **brighter** of the two, because its sun is not being halved by that dome's dark horizon. Planet's lit side 199, its night side 22, the void 3–4.
- **Space forces no dome at all**, unlike the sea and the savanna. Its dome is neither drawn nor read, so it is completely inert in this scene — and silently changing the player's dome to no visible effect would be a side effect rather than a setting.
- **Planetshine is a scene point light, not more ambient.** Ambient is directionless, so raising it to get a coloured flank flattens everything instead; a real light also puts a highlight back into the metallic beads. It stands `PlanetshineDistance` out along the planet's own direction with a range three times that, so across a 26-unit island the `(1 - d/range)²` falloff varies by a few per cent and reads as directional.
- **`SceneRenderer.OpenBelow` includes space**, so the drop cinematic dives under the island and watches the balls pour out of the drain against the starfield. Nothing is down there to hide them; and it is **not** an `IsSolidTerrainScene`, so it gets no pit shaft — the drain looks straight through onto the stars, which is the best thing in the scene.

The sky itself, and the things that decide whether it reads:

- **Everything SMALL is kept UNDER `GLARE_THRESHOLD` (0.55 on luminance), and the brightest stars carry their spikes in the shader instead.** This is forced, not timid: `DrawGlare` binds the *supersampled* scene target into a **half**-back-buffer bright pass — a 4:1 reduction per axis at `ssaa 2` (it was 8:1 before the bloom pyramid, which is when this rule was learned) — taken with a single bilinear tap, so a one-pixel star over the threshold is still sampled *stochastically* and can pop in and out of the glare as the camera turns, which reads as a fault rather than as a star. The pyramid halved the problem; it did not repeal the rule. **The one deliberate exception is the planet's lit limb**, and the difference is size and coherence rather than brightness: the limb is a smooth arc hundreds of pixels long, so the sparse grid lands on it many times over and it blooms steadily — which is what a planet's atmosphere should do — while the disc's own body stays under, or the planet stops being an object and becomes a lamp.
- **A spike must be a MAX with the star's core, never an addition** — the review caught this. Added, the two arms cross at the centre and *double* the peak, so a full-magnitude spiked star reached ~0.97 luminance: nearly twice the threshold, making the one thing in this sky the glare samples stochastically the very thing the in-shader spikes exist to avoid. A max also reads correctly as physics — a diffraction spike is the star's own light spread out, not extra light. Useful corollary: the threshold is on **luminance**, so a saturated hue can be far brighter than a neutral one before it blooms — the red nebula peaks at 0.78 in its red channel for a luminance of 0.32, while the same authored number in teal would be twice as close to the edge.
- **Stars sit one per cell of a cube-face lattice and only ONE cell is ever sampled.** The star is jittered inside its cell but held clear of the edges by however far it actually reaches, so it can never straddle a boundary and the eight neighbours never have to be looked at — the meadow's wildflowers solve their clipping the same way. Three layers (coarse/bright with spikes, middle, fine) give the magnitude spread; brightness is `hash^Falloff` with a steep exponent, because real star counts climb hard towards the faint end and a field of evenly bright dots reads as noise.
- **That margin is subtracted from BOTH ends of the cell, so it is what decides whether the lattice shows** — and #88 was it showing. A star may only land in the middle `1 - 2 · margin` of its cell, and under about half a cell of that box the spacing between neighbours stops reading as random. The margin is in **cell units** while the star is sized in **pixels**, so the box closes as the cells get smaller on screen: the same sky is scattered at 4K and a grid on a laptop, which is why this went unseen for so long and why every figure here is per-resolution. Two things were charging far more margin than they needed, both since fixed:
  - **`spikes` was a per-LAYER flag, so every star in the coarse layer paid the spike reach.** A spiked star needs `StarSpikeLength · 2.5` = 17.5 core radii against a plain star's ~3 — 5.8× — and only **4.2 %** of the layer is over `SpikeThreshold`. Deciding it per star, before the margin, takes the coarse layer's capped share from **96.8 % to 1.4 %** at 1366×768 and **46.9 % to 1.6 %** at 1920×1080 on the menu's 60° camera; its median jitter box goes from 0.33 to 0.90 of a cell.
  - **The plain cutoff was a flat three core radii for every star in every layer**, the same distance for a 0.50-peak star at full magnitude as for a faint one at a hundredth of it. It is now solved per star from the star's own peak — `exp(-r²) · peak · magnitude = STAR_CUT`, 1e-4 linear, a third of one 8-bit code — which lands at about 2.2 radii typically. That is what the **fine** layer needed: its cells are only ~10 px across at 1920×1080/60°, where three radii of a 0.16-peak star is a third of the cell. Capped share **91 % → 42 %** there. It costs one `sqrt` and no extra transcendental, because `magnitude` is spelled `exp(Falloff · log(roll))` and the margin shares that logarithm.
  - **Still open at the bottom of the ladder**: the fine layer is 66.8 % capped at 1366×768/60° and 32.2 % at 1366×768/43°. Closing that needs more than a one-cell lookup, so it was left rather than papered over. Two smaller findings from the same measurement pass, also left alone: `Hash33`'s components 0 and 2 share a factor, so selecting on the existence roll tilts one jitter axis by about ±6 % across the cell (χ² 144 on 9 dof over 87 k cells — real, but nothing next to a squeezed box); and stars near a face edge are drawn elongated, because the profile is a circle in **chart** space and `uv = tan(angle)` stretches radially by `sec θ` more than tangentially (that one belongs with #87).
- **The galaxies had the very defect the star code documents as fixed.** `Galaxies` placed its smudge with `clamp(rollA.yz, margin, 1 - margin)` where `StarLayer` remaps — and a clamp piles every roll outside the box onto the two margin lines themselves, which at that layer's margins is about a fifth of all galaxies sitting on four straight lines per cell. It remaps now, like the stars.
- **The pixel footprint is measured on the DIRECTION, never on the chart.** The cube chart is `uv = tan(angle)`, so `duv/dangle = 1 + uv²` — which depends only on `|uv|`, is therefore continuous across all twelve face seams, and is what converts the direction-space footprint into cell units. `fwidth()` of the chart coordinate instead jumps by a whole cell at every seam and rings the sky with twelve lines of wrongly sized stars.
- **A star's core is sized in OUTPUT pixels, not texels and not radians** (hence `SceneRenderer.SupersampleFactor`, which each executable sets — the Game's moves with the quality tier). Sized in texels the same star comes out four times dimmer at 2× than at 1×, i.e. the same sky looking different on two quality settings. `Spread` ships at exactly the shader's own anti-aliasing floor (0.62 texels), which is what makes the two factors *measurably* the same rather than roughly: at 0.45 the floor took over at 1× and drew the stars 38 % wider there. Measured over one star field, mean radiance 8.37 at 1× against 7.05 at 2× — a 19 % difference where getting this wrong is a 4× one. **In vacuum a star must not twinkle** — there is no atmosphere to make it — so any shimmer here is an artifact and never a feature. A brighter star is drawn a little *wider* as well as brighter, because no optics resolve a star as a point and a field of equal-width dots reads as a texture.
- **The Milky Way's elongation is mild, and the first build proved why.** Squashing the galactic latitude by five and sampling at a third of the shipping frequencies gave long smeared streaks that read as **motion blur across the sky**, at a brightness (0.30) that tonemapped to a 75 %-grey wash burying the stars it is supposed to be made of. Elongation says "in a plane"; too much of it says "something is broken". It ships at a squash of **1.6** over a cloud frequency of 13 (2.2 over 9 still read airbrushed), `Brightness` 0.115, and most of the band's weight carried by `StarBoost` — which is the right way round, since a Milky Way *is* stars.
- **Dust must dim the stars as well as the glow.** The dark rifts are dark because opaque dust stands in front, so a lane that dims the band but leaves the starfield shining through reads as a stripe painted over the sky. The nebulae do the same through a transmittance the pixel shader carries out of them — without it a nebula is a glow decal with the whole starfield coming out of its densest knots.
- **Domain warping is the single biggest quality lever in the nebulae.** At 0 they are blobs of cloud; at 1.5 they are filaments and cavities. And the density has to be spread hard about its own mean (`(body - 0.44) * 2.9`) before it is used, for the reason the forest's canopy and the meadow's flowers both learned: a timid mottle is exactly what the ACES curve flattens into one tone.
- **The planet is solved analytically in the same pass**, not drawn: a unit sphere at the distance that gives the configured angular radius, so the ray test is a quadratic and the surface normal falls out of it. It is lit by the very `SunDirection` the island and the balls take, which is what ties it to the frame instead of leaving it a sticker on the sky. Its direction decides the **phase** as well as the framing — the lit fraction is `(1 - dot(Direction, SunDirection)) / 2`, and the dot wants to land in −0.4…−0.7 (it ships at −0.51, a fat gibbous): a full disc is flat and shows nothing of the terminator, a thin crescent hides the bands. The terminator is a `smoothstep`, never a hard `N·L` cut, and the night side keeps `NightAmbient` or the planet reads as a hole punched in the starfield.
- **The storm oval is placed by a DIRECTION, and the first version stamped it twice.** `(dot(n, forward), latitude)` is satisfied by two points, one either side of the forward-pole plane, so the oval appeared mirrored — and half of all placements put the ghost on the very face being drawn. Measured as a tangential offset from a centre direction with the antipode rejected (`step(0, dot(n, centre))`), there is exactly one of it. The same trap waits for anything else placed on a sphere by a pair of projections.
- **Angles are authored in DEGREES in the config** and converted where they are pushed at the shader, and the galactic core is **orthogonalised against the pole** on the way through, so a hand-typed pair never has to be exactly perpendicular for the bulge to sit in the plane. Directions go through a `SafeNormal` with a fallback, because these are values typed into a JSON file and a property grid where a zero is one keystroke away and a NaN direction would take the whole sky with it.
- **The config uses named nested objects, never arrays** (`NebulaOne`/`Two`/`Three`, `Stars`, `MilkyWay`, `Galaxies`, `Planet`, `Lighting`) — the map editor's `PropertyGrid` is built `IgnoreCollections = true`, so a list would be invisible in the live scene-config editor.
- Cost is a full-screen procedural pass, so the expensive parts are gated on **uniform-coherent branches** (`[branch]` on a nebula's angular falloff, on the Milky Way's band mask, on the planet's disc) and the footprint is taken **once at the top of the pixel shader**, before any of them — a gradient operation inside a divergent branch is undefined, and that ordering is the only reason those branches are safe. Measured frame cost is in "The quality tier".

## The dream (Game)

The tenth `SceneKind`, and the second that **replaces the SKY** (see Space above — the full-screen-quad machinery, the view ray through `InverseViewProjection`, `DepthStencilState.None` with `Default` restored on the way out, no dome, no weather, black clear, its own light rig — is all the same, and the two share one quad). Where space is the *picture of space*, this is the picture of a hallucination: slow marbled colour flowing across the whole sphere of the sky, hard glassy solids tumbling and melting into one another, soft luminous orbs breathing through the murk, fast sparks whipping between them. `Dream.fx` + `DreamSceneConfig` (named nested groups — Palette, Background, Shapes, Glows, Lighting — the PropertyGrid rule), `scene=dream` on either command line, on the Game's scene menu and in its random pick.

It is deliberately a scene of **contrasts**, and each pair is carried by a different element: **sharp against blurred** — the solids are raymarched surfaces with crisp silhouettes and fresnel rims, the orbs are pure closest-approach gaussians with no edge at all; **fast against slow** — the background marbling drifts over minutes while the sparks cross the sky in seconds (and the marbling itself carries both: thin ribbons race through the slow broad flow, `pow`-sharpened so only their crests survive). Decisions that hold it together:

- **Everything is analytic 3D** — fields evaluated on the view direction, ray-sphere tests, closest-approach glows — and never a 2D chart of the sphere, so there are no pole seams and nothing hides them. The marbling is **twice-warped fractal noise** (`Noise.fxh` — see "The noise library" in `docs/rendering.md`): the first build was plane-wave sines under a sine warp and read as the plasma effect of a 1994 demo, because summed plane waves keep their planes however many there are; two fBm warp layers produce the filaments and mixing of real turbulence, the time rides *inside* the domains so the fluid evolves rather than scrolls, and the **warp intermediates shade the colour** (where the domain was dragged furthest, the fluid "mixed") — most of why it reads as a substance instead of a pattern. The solids carry **SDF ambient occlusion** (four probes up the normal), so the torus's inner ring and the melt seams sit visibly deeper than the open faces — emission dims in the folds, and a hallucination still has depth.
- **One cosine palette** (`colour(t) = A + B·cos(2π(C·t + D))`) is the scene's whole colour identity: every element — marbling, ribbons, solids, orbs, sparks — indexes the same ramp at its own phase, which is what keeps a frame full of saturated colour reading as one hallucination rather than as a box of crayons.
- **The solids morph as distance fields**: each melts between a sphere, a rounded box and a torus on its own slow cycle (half the solids cycle sphere↔box, half box↔torus), and the lerp of SDFs is what makes the change a *melt* rather than a swap. Each is lit by the dream itself — its palette colour glowing from inside (with a **high emission floor**, or a solid whose palette phase lands dark vanishes into the marbling as a silhouette; a hallucination has no unlit objects) plus the marbled sky **mirrored off its surface**.
- **The march is gated per shape**: each solid carries an analytic bounding sphere, the ray is tested against those first (eight quadratics), and only a crossed bound is sphere-traced, inside its own `[t0, t1]` interval. Most pixels march nothing, which is what makes a raymarched element affordable in a full-screen pass.
- **The solids write no depth** (the whole pass draws behind the scene), so they must roam **well outside anything the camera can stand near** (`OrbitRadius` 130 against the island's 26 and the menu orbit's 44) — a solid that drifted between the lens and the island would draw *behind* it.
- **The background stays well under the glare threshold** (`Brightness` 0.24 — at 0.32 it washed out everything hung on it): it is the canvas. The **orbs are allowed over** deliberately (smooth areas hundreds of pixels wide bloom steadily — the planet's lit-limb reasoning); the **sparks stay at the threshold's edge** and read fast through their three-sample trails instead (a small point over the threshold is sampled stochastically by the glare's sparse grid and flickers, which reads as a fault).
- **The light rig is deliberately coloured** (`DreamLightingConfig`: violet sky ambient over teal ground bounce, a rose key against a cyan fill), so the island, the gun and the balls sit *in* the hallucination instead of standing greyly in front of it. `SceneRenderer.OpenBelow` includes the dream (the drop cinematic dives under the island against the flowing sky); it is **not** an `IsSolidTerrainScene`, so no pit shaft.
- **Measured** (front end, 1600×900, dome 13, vsync off, the APU): **61.7 ms at `High`, 18.8 at `Medium`** — `High` is a desktop setting now, deliberately (the twice-warped marbling was bought at full price), and the adaptive probe steps this class of hardware to `Medium`, where it holds 53 FPS. Before the fractal rework the sine version measured 26.9/9.7 — the plasma look was cheap, which is much of why things look like it.

## The cavern (Game)

The eleventh `SceneKind`, and the third sky-replacing pass (Space's machinery again, the shared quad): a **bioluminescent crystal cavern over an underground river** — deep noise-carved rock veined with glowing minerals, god rays falling from unseen gaps in the ceiling, sharp crystal clusters pulsing cyan and magenta on the walls, spores drifting up through the dark, and the river mirroring the lot. `Cavern.fx` + `CavernSceneConfig` (named nested groups — Rock, Water, Air, Crystals, Lighting), `scene=cavern` on either command line, on the Game's scene menu and in its random pick.

The scene is **analytic except where marching is gated**, and each layer has one trick worth keeping:

- **The cave shell is a quadratic and a plane** — an infinite cylinder and a ceiling, hit analytically — but shaded as **lit, folded stone**: a 3D fBm's gradient (three extra taps, `Noise.fxh`) perturbs the shell's smooth normal, and every light in the scene — a cool key from the ceiling gaps, and **each crystal as a real point light, N·L over inverse square** — works against that perturbed normal, so the rock around a magenta cluster is *lit from the cluster's side*, bumps shadowing away from it. The first build had only a flat colour mottle, and a wall whose colour varies while its light does not reads as painted plaster. A ridged crack network presses darkness into the crevices (light cannot reach into them — the poor man's occlusion); the **mineral veins** are `pow`-sharpened ridge lines of a **3D world-space field with a patch mask** — they were the scene's one 2D-parameterized field (`atan2` of the bearing) and it paid twice: the branch cut stood as a full-height seam of unrelated pattern down the −X wall (mirrored again by the river), and on the ceiling the mapping degenerated to radial spokes converging on a singularity over the origin. A 3D field has no seam and no pole by construction, and its ceiling slice continues its wall slice across the junction for free; the mask keeps the veins *threading* — ore follows some fractures and leaves others bare — where the unmasked web wallpapered the cave with even neon, half of what read as a screensaver. Rock within a few units of the river is **wet** (darker, a waterline stain). Exponential distance fog sinks the far side into an abyssal blue-purple.
- **The river is displaced geometry near the lens** — the sea's recipe restated for a full-screen pass: a seven-component sum-of-sines height field over a decade of wavelengths (26 → 1.15 wu, deep-water dispersion so the swell visibly outruns the chop), **marched** — sixteen bracketing steps and a secant — inside the band the waves can reach, with the amplitude dying between 70 and 170 units of horizontal distance so the far river is the exact flat plane again (no march cost, no silhouette aliasing, and the steam owns that distance anyway). The flat-plane version read as a *blue patterned floor*, and half of that was its fake normal damped to 0.18 — the marched surface uses its full gradient. `WaveAmplitude` (0.7 wu) joins `WaveScale`/`WaveSpeed` in the Water config group, the latter two now global multipliers around their shipped defaults.
- **The river's mirror is the real cave**: the reflected ray is handed back to the same wall-shading function, so the water genuinely reflects the veins, the fog, the steam and the crystal light standing over it — one extra shell test and wall shade per water pixel, where a screen-space approximation would smear. Fresnel's floor is **high for water (0.25)**: a cave river is a black mirror before it is a window, and the more the depths show through, the more the surface reads as flooring. The downward fold of a reflected ray is a **soft max** now — `abs()` folded the mirrored image back on itself along a crease that slid with the waves. The **crystals glint on the chop** (a Blinn lobe per cluster on the wave normal, inverse-square, riding the pulse) — the cave's stand-in for the sea's sun sparkle — and the crest bioluminescence rides the real marched height.
- **The caustics are a cellular edge field now** (`VoronoiEdge2`, `F2 − F1` — zero exactly on the borders between cells), because that is what pool caustics are: a bright **web** where wavelets focus, running around dark cells. Two attempts preceded it and both lessons stand: a wave-interference field *must be normalized before shaping* (`saturate(0.5 + 0.5·h)` over an amplitude of 1.95 clips into broad plateaus — the first "caustics" were a cyan checkerboard), and even normalized, a sine field's crests are blobs, not a net. Caustics also run at a *finer* scale than the waves themselves, being the focused image of the surface — and `CausticStrength` dropped from the flat-plane era's 0.8 to **0.5**: a web bright enough to carry a textureless plane reads as patterned flooring on real waves, which carry themselves.
- **The crystals are the gated march again** (the dream's pattern — per-cluster bounding spheres, a short interval trace), each cluster three interpenetrating octahedra at different elongations and yaws. Two lessons: they must stand **on** the wall (at 0.86 R they floated thirty units off it and read as balloons; 0.965 R), and they must be shaded off the **SDF's own normal** (four tetrahedral taps) — the octahedra's flat faces give a constant normal per facet that jumps at the arris, so the facet structure appears in the shading for free, where the first build's smooth radial term made every cluster a rounded gem. The facet *contrast* is what says "crystal", not the outline. They also carry **SDF ambient occlusion** (four probes up the normal), so the clefts where the three octahedra interpenetrate sit visibly deeper than the open faces — a crystal glows from inside, and its junctions still swallow light.
- **The clusters light the cave**: an inverse-square sum of all eight pools their colour onto the rock and the water around them — eight multiply-adds in the wall shade, and the single thing that makes the crystals belong to the cave instead of being stickers on it.
- **The god rays and spores are closest-approach glows** (no geometry, no march): vertical-line distance for the shafts — height-enveloped (a `smoothstep`, C1 the whole way down: the old clamped ramp put a visible Mach shelf at each end), dust-shimmered, breathing — and the dream's spark gaussians slowed right down for the spores. Both are clamped to the **final** solid including the crystals, and the shafts are added **after** the crystal branch: accumulated before it, every beam crossing a cluster was punched out along the crystal's silhouette. A spore's climb wraps on a **continuous envelope** (born dark at the water, dimmed out under the ceiling) — the raw `fmod` teleported each mote from ceiling to water at full brightness, a hard pop every few seconds in a scene whose whole point is stillness.
- **The shell's two seams are hidden, in depth.** An analytic shell has machined edges no real cave has, and both showed: the wall-ceiling crease was a sharp lit line (the base normal snapped from radial to down-facing in a hard select) — now the normal **coves**, blending over the top twenty-odd units, because rock coves, it does not mitre. The waterline gets three layers of defence: **steam over the river** — an exponential height layer (`MistColor`/`MistDensity`/`MistHeight` in the Water group), integrated *analytically* along the view ray (height fog rather than a slab, because a slab has a top and its top is a new hard line), so a ray grazing toward the far shore runs its whole length through the densest air and the seam fades exactly where it was sharpest — plus a **shore band** (the last few units of water before the wall show pure mirror, and the mirror of the wall right above converges to that wall by construction, so the two shading rules meet at a shared value — the steam alone left the raw line under a steep look at a near shoreline) plus the rock's **wet stain** above the contact. An fBm wisp factor rides on the mist's optical depth (not on the blend, so the far shore still saturates while the mid-water steam drifts) and is keyed to the **ray direction, not the hit point** — `bestT` jumps across every crystal silhouette, and a hit-keyed wisp printed a ghostly crystal-shaped cutout into the fog. The water's mirror gets the same mist integral along the reflected path, and the spores are added after it: a mote climbs *out* of the layer, and stays visible doing it.
- **One deliberate deviation from the brief that shaped it**: no per-scene colour grade or contrast pass — BS3D renders linear radiance and tonemaps once (`docs/rendering.md`), so the grade lives in the authored palette and the scene's light rig (dim and cool, the river's teal in the ground bounce, a faint magenta fill answering from the walls). The one cinematic touch kept in-pass is a **subtle vignette on the backdrop alone**, which darkens the cave's corners without touching the island drawn over it.
- The river sits at `LevelY` −34, **below the drain's kill plane**, so a ball that falls through the funnel is culled before it would visibly reach water it can never splash. `SceneRenderer.OpenBelow` includes the cavern; it is not an `IsSolidTerrainScene`, so no pit shaft.
- **Measured** (front end, 1600×900, dome 13, vsync off, the APU): **73.5 ms at `High`, 21.4 at `Medium` (46.8 FPS)** — `High` is a desktop setting (the bumped, point-lit rock, the cellular caustics and now the marched river were bought at full price, deliberately), and the adaptive probe steps this class of hardware to `Medium`, which still clears its 45 FPS floor. The flat-plane river measured 59.5/17.5; the flat-mottle wall before it, 24.2/9.0.
